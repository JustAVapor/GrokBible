Moses and the Exodus (11:23–29)

11:23

By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden for three months by his parents, because they saw that the child was beautiful, and they did not fear the king’s edict.

With Moses the roll call reaches the second great pillar of Israel’s story. Abraham was the father of the promise; Moses was the mediator of the covenant at Sinai, the man through whom the people were redeemed from Egypt and given the law and the tabernacle. In Hebrews he has already appeared as the faithful servant in God’s house, honored yet surpassed by Jesus, who is Son over that house (3:1–6). Now the author gives more space to Moses than to anyone except Abraham: five “by faith” statements gather around him (11:23, 24, 27, 28, 29). For a congregation of Jewish believers who revered Moses, the point is pointed: the great lawgiver himself lived not by sight, security, and visible privilege but by faith in the unseen God and his future reward.

Strikingly, the first “by faith” in the Moses story is not Moses’ faith at all but his parents’. Moses was a helpless infant; the faith was that of the father and mother who hid him. The Greek says he “was hidden … by his fathers” (ὑπὸ τῶν πατέρων αὐτοῦ hypo tōn paterōn autou), using the plural of πατήρ patēr, “father,” in the sense “parents,” a usage found elsewhere in Greek. Exodus names them later as Amram and Jochebed (Exod 6:20). Faith often begins in the home, in the quiet courage of ordinary people whose names the original story does not even bother to mention at the moment of their decision (Exod 2:1–2 calls them simply “a man from the house of Levi” and “a daughter of Levi”).

The verb ἐκρύβη ekrybē, “he was hidden,” is an aorist passive of κρύπτω kryptō, “hide.” The duration, τρίμηνον trimēnon, “for three months,” comes straight from Exodus 2:2. Three months of hiding a crying infant from Egyptian neighbors and officials is a long time; every day carried risk. Faith here is not a single heroic gesture but a sustained, costly, dangerous refusal to comply with evil.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 2:2 and 1:22

Hebrew (Exod 2:2): וַתֵּרֶא אֹתוֹ כִּי־טוֹב הוּא וַתִּצְפְּנֵהוּ שְׁלֹשָׁה יְרָחִים — vattere oto ki-tov hu vattitspenehu sheloshah yerachim, “and she saw him, that he was good [fine, beautiful], and she hid him three months.”

In the Hebrew it is the mother who sees and hides. The Greek translation of Exodus, which Hebrews follows, puts the verbs in the plural (“seeing him to be beautiful, they sheltered him three months”), and so Hebrews speaks of both parents. There is no contradiction: a mother could not have hidden a child for three months without the father’s consent and help; the Greek simply makes explicit what the household must have shared.

The edict (Exod 1:22): וַיְצַו פַּרְעֹה לְכָל־עַמּוֹ לֵאמֹר כָּל־הַבֵּן הַיִּלּוֹד הַיְאֹרָה תַּשְׁלִיכֻהוּ וְכָל־הַבַּת תְּחַיּוּן — vaytsav Par‘oh lekhol-‘ammo lemor kol-habben hayyillod hay’orah tashlikhuhu vekhol-habbat techayyun, “Then Pharaoh commanded all his people, saying, ‘Every son that is born you shall throw into the Nile, but every daughter you shall let live.’”

Why did they hide him? “Because they saw that the child was beautiful.” The Greek word ἀστεῖος asteios originally meant “belonging to the town” (from ἄστυ asty, “town”), hence “refined, elegant,” and then “fine, handsome, beautiful.” It is the word used in the Greek of Exodus 2:2, and Stephen uses it of Moses in Acts 7:20, adding a remarkable phrase: he was ἀστεῖος τῷ θεῷ asteios tō theō, “beautiful to God” or “beautiful in God’s sight.” The Hebrew word behind it is the simple and rich טוֹב tov, “good.” Readers of the Hebrew Bible would hear an echo of creation: וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאוֹר כִּי־טוֹב vayyar Elohim et-ha’or ki-tov, “And God saw the light, that it was good” (Gen 1:4). The same pattern, “saw … that … good,” is used of the mother’s gaze on her son.

Surely the author does not mean merely that parents of a good-looking baby were moved by his appearance; all parents find their children beautiful, and the Hebrew parents of many drowned boys loved their sons no less. The point is that the parents perceived in this child something that stirred faith: that God had a purpose for him. Jewish tradition elaborated this. Josephus tells of a dream given to Amram announcing that the child would deliver the Hebrews (Ant. 2.210–216), and Philo dwells on the child’s unusual appearance (Life of Moses 1.9, 15). These embellishments go beyond Scripture and need not be adopted, but they show that ancient readers sensed what Hebrews implies: the parents’ act was not sentimental but believing. They looked at the child and trusted that the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, whose promise of a great nation was even then being fulfilled in Egypt (Exod 1:7), would not allow Pharaoh to have the last word. Faith sees God’s purpose where others see only an infant under a death sentence.

“And they did not fear the king’s edict.” διάταγμα diatagma is a formal word for a royal ordinance or decree, used in the Greek of official documents; the Wisdom of Solomon recalls the same command as the decree to kill infants (Wis 11:7). The verb ἐφοβήθησαν ephobēthēsan, “they feared,” is negated: their faith expressed itself as fearlessness before the most powerful ruler of their world. The midwives had shown the same spirit: וַתִּירֶאןָ הַמְיַלְּדֹת אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים וְלֹא עָשׂוּ כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר אֲלֵיהֶן מֶלֶךְ מִצְרָיִם vattirena hamyalledot et-ha’Elohim velo ‘asu ka’asher dibber alehen melekh Mitsrayim, “But the midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt had told them” (Exod 1:17). Those who fear God most fear kings least. This theme of fearlessness before the king will return twice in this paragraph (11:27, “not fearing the wrath of the king”), and it is precisely what the readers need. They had once endured public abuse and the seizure of their property (10:32–34), and the preacher will soon remind them of the bold words of Psalm 118: “The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?” (13:6).

There is also a quiet lesson in the fact that this faith involved hiding. Faith is not reckless. The parents did not parade the child before Pharaoh’s soldiers; they concealed him, and when concealment was no longer possible, they placed him in the ark of reeds on the Nile (Exod 2:3), the same Nile into which the edict commanded he be thrown. God used their faithful, prudent action and turned the instrument of death into the path to Pharaoh’s own household. Faith uses ordinary means while trusting God for the outcome.

11:24

By faith Moses, when he had become great, refused to be called son of Pharaoh’s daughter,

Now Moses himself becomes the subject. The phrase μέγας γενόμενος megas genomenos, “having become great,” is taken word for word from the Greek of Exodus 2:11, where it translates the Hebrew וַיִּגְדַּל מֹשֶׁה vayyigdal Moshe, “and Moses grew up.” In both languages the expression can mean simply “grown up, adult,” but it can also carry the overtone of greatness in rank or status. Both senses fit Moses: he had reached maturity, and he had reached it as a man of standing in the most powerful court in the world. Stephen says that Moses “was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, and he was powerful in his words and deeds,” and that he was about forty years old when he went to visit his brothers (Acts 7:22–23).

Old Testament Text: Exodus 2:10–11

Hebrew (Exod 2:10): וַיִגְדַּל הַיֶּלֶד וַתְּבִאֵהוּ לְבַת־פַּרְעֹה וַיְהִי־לָהּ לְבֵן — vayigdal hayyeled vattevi’ehu levat-Par‘oh vayhi-lah leven, “The child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son.”

Hebrew (Exod 2:11): וַיְהִי בַּיָּמִים הָהֵם וַיִּגְדַּל מֹשֶׁה וַיֵּצֵא אֶל־אֶחָיו וַיַּרְא בְּסִבְלֹתָם — vayhi bayyamim hahem vayyigdal Moshe vayyetse el-echav vayyar besivlotam, “In those days, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his brothers and looked on their burdens.”

Notice the deliberate contrast. In 2:10 Moses “became her son,” the son of Pharaoh’s daughter. In 2:11 the narrator calls the Hebrew slaves “his brothers.” Hebrews reads the movement from the one verse to the other as an act of faith: Moses went out from the palace to identify with the slaves.

The verb ἠρνήσατο ērnēsato is the aorist of ἀρνέομαι arneomai, “say no, refuse, disown, deny.” It is the word used in the Gospels for Peter’s denial of Jesus (Matt 26:70, 72) and for the danger of denying Christ before others (Matt 10:33). Here the direction is reversed: Moses denied, not God, but the identity and privileges offered to him by the world. The aorist presents the refusal as a decisive act. Exodus does not record a formal speech of renunciation; Hebrews interprets Moses’ conduct in Exodus 2:11–12, where he went out to his own people and struck down the Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew, as a public repudiation of his Egyptian identity. By taking the side of a Hebrew slave against an Egyptian, Moses made his allegiance unmistakable. Stephen draws the same conclusion: “he supposed that his brothers would understand that God was giving them deliverance by his hand” (Acts 7:25).

“To be called son of Pharaoh’s daughter” names a real social status. In Egypt’s royal court, foreign-born children were sometimes raised and educated alongside Egyptian princes, and an adopted son of a princess would have stood within the circle of royal privilege, wealth, education, and possible influence. Jewish writers of the first century magnified this. Philo presents Moses as raised as a prince, regarded as the expected heir to the throne, schooled by teachers from Egypt and abroad, and yet choosing his ancestral people (Life of Moses 1.19–33). Josephus records traditions that Pharaoh’s daughter (whom he names Thermuthis) adopted him with a view to the succession, and that he later led an Egyptian campaign against the Ethiopians (Ant. 2.232–253). These details are not Scripture and some are clearly legendary; they are useful only as a window into how highly Moses’ worldly prospects were rated by Jews of the author’s day. The biblical point stands without them: Moses had much to lose.

The nature of his faith deserves attention. In this chapter, faith is “the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1). Moses’ refusal makes sense only if the unseen is real. If there is no God who keeps his promises to Abraham, then Moses threw away a royal career for nothing; if there is, then the palace is a gilded prison and the slave huts are the household of God. The language of being “called” son of Pharaoh’s daughter also has an honor dimension. In a culture where identity and honor came from one’s family and patron, Moses declined the honor of the most prestigious household on earth in order to be counted with a despised and enslaved people. Hebrews has already said that Jesus “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:11) and that God “is not ashamed to be called their God” (11:16). Moses, in his own way, chose not to be ashamed of God’s people.

For the first readers, the application was close at hand. They were tempted to drift back from public identification with Jesus and his people toward the relative safety of a more respectable religious identity. Moses shows that faith sometimes requires refusing a name, a status, or a belonging that the world offers, because it would compromise our belonging to God and his people.

11:25

choosing rather to be mistreated along with the people of God than to have the fleeting enjoyment of sin,

Verse 25 explains what Moses’ refusal involved: it was a choice between two ways of life. The participle ἑλόμενος helomenos, from αἱρέομαι haireomai, “choose, prefer,” is in the middle voice, which in this verb carries the sense “choose for oneself, take as one’s own.” It was a deliberate preference, weighed and settled, and the adverb μᾶλλον mallon, “rather,” sets the two options side by side as on a scale.

On one side of the scale: συγκακουχεῖσθαι τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ synkakoucheisthai tō laō tou theou, “to be mistreated along with the people of God.” The verb occurs only here in the New Testament. It is a compound of σύν syn, “with,” and κακουχέομαι kakoucheomai, “to be maltreated, to suffer ill-treatment.” The prefix “with” is the heart of the matter: Moses chose solidarity. He was not merely willing to suffer; he was willing to suffer with others, sharing their lot. The simple verb returns twice later in the letter. The persecuted faithful of 11:37 “went about … mistreated” (κακουχούμενοι kakouchoumenoi), and in 13:3 the readers are told: “Remember the prisoners as though bound with them, and those who are mistreated (τῶν κακουχουμένων tōn kakouchoumenōn), since you yourselves also are in the body.” The preacher is shaping a community that will stand with its suffering members as Moses stood with his. They had done so once: “you became partners with those so treated,” and “you sympathized with the prisoners” (10:33–34). Moses is their model for doing so again.

Note also the phrase “the people of God” (τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ tō laō tou theou). Exodus repeatedly has God call the Hebrew slaves “my people” (Exod 3:7, 10; 5:1). In the eyes of Egypt they were a labor force, dangerous foreigners to be contained (Exod 1:9–11); in the eyes of faith they were God’s own people, heirs of the promise to Abraham. To join them was to join God. Hebrews uses the same phrase in 4:9: “there remains a Sabbath-rest for the people of God.” The suffering people of God are also the people for whom the rest is reserved.

On the other side of the scale: πρόσκαιρον ἔχειν ἁμαρτίας ἀπόλαυσιν proskairon echein hamartias apolausin, “to have the fleeting enjoyment of sin.” ἀπόλαυσις apolausis means “enjoyment, pleasure” (Paul uses it in 1 Tim 6:17 of the good things God richly provides “for enjoyment”). The adjective πρόσκαιρος proskairos, “for a season, temporary, transitory,” is the decisive qualifier. Paul uses it in 2 Corinthians 4:18: “the things that are seen are temporary (proskaira), but the things that are not seen are eternal.” Jesus uses it of the shallow hearer who “is temporary” and falls away when tribulation comes (Mark 4:17). The pleasures Moses refused were real, but they were brief; the reward he sought (11:26) was lasting. Hebrews repeatedly sets the transient against the permanent: the readers knew that they had “a better and abiding possession” (10:34), and they are reminded that “here we have no lasting city” (13:14).

Why call this enjoyment “sin”? The pleasures of the Egyptian court were not sinful in themselves; food, learning, wealth, and honor are gifts of God. But for Moses, once he knew who his people were and who his God was, to remain comfortably in Pharaoh’s house while God’s people groaned under the lash would have been an act of unfaithfulness, a denial of the God of the promise. The “sin” in view is, in the context of Hebrews, the sin of apostasy: choosing security and acceptance in the world at the price of disowning God’s people and God’s purposes. That is exactly the danger the letter has been warning against (3:12–13; 10:26–29; 12:16–17). Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal (12:16), is the dark counterpart to Moses, who gave up a kingdom for a birthright he could not yet see.

Pastorally, the verse quietly corrects a common illusion. Sin offers itself as permanent satisfaction; faith sees that its pleasures are proskairos, for a season. Suffering with God’s people looks like loss; faith sees it as the path of those who will inherit. Moses did not choose affliction because suffering is good in itself; he chose it because he weighed where each road ends. The verse does not glorify pain; it calls us to weigh things by their outcome.

11:26

considering the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, for he was looking away to the reward.

Verse 26 gives the inner reasoning that lay behind Moses’ choice. The participle ἡγησάμενος hēgēsamenos, “considering, regarding, reckoning,” is the language of evaluation. Faith is not a leap in the dark; it is a judgment about value made in the light of God’s word. The same verb is used of Sarah, who “considered him faithful who had promised” (11:11), and Paul uses it in his own great reckoning: “whatever gain I had, these I have considered loss for the sake of Christ” (Phil 3:7).

The comparison is between μείζονα πλοῦτον meizona plouton, “greater wealth,” and τῶν Αἰγύπτου θησαυρῶν tōn Aigyptou thēsaurōn, “the treasures of Egypt.” The treasures of Egypt were proverbial. Egypt’s royal storehouses held grain, gold, and goods; its tombs were filled with treasure. Yet Moses reckoned that something else was greater wealth, and the thing he chose sounds like the opposite of wealth: τὸν ὀνειδισμὸν τοῦ Χριστοῦ ton oneidismon tou Christou, “the reproach of Christ.”

Word Study: ὀνειδισμός oneidismos

Oneidismos means “reproach, insult, disgrace, public abuse.” It belongs to the vocabulary of honor and shame: it is what is hurled at someone to lower their standing in the eyes of others. In the Greek Old Testament it often translates the Hebrew חֶרְפָּה cherpah, “scorn, taunt, disgrace.” In Hebrews it appears three times, and the three occurrences interpret each other: the readers had been “publicly exposed to reproaches (ὀνειδισμοῖς oneidismois) and afflictions” (10:33); Moses considered “the reproach of Christ” greater wealth (11:26); and the readers are summoned to go out to Jesus “outside the camp, bearing his reproach (τὸν ὀνειδισμὸν αὐτοῦ ton oneidismon autou)” (13:13). The thread is unmistakable: the reproach the readers suffer, the reproach Moses chose, and the reproach Jesus bore are one and the same reproach.

But in what sense could Moses, some fourteen centuries before Jesus, bear “the reproach of Christ”? This is one of the most discussed phrases in the chapter. Several answers have been given.

Interpretive Options: “The Reproach of Christ”

  • Reproach for the sake of the coming Messiah. Moses, by faith, looked forward to the promised Deliverer, and the reproach he endured was endured for the sake of that future Christ. This is true as far as it goes (compare John 5:46, “he wrote of me”; Deut 18:15), though Hebrews does not say how clearly Moses understood the Messiah.
  • Reproach like Christ’s. Moses suffered the kind of reproach that Christ would later suffer, as a type or foreshadowing. This captures a real pattern (both were rejected deliverers, compare Acts 7:35, 51–52), but it weakens the genitive “of Christ” into a mere comparison.
  • “Christ” as God’s anointed people. Some take Christos, “anointed,” in its Old Testament sense: God’s anointed ones are his people (Ps 105:15), so the reproach of the anointed is the reproach that falls on God’s people. This rightly sees the corporate dimension, but in Hebrews ho Christos always means Jesus the Messiah (3:6, 14; 5:5; 9:11, 14, 24, 28), and the parallel in 13:13 (“his reproach,” that is, Jesus’) is decisive against reading it as a mere synonym for Israel.
  • The reproach that belongs to Christ, shared by his people in every age. Christ is so united with his people that the scorn they suffer for God’s sake is his reproach, and the reproach he bore on the cross is the reproach his people share. Moses, by casting his lot with the people through whom the Messiah would come and for whose redemption he would come, entered into the reproach that the Messiah himself would finally and fully bear.

The last view best fits Hebrews and the wider canon, and it incorporates what is true in the others. Calvin, for example, reasons along these lines: because Christ has always been the head of the church, the reproaches the faithful suffered for God’s promise, even before his coming, were in a true sense his. The Old Testament itself laid the groundwork, as the next box shows.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 89:50–51 (Hebrew 89:51–52) and Psalm 69:9 (Hebrew 69:10)

Hebrew (Ps 89:51–52): זְכֹר אֲדֹנָי חֶרְפַּת עֲבָדֶיךָ שְׂאֵתִי בְחֵיקִי כָּל־רַבִּים עַמִּים׃ אֲשֶׁר חֵרְפוּ אוֹיְבֶיךָ יְהוָה אֲשֶׁר חֵרְפוּ עִקְּבוֹת מְשִׁיחֶךָ — zekhor Adonai cherpat ‘avadekha se’eti vecheqi kol-rabbim ‘ammim; asher cherpu oyevekha YHWH, asher cherpu ‘iqqevot meshichekha, “Remember, O Lord, the reproach of your servants, how I carry in my bosom [the insults of] all the many peoples, with which your enemies have reproached, O Lord, with which they have reproached the footsteps of your anointed.”

Here the “reproach of your servants” and the reproach of “your anointed” (מְשִׁיחֶךָ meshichekha, “your messiah, your anointed one”) are bound together. The Greek version renders “reproach” with oneidismos and “your anointed” with tou christou sou, so that a Greek-speaking reader heard of “the reproach of your servants” and “your Christ” in a single breath. The scorn heaped on God’s people and the scorn heaped on God’s anointed king are one reproach.

Hebrew (Ps 69:10): כִּי־קִנְאַת בֵּיתְךָ אֲכָלָתְנִי וְחֶרְפּוֹת חוֹרְפֶיךָ נָפְלוּ עָלָי — ki-qin’at betekha akhalatni vecherpot chorefekha nafelu ‘alai, “For zeal for your house has consumed me, and the reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me.” Paul applies the second half of this verse directly to Christ: “For Christ did not please himself, but as it is written, ‘The reproaches of those who reproach you fell on me’” (Rom 15:3). Reproach aimed at God falls on his Christ; reproach aimed at God’s people for his sake falls on Christ as well.

This is the scriptural logic of Hebrews 11:26. From the beginning, those who belonged to God and his promise shared in a reproach that would reach its climax at the cross, where the Messiah “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2). Paul can speak of filling up in his flesh “what is lacking in Christ’s afflictions for the sake of his body, the church” (Col 1:24), and the risen Jesus could ask Saul, who was persecuting Christians, “Why are you persecuting me?” (Acts 9:4). Because Christ is one with his people, the scorn they bear for God’s sake is his scorn. Moses, by identifying himself with Abraham’s seed, the people from whom and for whom the Messiah would come, took his place in that line of reproach. Whether Moses could have named Jesus is not the point; Hebrews describes the reality of what he chose, seen now in the full light of its fulfillment.

And Moses reckoned this reproach “greater wealth.” The paradox is deliberate. What the world regards as poverty and disgrace, faith counts as riches, because it is the path along which God’s promised inheritance comes. Jesus himself pronounced the same verdict: “Blessed are you when they reproach (ὀνειδίσωσιν oneidisōsin) you … for your reward is great in heaven” (Matt 5:11–12). Peter adds: “If you are reproached for the name of Christ, you are blessed” (1 Pet 4:14).

The final clause supplies the reason: ἀπέβλεπεν γὰρ εἰς τὴν μισθαποδοσίαν apeblepen gar eis tēn misthapodosian, “for he was looking away to the reward.” The verb ἀποβλέπω apoblepō means “to look away from other things and fix one’s gaze on something.” The imperfect tense pictures a continuing, habitual gaze: Moses kept his eyes on the reward. The same idea, with a related verb, describes the readers’ calling in 12:2: “looking away (ἀφορῶντες aphorōntes) to Jesus.” The noun μισθαποδοσία misthapodosia, “payment of wages, recompense, reward,” is a word Hebrews uses with care. In 2:2 it is the just “recompense” of disobedience; in 10:35 the readers are urged not to throw away their boldness, “which has a great reward”; and in 11:6 God is the one who “becomes a rewarder” (μισθαποδότης misthapodotēs) to those who seek him.

Some have worried that motivation by reward is unworthy, as though true faith should be disinterested. Hebrews does not share that worry, and neither does the rest of Scripture (Gen 15:1; Matt 6:19–21; 2 Tim 4:8). The reward is not wages earned in a commercial sense; it is the inheritance promised by a faithful God, and finally it is God himself and life in his presence (11:16; 12:22–24). To desire it is simply to believe that God is good and that his promises are better than Egypt’s treasures. Moses was not giving up wealth for nothing; he was exchanging lesser wealth for greater, the transient for the eternal. That is the arithmetic of faith.

11:27

By faith he left Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible.

The third “by faith” statement about Moses raises a well-known question. The verb κατέλιπεν katelipen, aorist of καταλείπω kataleipō, “leave behind, abandon, forsake,” is singular: “he left Egypt.” But Moses left Egypt twice in the story: first when he fled to Midian after killing the Egyptian (Exod 2:15), and then forty years later when he led Israel out at the exodus (Exod 12–14). Which departure does Hebrews mean?

Old Testament Text: Exodus 2:14–15

Hebrew: וַיִּירָא מֹשֶׁה וַיֹּאמַר אָכֵן נוֹדַע הַדָּבָר׃ וַיִּשְׁמַע פַּרְעֹה אֶת־הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה וַיְבַקֵּשׁ לַהֲרֹג אֶת־מֹשֶׁה וַיִּבְרַח מֹשֶׁה מִפְּנֵי פַרְעֹה וַיֵּשֶׁב בְּאֶרֶץ־מִדְיָן — vayyira Moshe vayyomar akhen noda‘ haddavar. vayyishma‘ Par‘oh et-haddavar hazzeh vayvaqqesh laharog et-Moshe vayyivrach Moshe mippene Far‘oh vayyeshev be’erets-Midyan, “Then Moses was afraid, and he said, ‘Surely the thing is known.’ When Pharaoh heard of this thing, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from Pharaoh and stayed in the land of Midian.”

The difficulty is plain: Exodus says Moses “was afraid” (וַיִּירָא vayyira), while Hebrews says he left “not fearing the wrath of the king.”

Interpretive Options: Which Departure?

  • The flight to Midian (Exod 2:15). In favor: (1) Hebrews has been following the order of Exodus, and this statement stands before the Passover (11:28), which preceded the exodus; (2) the verb is singular, describing Moses alone, while the exodus is described in the plural in 11:29 (“they crossed”); (3) the phrase “the wrath of the king” fits Exodus 2:15, where Pharaoh sought to kill Moses; (4) the theme of 11:24–26 (Moses’ break with Egypt) leads naturally into his departure from Egypt as its consequence. Against: Exodus 2:14 says Moses was afraid.
  • The exodus (Exod 12–14). In favor: at the exodus Moses truly defied Pharaoh, who had threatened him with death (Exod 10:28–29) and then pursued Israel in fury (Exod 14:5–8); “left Egypt” is the natural description of the exodus; and Moses’ fearlessness there is explicit (Exod 14:13). Against: it breaks the chronological order of the chapter, since the Passover (11:28) came before the departure, and Pharaoh at the moment of departure was urging Israel to leave (Exod 12:31–33).
  • A summary of Moses’ whole break with Egypt. Some suggest the author compresses both departures, or Moses’ entire stance toward Egypt, into one statement. This is possible but less precise than the author usually is.

The arguments from order, the singular verb, and the flow from 11:24–26 weigh in favor of the flight to Midian, and many modern interpreters take it so. How then can Moses be said not to fear? The answer lies in what Hebrews means by fear. Exodus says Moses was afraid because the matter had become known; it does not say he was afraid of the king in the sense of submitting to him. The question Hebrews asks is what governed his choice. Moses did not flee in order to make peace with Pharaoh, return to the palace, and recover his place as a son of Pharaoh’s daughter. He left Egypt behind (kataleipō has the sense of abandoning, forsaking), refusing the one move that would have secured the king’s favor. His flight was the outworking of his refusal (11:24), not a retreat from it. Prudent withdrawal from danger is not the same as fear of man; Jesus himself withdrew from those who sought to kill him until his hour had come (John 8:59; 10:39–40), and he told his disciples to flee from one town to the next when persecuted (Matt 10:23). What faith refuses is not caution but capitulation. Whichever departure one adopts, the theological point is the same: Moses’ allegiance was not governed by Pharaoh’s anger.

The noun θυμός thymos, “wrath, fury, rage,” denotes passionate anger. The king of Egypt, who would have seemed to most of his subjects the most powerful being on earth, regarded as a god, was nonetheless not the one Moses feared. Faith relocates fear: “Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul; rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body” (Matt 10:28).

The reason (γάρ gar, “for”) is given in one of the most beautiful phrases of the chapter: τὸν γὰρ ἀόρατον ὡς ὁρῶν ἐκαρτέρησεν ton gar aoraton hōs horōn ekarterēsen, “for he endured as seeing him who is invisible.” The Greek word order places “the invisible one” first, for emphasis, and sets it immediately beside “seeing”: literally, “the unseen one as seeing.” It is a deliberate paradox. ἀόρατος aoratos, “invisible, unseen,” is used in the New Testament of God (Col 1:15; 1 Tim 1:17) and of his eternal power and divine nature, which are known through creation (Rom 1:20). God cannot be seen with bodily eyes: “you cannot see my face, for no one may see me and live” (Exod 33:20). Yet Moses, more than any other prophet, was granted a unique nearness: the Lord spoke with him “mouth to mouth,” and “he beholds the form of the Lord” (Num 12:8, וּתְמֻנַת יְהוָה יַבִּיט utemunat YHWH yabbit). Deuteronomy closes by saying that no prophet arose like Moses, “whom the Lord knew face to face” (Deut 34:10).

But Hebrews does not say that Moses saw God; it says that he endured as seeing (ὡς ὁρῶν hōs horōn). The particle hōs here does not mean “as if, pretending,” but “as one who sees”: Moses lived with the settled awareness of God’s presence that a person has who sees someone standing before him. The present participle horōn, “seeing,” describes an ongoing perception. This is exactly the definition of faith given at the beginning of the chapter: “the conviction of things not seen” (11:1). Faith is a kind of seeing; it perceives the unseen God as real, present, and decisive, and so it is not paralyzed by the very visible wrath of a king.

The main verb, ἐκαρτέρησεν ekarterēsen, from καρτερέω kartereō, occurs only here in the New Testament. It comes from κάρτος kartos (strength) and means “to be strong, steadfast, to hold out, persevere, endure patiently.” Greek moral writers used it of steadfast endurance under hardship. If the flight to Midian is in view, the word is especially fitting: Moses endured forty years of obscurity as a shepherd in the desert (Acts 7:29–30), far from the palace and seemingly far from any fulfillment of his hopes for his people, until God appeared to him in the burning bush. The aorist sums up that whole period of endurance as a single, completed achievement. The burning bush itself was a moment when the invisible God made himself known in a visible sign, and Moses “hid his face, for he was afraid to look at God” (Exod 3:6). But long before and long after that encounter, Moses’ endurance was sustained by an inner sight of the unseen.

This is precisely what the first readers needed. Their calling was to “endure” (ὑπομονή hypomonē, 10:36; 12:1–3), and they were tempted to measure reality by what they could see: the visible temple, the visible priesthood, the visible threat of the authorities. The preacher says: Moses held out because he saw the invisible God. Christians now “see Jesus … crowned with glory and honor” (2:9) by the same faith, and that sight steadies the soul.

11:28

By faith he kept the Passover and the sprinkling of the blood, so that the destroyer of the firstborn would not touch them.

The fourth Moses statement turns to the night of the Passover (Exod 12). The verb is unusual: πεποίηκεν pepoiēken, the perfect tense of ποιέω poieō, “do, make, keep (a festival).” Most of the verbs in the chapter are aorists, simply narrating past events. The perfect tense in Greek describes a past action whose result continues. Westcott and others have seen significance here: Moses did not only keep a Passover once; by faith he instituted a festival that remained in force, kept year by year “throughout your generations, as a statute forever” (Exod 12:14). The Passover Moses kept by faith was still being celebrated by Jews in the author’s own day. Whether or not the perfect should be pressed this far, it is true to the Exodus narrative, which presents the first Passover as the foundation of a permanent memorial.

The faith involved was remarkable. Moses received from God a command that would have seemed strange to anyone judging by sight: slaughter a lamb, put its blood on the doorposts and lintel, eat it in haste, and stay indoors until morning, because the Lord would pass through Egypt and strike every firstborn (Exod 12:1–13, 21–23). Nothing visible supported the promise; the night had not yet come. Moses believed God’s word about a judgment not yet seen (compare Noah in 11:7, “warned about things not yet seen”), and he believed God’s promise that the blood would protect.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 12:13, 22–23

Hebrew (Exod 12:13): וְהָיָה הַדָּם לָכֶם לְאֹת עַל הַבָּתִּים אֲשֶׁר אַתֶּם שָׁם וְרָאִיתִי אֶת־הַדָּם וּפָסַחְתִּי עֲלֵכֶם וְלֹא־יִהְיֶה בָכֶם נֶגֶף לְמַשְׁחִית בְּהַכֹּתִי בְּאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם — vehayah haddam lakhem le’ot ‘al habbattim asher attem sham vera’iti et-haddam ufasachti ‘alekhem velo-yihyeh vakhem negef lemashchit behakkoti be’erets Mitsrayim, “The blood shall be a sign for you on the houses where you are. When I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague will be among you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.”

Hebrew (Exod 12:22–23, in part): וּלְקַחְתֶּם אֲגֻדַּת אֵזוֹב וּטְבַלְתֶּם בַּדָּם אֲשֶׁר־בַּסַּף וְהִגַּעְתֶּם אֶל־הַמַּשְׁקוֹף וְאֶל־שְׁתֵּי הַמְּזוּזֹת — ulqachtem aguddat ezov utevaltem baddam asher-bassaf vehigga‘tem el-hammashqof ve’el-shete hammezuzot, “Take a bunch of hyssop, dip it in the blood that is in the basin, and touch the lintel and the two doorposts …”; וּפָסַח יְהוָה עַל־הַפֶּתַח וְלֹא יִתֵּן הַמַּשְׁחִית לָבֹא אֶל־בָּתֵּיכֶם לִנְגֹּף — ufasach YHWH ‘al-happetach velo yitten hammashchit lavo el-battekhem lingof, “the Lord will pass over the door and will not allow the destroyer to enter your houses to strike you.”

The Hebrew verb פָּסַח pasach, from which pesach, “Passover,” is named, means “pass over,” and in context it carries the sense of sparing and shielding: the Lord stands over the marked door and does not allow the destroyer in. “The destroyer” is הַמַּשְׁחִית hammashchit, a participle meaning “the one who destroys.” The Greek translation of Exodus 12:23 renders it with a participle from the same verb family that Hebrews uses.

The Greek word for Passover, πάσχα pascha, is a transliteration of the Aramaic form of pesach. Alongside it Hebrews names “the sprinkling of the blood,” τὴν πρόσχυσιν τοῦ αἵματος tēn proschysin tou haimatos. πρόσχυσις proschysis occurs only here in the Bible. It is related to the verb προσχέω proscheō, “pour out against, dash against,” which the Greek Old Testament uses for the priests’ dashing of sacrificial blood against the altar (for example Lev 1:5, 11). The author has chosen a word with sacrificial associations. The Passover lamb was not merely a meal; its blood was applied as the sign of protection, and the lamb died in place of the firstborn.

The purpose clause names the danger: ἵνα μὴ ὁ ὀλοθρεύων τὰ πρωτότοκα θίγῃ αὐτῶν hina mē ho olothreuōn ta prōtotoka thigē autōn, “so that the one destroying the firstborn would not touch them.” ὁ ὀλοθρεύων ho olothreuōn, “the destroyer,” is a present participle used as a noun, from ὀλοθρεύω olothreuō, “destroy utterly.” The neuter plural τὰ πρωτότοκα ta prōtotoka, “the firstborn,” includes both humans and animals, as in Exodus 12:12 (“from man to beast”). The verb θιγγάνω thinganō, “touch,” appears again in 12:20, of the beast that must not touch Mount Sinai: in both places, contact with the holy power of God is deadly apart from his provision. And “them” (autōn) refers to the Israelites, whose firstborn were spared.

Who is “the destroyer”? Exodus 12 speaks both of the Lord himself striking the firstborn (12:12, 29) and of “the destroyer” whom the Lord would not allow to enter the protected houses (12:23). Elsewhere Scripture speaks of a destroying angel sent by God in judgment (2 Sam 24:16, where the same Hebrew word appears: הַמַּלְאָךְ הַמַּשְׁחִית hammal’akh hammashchit, “the destroying angel”), and Psalm 78:49 describes the Egyptian plagues as “a band of angels of disaster.” Paul warns the Corinthians against grumbling as some of the wilderness generation did and “were destroyed by the destroyer” (1 Cor 10:10, using the related noun olothreutēs). The best reading is that the destroyer is an agent of God’s judgment, acting under his authority; the judgment is God’s own, and the protection is God’s own. There is no hint of a power acting independently of him.

The Christian reader cannot miss the significance. Paul writes, “Christ our Passover lamb has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7). John records that the Lord’s bones were not broken, “that the Scripture might be fulfilled” (John 19:36, echoing Exod 12:46). Hebrews has already explained that “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22), and it will soon speak of “the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel” (12:24) and of “the church of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven” (12:23). Believers are God’s firstborn, sheltered from judgment by the blood of the Lamb. Moses believed God’s word about a blood that protected one night in Egypt; the readers are called to trust the blood of Jesus, which secures “an eternal redemption” (9:12). To abandon that blood would be to step outside the only door the destroyer cannot enter (10:29).

11:29

By faith they passed through the Red Sea as though on dry land; and when the Egyptians attempted it, they were swallowed up.

The subject changes from “he” to “they.” The faith of Moses now becomes the faith of the people as they pass through the sea. The verb διέβησαν diebēsan, from διαβαίνω diabainō, “go through, cross over,” describes the whole crossing as one completed event.

Historical Background: “The Red Sea”

The Hebrew name of the sea is יַם־סוּף yam-suf (Exod 15:4; Josh 2:10), literally “Sea of Reeds” (suf is the word for the reeds among which Moses’ basket was placed, Exod 2:3). The Greek translators rendered it Ἐρυθρὰ Θάλασσα Erythra Thalassa, “Red Sea,” the name Greeks used for the waters south and east of Egypt, including what we call the Red Sea and its gulfs. The exact location of the crossing has been much debated and cannot be settled with certainty. Hebrews, like Stephen (Acts 7:36), simply uses the familiar Greek name. What matters for the text is not the precise geography but the event: God opened a way through deep waters that no army could pass.

“As though on dry land” (ὡς διὰ ξηρᾶς γῆς hōs dia xēras gēs) echoes Exodus 14:22: וַיָּבֹאוּ בְנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל בְּתוֹךְ הַיָּם בַּיַּבָּשָׁה וְהַמַּיִם לָהֶם חֹמָה מִימִינָם וּמִשְּׂמֹאלָם — vayyavo’u vene-Yisra’el betokh hayyam bayyabbashah vehammayim lahem chomah mimminam umissemolam, “And the people of Israel went into the midst of the sea on dry ground, and the waters were a wall to them on their right and on their left.” The Hebrew word for “wall” here, חוֹמָה chomah, is the same word used of the wall of Jericho that fell (Josh 6:5, 20). The walls of water stood for God’s people; the wall of stone fell before them. Hebrews places the two events side by side (11:29–30).

What was the faith involved? Israel at the sea was not a model of steady trust. When they saw Pharaoh’s army, they cried out in terror and accused Moses of bringing them out to die (Exod 14:10–12). Moses answered, “Do not fear; stand firm and see the salvation of the Lord” (Exod 14:13). Then at God’s command they went forward into the sea bed between walls of water, which is itself an act of trust. Exodus concludes: “Israel saw the great hand that the Lord had used against Egypt, and the people feared the Lord, and they believed in the Lord and in Moses his servant” (Exod 14:31: וַיַּאֲמִינוּ בַּיהוָה וּבְמֹשֶׁה עַבְדּוֹ vayya’aminu baYHWH uveMoshe ‘avdo). Faith here is the willingness to walk where God has opened a way, even when the way runs through the very waters that threaten death.

The second half of the verse draws a sharp contrast: ἧς πεῖραν λαβόντες οἱ Αἰγύπτιοι κατεπόθησαν hēs peiran labontes hoi Aigyptioi katepothēsan, literally “of which [the sea, or the dry ground] the Egyptians, taking a trial, were swallowed up.” The idiom πεῖραν λαμβάνω peiran lambanō, “to take a trial of,” means “to attempt” or “to experience.” It reappears in 11:36, where the faithful “experienced” mockings and scourgings. The Egyptians attempted the very same path that Israel took, but without faith, and the same sea that was a road for Israel became a grave for them. The difference was not in the route but in the relationship to God. What faith does in obedience, presumption cannot copy.

“They were swallowed up” (κατεπόθησαν katepothēsan, aorist passive of καταπίνω katapinō, “drink down, gulp, swallow”) is vivid. In the Song of the Sea, Moses sings: נָטִיתָ יְמִינְךָ תִּבְלָעֵמוֹ אָרֶץ — natita yeminkha tivla‘emo arets, “You stretched out your right hand; the earth swallowed them” (Exod 15:12). Exodus 14:28 adds: “not one of them remained.”

One more feature of the verse is worth noticing. The “they” who crossed the sea by faith are the very generation whose later unbelief Hebrews has already described in sobering terms: “Who were those who heard and rebelled? Was it not all those who came out of Egypt through Moses?” (3:16). The same people who passed through the sea by faith fell in the wilderness through unbelief (3:17–19; compare 1 Cor 10:1–5). It is surely significant that after 11:29 the author passes over the entire wilderness period without a single “by faith” and resumes only at Jericho with the next generation. The lesson for the readers is solemn: a moment of faith at the beginning is not enough. They too had once “endured a great contest of sufferings” (10:32); what they need now is the faith that continues to the end (3:14; 10:36–39).
