Hebrews 13

Outside the Camp: Sacrifices Pleasing to God

Chapter 12 closed on Mount Zion before the unshakable kingdom, with the summons, “Let us have grace, through which we may worship God in a way pleasing to him, with reverence and awe” (12:28), and with the warning that “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29). Chapter 13 tells the congregation what that pleasing worship looks like on an ordinary day. The verb in 12:28 was λατρεύωμεν latreuōmen, “let us worship” or “serve” as a priest serves. The adverb was εὐαρέστως euarestōs, “in a way pleasing.” Chapter 13 takes up both. It speaks of an altar, of sacrifices, of those who “serve the tabernacle” (13:10), and twice more of what is “pleasing” to God (13:16, 21). Under the new covenant the worship God accepts is brotherly love, hospitality, care for prisoners, faithful marriage, contentment, steadfast faith, praise, and sharing with others, all offered “through him,” Jesus, whose own blood was shed “outside the gate.”

The chapter has four movements. (1) Verses 1–6 give a series of short commands about life together: love, hospitality, prisoners, marriage, money. They end with God’s promise never to abandon his people. (2) Verses 7–19 are framed by two calls about “your leaders” (13:7, 17). At their center stands the confession that “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever” (13:8), followed by the summons to go out to him “outside the camp” (13:13). (3) Verses 20–21 are a benediction, a prayer of great theological richness. In it Hebrews explicitly mentions Jesus’ resurrection for the only time. (4) Verses 22–25 are a short letter-ending with news and greetings.

Some older critics asked whether chapter 13 originally belonged to Hebrews. Chapters 1–12 open like a sermon. They have no greeting, no named sender, and no named recipients. Chapter 13 shifts to short commands, and in its last verses it reads like the close of a Pauline letter, with Timothy, travel plans, and greetings. A few scholars in the early twentieth century, notably William Wrede, suggested that the ending had been added later, perhaps to give the work a Pauline look. Others thought the whole chapter was a separate fragment. Most interpreters today reject these theories, and the text itself gives good reason to.

  • The vocabulary of chapters 1–12 runs all through chapter 13. Love must “remain” (μενέτω menetō, 13:1), echoing the “lasting” possession of 10:34 and the things that “remain” in 12:27. There is “no lasting city” (13:14). God says “I will not … forsake you” (13:5, ἐγκαταλίπω enkatalipō), which answers the warning against “forsaking” the assembly (10:25). The Christ who is “the same” (13:8) is the Son who is “the same” in 1:12. Remembering “the prisoners” (13:3) recalls 10:34. The “sexually immoral” (13:4) recalls Esau the πόρνος pornos (12:16), who sold his birthright “for a single meal.” That meal in turn connects with the “foods” of 13:9. “Grace” (13:9, 25) picks up 4:16, 10:29, 12:15, and 12:28. “Reproach” (13:13) repeats 10:33 and 11:26. “The city that is to come” (13:14) gathers up 11:10, 11:16, and 12:22. “Confess” (13:15) echoes the “confession” of 3:1, 4:14, and 10:23. “A good conscience” (13:18) picks up the cleansed conscience of 9:14 and 10:22. “Blood of the covenant,” “sanctify,” “tabernacle,” “the high priest,” “outside the camp,” and the Day of Atonement (13:10–12, 20) summarize the argument of chapters 9–10. “Do his will” (13:21) echoes 10:7, 9, and 36. “Word of exhortation” (13:22) echoes 12:5.
  • The argument of 13:9–16 depends on chapters 7–10. It assumes everything the author has said about the Levitical cult, the Day of Atonement, and Christ’s self-offering. A detached fragment, or an ending written by someone else, would hardly reason so closely in the main author’s own terms.
  • Ancient sermons commonly closed with practical exhortation, and a sermon sent to an absent congregation naturally needed a few lines of letter-ending (13:22–25). The sudden change of style fits the change of purpose. It does not point to a change of author.

For these reasons chapter 13 is best read as the author’s own conclusion. It is the place where the high priestly theology of the sermon is turned into the shape of a congregation’s life.

Some key words to watch: θυσία thysia, “sacrifice” (13:15, 16); εὐάρεστος euarestos, “pleasing” (13:16, 21); ἡγούμενοι hēgoumenoi, “leaders” (13:7, 17, 24); ἔξω exō, “outside” (13:11, 12, 13); and χάρις charis, “grace,” which appears near the start of the doctrinal section (13:9) and in the final word of the book (13:25). In this chapter the great “let us” of Hebrews takes its last form. Earlier it was “let us draw near” (4:16; 10:22). Now it is “let us go out to him” (13:13) and “let us offer up a sacrifice of praise” (13:15). Those who have entered God’s presence through Jesus now follow him outside the gate, bearing his reproach, on the way to the city that is to come.
