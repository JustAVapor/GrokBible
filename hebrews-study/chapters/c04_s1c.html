4:7

he again appoints a certain day, “Today,” saying through David after so long a time, just as has been said before, “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.”

This verse completes the sentence begun in 4:6. Since the rest must still be entered by some, and the first hearers failed to enter, God “again appoints a certain day.” The verb ὁρίζει horizei, from ὁρίζω horizō (“to mark out a boundary, determine, appoint”; English “horizon” comes from the same root), is in the present tense. God sets a fixed day, a definite boundary in time. The day is named with a single word: Σήμερον Sēmeron, “Today.”

The force of the argument lies in the timing. God spoke this “Today” “through David after so long a time.” The wilderness generation lived and died under Moses; Joshua then led the next generation into Canaan. Psalm 95, which the preacher attributes to David, was spoken centuries later, long after the conquest. If Israel had already entered God’s rest under Joshua, why would God, through David, still be calling his people to hear his voice “today” and warning them not to miss the rest as their fathers did? The very existence of the psalm proves that the offer was still open in David’s day. And since the psalm is still being read and sung, it is still open in the hearers’ day too.

The phrase “through David” is literally “in David” (ἐν Δαυίδ en Dauid). It can mean “through David as the speaker” or “in David” as the name for the book of Psalms, much as Jews could speak of reading “in Moses” for the Torah. In either case the speaker is God. The Hebrew text of Psalm 95 has no title naming an author, but the Greek Septuagint gives it a heading that attributes it to David, and Jewish tradition commonly treated the Psalter as David’s book. The New Testament does the same with other untitled psalms (compare Acts 4:25, which cites Psalm 2 as spoken by the Holy Spirit “through the mouth of David”). Notice that in 3:7 this same psalm was introduced with “as the Holy Spirit says.” For Hebrews, God speaks, the Spirit speaks, and David speaks—all at once. The human writer is the instrument of the divine voice.

“Just as has been said before” (καθὼς προείρηται kathōs proeirētai) refers back to the preacher’s earlier quotations of the psalm in 3:7–8 and 3:15. He now quotes its opening warning a third time.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 95:7–8

Hebrew: הַיּוֹם אִם־בְּקֹלוֹ תִשְׁמָעוּ אַל־תַּקְשׁוּ לְבַבְכֶם — hayyom im-beqolo tishma‘u, al-taqshu levavkhem, “Today, if you would listen to his voice—do not harden your heart.”

The Hebrew word im usually means “if,” but in this kind of sentence it can also express a longing wish: “O that today you would listen to his voice!” Either way the psalm presses for an immediate response. The verb “listen” (shama‘) in Hebrew often means more than hearing sounds; it means to heed and obey. To “hear his voice” is to submit to it. The Greek of Hebrews, following the Septuagint, renders the clause as a condition: “if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.”

The psalm itself began as a call to worship: “Come, let us bow down and kneel; let us bless the Lord our Maker” (Ps 95:6). The warning about the wilderness follows the invitation to enter God’s presence in praise. Hebrews keeps that order. The rest it holds out is fellowship with God, and the hearers are called to enter it as worshipers, not merely as people seeking relief.

The repeated “Today” is the heartbeat of the passage. Earlier the preacher urged, “exhort one another every day, as long as it is called ‘Today’” (3:13). God’s “Today” is the time of opportunity that stretches from the moment his voice is heard to the moment the opportunity closes—whether by death, by the return of Christ, or by the hardening of the heart. Paul speaks the same way when he quotes Isaiah 49:8: “Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation” (2 Cor 6:2). The gospel is never a word merely about the past or the future. It always speaks in the present tense: “Today, if you hear his voice.” The danger is that the hearer will treat God’s “Today” as “tomorrow.”

4:8

For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken afterward about another day.

The preacher now anticipates an objection. Someone might say, “But Israel did enter the land under Joshua. Was that not the rest?” The answer comes in the form of a contrary-to-fact condition. In Greek, a condition with εἰ ei (“if”) plus a past tense verb, followed by a main clause with the particle ἄν an, describes something that did not happen: “If Joshua had given them rest (but he did not), God would not have spoken of another day (but he did).” The fact that God later spoke of “another day” proves that Joshua did not give the final rest.

The verb is κατέπαυσεν katepausen, the same verb used of God in 4:4, but here it is transitive: Joshua “caused them to rest,” “gave them rest.” This is precisely the language of the book of Joshua. Moses told the eastern tribes that “the Lord your God is giving you rest” (Josh 1:13). At the end of the conquest the narrator declares, וַיָּנַח יְהוָה לָהֶם מִסָּבִיב wayyanach YHWH lahem missaviv, “and the Lord gave them rest all around” (Josh 21:44). Joshua tells the eastern tribes, וְעַתָּה הֵנִיחַ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם לַאֲחֵיכֶם we‘attah heniach YHWH eloheikhem la’acheikhem, “and now the Lord your God has given rest to your brothers” (Josh 22:4; compare 23:1). The Greek translation of Joshua uses the same verb, katapauō.

So the preacher does not deny that Joshua’s generation received a rest. They did. God kept his promise about the land, and the book of Joshua celebrates that “not one word failed of all the good promises” (Josh 21:45). But that rest was real and yet provisional. The book of Judges shows enemies still in the land and Israel repeatedly falling into idolatry and oppression. Even when David later enjoyed rest from his enemies (2 Sam 7:1) and Solomon built the temple, the psalm still said “Today,” still warned, and still spoke of a rest yet to be entered. The land was a gift, but it was also a pointer—a pledge and picture of a greater inheritance. Hebrews 11 will say that Abraham, living in the promised land as a foreigner, “was looking for the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (11:10), and that the patriarchs desired “a better country, that is, a heavenly one” (11:16).

Historical Background: Joshua and Jesus—One Name

In Greek, Joshua’s name is Ἰησοῦς Iēsous—exactly the same name as Jesus. The Hebrew name is יְהוֹשֻׁעַ Yehoshua, meaning “the Lord saves” or “the Lord is salvation.” Numbers 13:16 records that Moses gave this name to his assistant: וַיִּקְרָא מֹשֶׁה לְהוֹשֵׁעַ בִּן־נוּן יְהוֹשֻׁעַ wayyiqra mosheh lehoshea‘ bin-nun yehoshua‘, “and Moses called Hoshea the son of Nun ‘Joshua.’” The shorter form Yeshua was common in the time of Jesus, and in Greek both forms became Iēsous. The angel’s words to Joseph draw on this meaning: “you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins” (Matt 1:21).

For Greek-speaking hearers who read the Old Testament in Greek, Hebrews 4:8 would literally read, “For if Jesus had given them rest….” Context makes clear that Joshua son of Nun is meant, and the preacher draws no explicit wordplay. Yet the hearers could hardly miss the resonance. The first Iēsous led God’s people into the land but could not give them the final rest. The second Iēsous, the Son, leads “many sons to glory” as their pioneer (2:10) and has entered the heavenly sanctuary as their forerunner (6:20). Jesus himself said, “Come to me, all who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest” (Matt 11:28). Early Christian writers such as Justin Martyr made much of the Joshua–Jesus parallel. Hebrews’ restraint is instructive: the point rests on the argument from Psalm 95, not on the name. But the name underlines the point. What the first Joshua could only foreshadow, the greater Jesus accomplishes.

The phrase “about another day” (περὶ ἄλλης … ἡμέρας peri allēs … hēmeras) refers to the “Today” of Psalm 95. The word order in Greek separates “another” from “day” by several words, placing weight on “another.” God’s speaking of another day, after Joshua, shows that the true rest lay beyond the conquest. This is an important principle for reading the Old Testament: its partial fulfillments open forward to a greater fulfillment in Christ. Hebrews will use the same kind of reasoning about the priesthood (7:11: if perfection had come through the Levitical priesthood, why speak of another priest?) and about the covenant (8:7: if the first covenant had been faultless, there would have been no occasion for a second). In each case, a later word of God shows that the earlier arrangement was not final.

4:9

So then, a Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God;

Here is the conclusion of the whole argument from 4:3 onward, introduced with ἄρα ara, “so then, consequently.” Since God’s rest has existed since creation (4:3–4), since the wilderness generation did not enter it (4:5–6), and since Joshua’s conquest did not exhaust it (4:7–8), it follows that a rest “remains” (ἀπολείπεται apoleipetai, the same verb as in 4:6) for God’s people. It is still waiting, unclaimed by the generations of the past, open to the generation that hears “Today.”

The striking feature of the verse is that the preacher suddenly changes his word for rest. Everywhere else in 3:11–4:11 he uses katapausis. Here he uses σαββατισμός sabbatismos.

Word Study: σαββατισμός sabbatismos

This noun appears nowhere in the Greek Old Testament and nowhere else in the New Testament. It is not found in any surviving Greek writing earlier than Hebrews, so the author may have coined it, or he may have taken it from the Greek-speaking synagogue. It is formed from the verb σαββατίζω sabbatizō, “to keep Sabbath,” which the Greek Old Testament uses several times. In Exodus 16:30, after the giving of the manna, “the people kept Sabbath on the seventh day” (Hebrew וַיִּשְׁבְּתוּ הָעָם בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִעִי wayyishbetu ha‘am bayyom hashevi‘i). Leviticus uses it of the Day of Atonement: “from evening to evening you shall keep your Sabbath” (תִּשְׁבְּתוּ שַׁבַּתְּכֶם tishbetu shabbatkhem, Lev 23:32), and of the land enjoying its Sabbaths (Lev 26:34–35; 2 Chr 36:21).

Greek nouns ending in -ismos usually describe an activity. So sabbatismos means not simply “a rest” but “a Sabbath-keeping,” the celebration of Sabbath. When the word appears in later writers—the Greek moralist Plutarch, writing a little after Hebrews, seems to use it of Jewish Sabbath observance, and some early Christian writers use it as well—it refers to the actual observance of the Sabbath, with its rest and worship. The choice of word therefore adds a new color to the idea of rest. The rest that remains is not mere inactivity or the absence of trouble. It is a festive, worshiping rest, a perpetual Sabbath celebration in the presence of God. It is the kind of rest God himself enjoyed on the seventh day when he blessed it and made it holy (Gen 2:3).

Why does the preacher make this change? Because he has just shown that the rest of Psalm 95 is God’s seventh-day rest (4:4). The word sabbatismos sums up that discovery in a single term: the rest that remains is a Sabbath rest, a sharing in God’s own Sabbath. The weekly Sabbath of Israel was a sign of this (Exod 31:13–17). Each Sabbath Israel stopped its labors, remembered God’s creation and redemption (Exod 20:11; Deut 5:15), and enjoyed a day set apart to God. Hebrews teaches that the weekly Sabbath pointed beyond itself to the great Sabbath of God, into which his people are invited.

Historical Background: The Sabbath and the Age to Come

The hearers, as Jewish Christians, would have known that their people already connected the Sabbath with the hope of the world to come. Psalm 92 bears the title מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת mizmor shir leyom hashabbat, “A psalm, a song for the Sabbath day” (Ps 92:1 in the Hebrew numbering). The Mishnah, which records the psalms the Levites sang in the temple on each day of the week, says that on the Sabbath they sang Psalm 92, and it explains the title as “a psalm, a song for the time to come, for the day that is all Sabbath and rest for everlasting life” (m. Tamid 7:4). The Mishnah was written down around AD 200, but it preserves older traditions about temple practice, and this understanding of the Sabbath as a foretaste of the age to come was likely current in the first century.

Early Christians developed similar ideas. The Epistle of Barnabas (chapter 15), written probably within a few decades of Hebrews, interpreted God’s six days of work and seventh day of rest as a picture of history culminating in a final age of rest. Its schemes of world-history go beyond anything Scripture teaches and should not be adopted. But such texts show that the link Hebrews draws between the Sabbath and the final rest was a natural one for its hearers. What is new in Hebrews is not the hope of a final Sabbath but the claim that this Sabbath is entered through faith in Jesus, the Son, and that it is open “Today.”

The recipients of this Sabbath-rest are “the people of God” (τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ tō laō tou theou). This was Israel’s great title, the people God had chosen and redeemed (Exod 6:7; Deut 7:6). Hebrews applies it to all who belong to Christ. The Son made propitiation “for the sins of the people” (2:17), and Jesus suffered outside the gate “to sanctify the people through his own blood” (13:12). Peter likewise says that those who were once “not a people” are now “the people of God” (1 Pet 2:10, echoing Hos 2:23). The rest promised to Israel is inherited by the believing people of God, Jew and Gentile, gathered in Christ—the true continuation of the faithful remnant like Joshua and Caleb.

Does this verse teach anything about the weekly day of rest for Christians? Christians have held different convictions on that question, and the New Testament speaks to it elsewhere (Rom 14:5–6; Col 2:16–17; compare Acts 20:7; Rev 1:10). Hebrews 4:9 does not address it directly. Its concern is the reality to which the Sabbath pointed: God’s own rest, now opened to his people. Whatever one’s conviction about the weekly day, all can agree on what this verse affirms: the deepest meaning of the Sabbath is fulfilled in the rest God gives his people in Christ, which they enter by faith now and will enjoy fully at the end. Augustine closed his great work The City of God by describing the final Sabbath, in which the saints will rest and see, see and love, love and praise, in a day that has no end. That is the hope Hebrews holds before weary pilgrims.

4:10

for the one who has entered his rest has himself also rested from his works, just as God did from his own.

This verse explains (“for,” γάρ gar) why the rest that remains can be called a Sabbath-rest. The one who enters God’s rest does what God did on the seventh day: he rests from his works. The parallel is exact. Literally the Greek reads, “For the one having entered into his rest, he himself also rested from his works, just as from his own [works] God [rested].” The words καὶ αὐτός kai autos, “he himself also,” stress that the one who enters shares God’s own experience. The phrase ἀπὸ τῶν ἰδίων apo tōn idiōn, “from his own,” refers to God’s own works of creation (4:3–4).

Both verbs are in the aorist tense: “the one who entered” (ὁ εἰσελθών ho eiselthōn) and “rested” (κατέπαυσεν katepausen). The aorist often views an action as a completed whole. Here it may describe a general truth (“whoever enters, rests”), or it may point to a particular person who has already entered. That second possibility gives rise to an important interpretive question.

Interpretive Options: Who is “the one who has entered his rest”?

  • The believer. Most interpreters, ancient and modern, take the singular as generic: any believer who enters God’s rest. On this view, 4:10 describes what the rest consists of for the people of God mentioned in 4:9: they cease from their labors as God ceased from his.
  • Christ. John Owen and a few others have argued that the verse refers to Christ. The singular, the aorist tense, and the emphatic “he himself also” suggest a particular person who has already entered. Christ finished his work (John 19:30), entered heaven, and “sat down” (Heb 1:3; 10:12), resting from his works as God rested from his. On this view, believers enter rest because their forerunner has gone ahead of them (6:20).

The Christ view is attractive and expresses a truth Hebrews teaches elsewhere. Nevertheless, the context favors the first view. Christ has not been mentioned since 3:14, and nothing in 4:1–11 signals a change of subject from the people who must enter. Verse 10 explains verse 9 (“a Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God; for the one who has entered …”), so the one who enters is naturally a member of that people. Verse 11 then draws the application: “Let us therefore strive to enter that rest”—the rest just described in which one ceases from one’s works. The aorist is best taken as a general statement: entering the rest means resting from one’s works. The generic singular “the one who …” is common in Scripture for “anyone who” (compare 11:6, “whoever would draw near to God”). Still, the Christ view reminds us of something true: believers enter God’s rest only in union with the one who has entered first. We are “partakers of Christ” (3:14), and where he has gone, his people follow.

What, then, are the “works” from which the believer rests? Here careful reading is needed, because this verse has often been used in discussions of faith and works. The text compares the believer’s works with God’s works. God’s works of creation were not sinful, and his resting from them was not repentance. They were good works, finished and complete. The primary sense, therefore, is that the people of God will cease from their labors, toils, and struggles when they enter God’s rest, just as God ceased from his labor of creation when it was complete. This matches the vivid picture in Revelation 14:13, where a voice from heaven says that those who die in the Lord are blessed, “that they may rest from their labors, for their deeds follow them.” For the hearers of Hebrews—weary, persecuted, tempted to drop their hands (12:12)—this is a strong encouragement. The journey has an end. The struggle is not forever. There is a rest in which every faithful labor is complete.

Does the verse also speak to the question of resting from works as a means of salvation? Calvin drew from this verse a call to cease from our own works in the sense of self-denial, renouncing our own will and effort so that God may work in us. Many Christians have applied it to ceasing from every attempt to earn God’s favor. That application is in harmony with Scripture as a whole, which teaches that a person is justified not by works but by faith (Rom 3:28; 4:4–5; Gal 2:16), saved by grace through faith and not by works (Eph 2:8–9; Titus 3:5). It is also in harmony with Hebrews itself, which speaks of “repentance from dead works” (6:1) and of Christ’s blood cleansing the conscience “from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14), and which insists that “without faith it is impossible to please God” (11:6). In this very passage, the rest is entered by faith (4:3) and lost through unbelief (3:19). So it is right to say that no one enters God’s rest by his own works.

But we should not make 4:10 say more than it says, nor set it against the rest of Hebrews. The “works” here are compared with God’s good works, so the verse does not condemn works as such. And Hebrews elsewhere warmly commends the works that faith produces: God “will not forget your work and the love you have shown” (6:10); believers are to stir one another “to love and good works” (10:24); God equips his people “with everything good to do his will” (13:21). Faith that enters God’s rest is not idle. The rest of 4:10 is the rest of completion, not the rest of indifference. Present faith rests in God’s finished provision and so is freed to labor without anxiety; future glory will bring the end of all labor. As Augustine prayed at the beginning of his Confessions, our hearts are restless until they rest in God. Hebrews adds that this rest is God’s own Sabbath, shared with his people.
