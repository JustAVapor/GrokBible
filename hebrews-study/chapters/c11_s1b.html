Faith before the Flood: Abel, Enoch, Noah (11:4–7)

11:4

By faith Abel offered to God a better sacrifice than Cain, through which he received testimony that he was righteous, God bearing witness over his gifts; and through it, though he died, he still speaks.

The catalogue of the elders begins at the beginning of human history outside Eden, with the first act of worship recorded in Scripture and the first death. The account in Genesis 4:3–5 is brief and leaves much unexplained. Hebrews reads it with one question in mind: what made the difference between the two brothers? Its answer is the chapter’s refrain: “by faith.”

Old Testament Text: Genesis 4:3–5

Hebrew (4:3–4a): וַיְהִי מִקֵּץ יָמִים וַיָּבֵא קַיִן מִפְּרִי הָאֲדָמָה מִנְחָה לַיהוָה׃ וְהֶבֶל הֵבִיא גַם־הוּא מִבְּכֹרוֹת צֹאנוֹ וּמֵחֶלְבֵהֶן — vayhi miqqets yamim vayyave qayin mipperi ha’adamah minchah laYHWH, vehevel hevi gam-hu mibbekhorot tso’no umechelvehen, “In the course of time Cain brought some of the fruit of the ground as an offering to the Lord, and Abel—he too brought some of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat portions.”

Hebrew (4:4b–5a): וַיִּשַׁע יְהוָה אֶל־הֶבֶל וְאֶל־מִנְחָתוֹ׃ וְאֶל־קַיִן וְאֶל־מִנְחָתוֹ לֹא שָׁעָה — vayyishaʿ YHWH el-hevel ve’el-minchato, ve’el-qayin ve’el-minchato lo shaʿah, “And the Lord looked with favor on Abel and on his offering, but on Cain and on his offering he did not look with favor.”

Three details in the Hebrew are worth noticing. First, both gifts are called מִנְחָה minchah, “gift, tribute, offering,” a word that in the later Law often names the grain offering (Lev 2). So Genesis does not present Cain’s offering as wrong merely because it came from the field. Second, Abel’s gift is described with care: “the firstborn” of the flock and “their fat portions” (חֵלֶב chelev), the choicest parts, which the Law would later reserve for God alone (“all fat is the Lord’s,” Lev 3:16). Cain’s is simply “some of the fruit of the ground,” not its firstfruits. Third, God regarded “Abel and his offering,” the person first and then the gift. The issue was not only what was offered but who offered it and how. God’s word to Cain confirms this: “If you do well, will there not be a lifting up?” (Gen 4:7).

Hebrews calls Abel’s sacrifice πλείονα pleiona, literally “more” or “greater,” the comparative of the word for “much.” It can describe quantity (a more abundant offering) or quality (a fuller, more excellent one); here quality is surely meant: a “better” sacrifice. The comparison is made with παρά para, “beyond, in comparison with,” the same construction Hebrews uses for its great comparisons of Christ with angels and with Moses (1:4; 3:3). The verb “offered” (προσήνεγκεν prosēnenken, from προσφέρω prospherō) is the regular word in Hebrews for priestly offering (5:1; 8:3; 9:14; 10:12).

Interpretive Options: Why Was Abel’s Sacrifice Better?

  • Because it was an animal sacrifice involving blood. Many interpreters have reasoned that Abel, by offering a slain animal, acknowledged his need of atonement, anticipating the principle that “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22). This idea fits the larger story of Scripture, but Genesis does not say that God had commanded blood offerings at this point, it calls both gifts by the same word, and Hebrews itself speaks of Abel’s “gifts” rather than of a sin offering. It cannot be the main point of the text.
  • Because of its quality. Abel brought the firstborn and the fat, the best he had; Cain brought a portion of his produce. Ancient readers already noticed the contrast; Philo of Alexandria drew attention to the fact that Abel’s offering is described as firstborn and choice, while Cain’s is not called first fruits. This is a genuine feature of the Hebrew text.
  • Because of the faith of the offerer. This is Hebrews’ explicit answer: “by faith Abel offered.” God regarded Abel first, then his gift. Cain’s life, by contrast, revealed an evil heart: John says that Cain “was of the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own works were evil and his brother’s righteous” (1 John 3:12). The Scriptures consistently teach that God weighs the worshiper before the worship: “the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the Lord” (Prov 15:8; compare Isa 1:11–17).

The second and third answers belong together. Abel’s faith showed itself in the quality of his gift: he trusted and honored God, and so he gave God the first and the best. Calvin argued along these lines that Abel’s offering was accepted because Abel himself had first been accepted through faith. The sacrifice was “better” because it was the expression of a heart that trusted God; that is what made it, in God’s eyes, “more.”

Through this faith (or through this sacrifice; the relative pronoun ἧς hēs, “which,” is feminine and could refer to either feminine noun, “faith” or “sacrifice,” but since the sacrifice was the expression of the faith the meaning is the same) Abel “received testimony that he was righteous” (ἐμαρτυρήθη εἶναι δίκαιος emartyrēthē einai dikaios). Here is the key verb of verse 2 again. Genesis does not use the word “righteous” of Abel; Hebrews draws it from God’s favorable regard. Jesus too spoke of “the blood of righteous Abel” (Matt 23:35), and Abel’s righteousness was a fixed point in Jewish and Christian memory. The phrase that follows explains how the testimony was given: μαρτυροῦντος ἐπὶ τοῖς δώροις αὐτοῦ τοῦ θεοῦ martyrountos epi tois dōrois autou tou theou, “God bearing witness over his gifts.” This is a genitive absolute, a Greek construction that adds an accompanying circumstance: as the sacrifice was offered, God himself gave his verdict on it. How God showed his favor Genesis does not say. Some ancient interpreters imagined fire falling from heaven to consume Abel’s offering, as later on Elijah’s (1 Kgs 18:38), but Scripture is silent, and Hebrews does not speculate. What matters is that God’s approval was given and recorded.

Then comes the striking final clause: “and through it, though he died, he still speaks” (καὶ δι’ αὐτῆς ἀποθανὼν ἔτι λαλεῖ kai di’ autēs apothanōn eti lalei). Abel is the first human being in the Bible to die, and he died because of his righteousness. His name in Hebrew, הֶבֶל hevel, means “breath” or “vapor,” the word Ecclesiastes uses for life’s fleeting emptiness. His life was as brief as his name. Yet “he still speaks.” The participle apothanōn, “having died,” is concessive (“although he died”), and the verb lalei, “speaks,” is present tense: his voice continues.

In what sense? Genesis records that after the murder God said to Cain, “The voice of your brother’s blood is crying out to me from the ground” (Gen 4:10). Abel’s blood spoke, calling for justice. Hebrews will return to that thought in 12:24, where believers have come “to the blood of sprinkling that speaks a better word than Abel.” Jewish tradition developed the image; in 1 Enoch 22 the spirit of Abel is pictured as still bringing complaint against Cain. But in 11:4 the emphasis is different: Abel speaks “through it,” that is, through his faith. Recorded in Scripture and attested by God, his faith still addresses every later generation. He is the first of the “cloud of witnesses” (12:1), and his message is that a life of faith may end in apparent defeat and still be crowned by God’s verdict.

For the hearers, who had suffered for their faith and might yet suffer more (12:4), the opening example is pointed. The very first person commended for faith in the Bible did not escape death because of it; he was killed because of it. Faith is not a guarantee of earthly safety. It is the way to please God, and God does not forget those who please him. A brief life, a violent death, no descendants, and yet the man still speaks, while the name of his murderer survives only as a warning (Jude 11).

11:5

By faith Enoch was taken up so that he should not see death, and “he was not found, because God had taken him up.” For before his being taken up he had received testimony that he had pleased God.

From Abel, who died, the preacher turns to Enoch, who did not. The contrast is deliberate. Genesis 5 is a genealogy that tolls like a funeral bell: of each patriarch from Adam onward it records the years of his life and then the words “and he died” (Gen 5:5, 8, 11, and so on). Enoch alone breaks the pattern. Instead of “and he died,” the text says that God took him. Placed side by side, Abel and Enoch show that faith pleases God whether its earthly outcome is martyrdom or rescue.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 5:24

Hebrew: וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ חֲנוֹךְ אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים וְאֵינֶנּוּ כִּי־לָקַח אֹתוֹ אֱלֹהִים — vayyithallekh chanokh et-ha’elohim ve’enennu ki-laqach oto elohim, “And Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him.”

The Hebrew verb וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ vayyithallekh is the intensive-reflexive form of the verb “to walk,” and it suggests continual, habitual walking: Enoch lived his life in God’s company. The phrase occurs twice (5:22, 24), and Genesis uses it again only of Noah (6:9). The Greek Septuagint did not translate this phrase literally. It rendered “Enoch walked with God” as “Enoch pleased God” (εὐηρέστησεν euērestēsen), and it did the same for Noah (Gen 6:9) and for God’s command to Abraham, “walk before me” (Gen 17:1). The translators understood the Hebrew idiom correctly: to walk with God is to live in a way that pleases him. Hebrews follows the Greek. The words in quotation marks, “he was not found, because God had taken him up” (οὐχ ηὑρίσκετο διότι μετέθηκεν αὐτὸν ὁ θεός ouch hēurisketo dioti metethēken auton ho theos), reproduce the Septuagint of Genesis 5:24 word for word. The Hebrew “and he was not” (וְאֵינֶנּוּ ve’enennu) becomes “he was not found,” a fair interpretation: he was no longer there to be found.

The verb “was taken up” (μετετέθη metetethē, from μετατίθημι metatithēmi) means “to transfer, to move from one place to another.” Its noun, μετάθεσις metathesis, “being taken up,” appears in the next clause. The older English word for this was “translated,” in its original sense of “carried across.” The Hebrew verb behind it, לָקַח laqach, “take,” is the same verb used when Elijah was taken up to heaven (2 Kgs 2:3, 5, 9–10). It also appears in two psalms where the psalmist expresses hope beyond death: “God will ransom my soul from the hand of Sheol, for he will take me” (כִּי יִקָּחֵנִי ki yiqqacheni, Ps 49:16 Hebrew; 49:15 English), and “afterward you will take me to glory” (וְאַחַר כָּבוֹד תִּקָּחֵנִי ve’achar kavod tiqqacheni, Ps 73:24). Enoch’s experience became, for Israel, a sign that communion with God is stronger than death.

The purpose or result of his being taken is expressed by τοῦ μὴ ἰδεῖν θάνατον tou mē idein thanaton, “so that he should not see death.” “To see death” is a Hebrew way of saying “to experience death” (Luke 2:26; John 8:51). Hebrews does not say where Enoch went or what became of him. Later Jewish writings filled the gap with elaborate journeys through the heavens; the book of 1 Enoch, which Jude quotes (Jude 14–15), is the best-known example. Wisdom of Solomon, alluding to him without naming him, says that he was taken up so that evil would not change his understanding (Wis 4:10–11), and Sirach that he “pleased the Lord and was taken up” (Sir 44:16). Hebrews stays with Scripture and draws from it the one point that matters for its argument.

That point comes in the final clause: “For before his being taken up he had received testimony that he had pleased God.” The verb is again “testify,” this time in the perfect tense (μεμαρτύρηται memartyrētai, “he has been attested”): the testimony stands written in Scripture to this day. The content of the testimony is the Septuagint’s phrase, “he pleased God” (εὐαρεστηκέναι τῷ θεῷ euarestēkenai tō theō). The order matters: in Genesis, the statement that Enoch walked with (pleased) God comes before the statement that God took him. His being taken was the consequence of a life that pleased God, not the other way around.

But where is faith in this? Genesis never mentions Enoch’s faith. The preacher supplies the missing link in the next verse, in a piece of careful reasoning from Scripture: Scripture says Enoch pleased God; without faith it is impossible to please God; therefore Enoch had faith. The argument is a model of how Hebrews reads the Old Testament. It does not invent details; it draws out what the text implies when read in the light of the whole of Scripture.

For the hearers, Enoch offered a quiet but profound encouragement. Their own lives might end, like Abel’s, in a violent death; or they might, like those who are alive at the Lord’s coming, never see death at all (1 Thess 4:17; 1 Cor 15:51–52). Either way, the life that “walks with God” is the life God takes to himself. Death was not the last word in Enoch’s story, and it is not the last word in theirs.

11:6

And without faith it is impossible to please him, for the one who draws near to God must believe that he exists and that he becomes a rewarder of those who seek him out.

This verse is the first of the preacher’s two pauses in the catalogue (the other is 11:13–16), and it is one of the great principle-statements of Scripture. It explains how Enoch could be said to have acted “by faith,” and it lays down a rule that applies to everyone who would come to God.

“Without faith it is impossible to please him.” The Greek begins with the phrase “without faith” (χωρὶς δὲ πίστεως chōris de pisteōs) for emphasis. The verb “to please” (εὐαρεστῆσαι euarestēsai) picks up the testimony about Enoch, and the word “impossible” (ἀδύνατον adynaton) is one Hebrews uses with great weight. There are four “impossibles” in the letter: it is impossible to restore again to repentance those who have fallen away (6:4–6); it is impossible for God to lie (6:18); it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins (10:4); and it is impossible to please God without faith (11:6). Each one closes a door absolutely. No amount of religious activity, moral effort, or ritual exactness can substitute for faith. The point echoes the Habakkuk quotation of 10:38: God has “no pleasure” (οὐκ εὐδοκεῖ ouk eudokei) in the one who shrinks back, while his righteous one lives by faith. Paul says the same in his own idiom: “whatever does not proceed from faith is sin” (Rom 14:23).

The reason (“for,” γάρ gar) follows: “the one who draws near to God must believe.” The word “must” (δεῖ dei) expresses necessity, what the nature of the case requires. “The one who draws near” (τὸν προσερχόμενον ton proserchomenon) is one of the key expressions of Hebrews. The verb προσέρχομαι proserchomai was used for approaching God in worship, as a priest drew near to the altar. Hebrews uses it of the believer’s access to God through Christ: “Let us then draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16); Christ “is able to save completely those who draw near to God through him” (7:25); “let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith” (10:22); “you have drawn near to Mount Zion” (12:22). The whole priestly argument of chapters 7–10 has shown that the way to God is open through Jesus’ blood. Now the preacher adds that the way is entered by faith. The access is Christ’s achievement; faith is the hand that takes it.

Faith, he says, has two convictions at its core. First, the one who draws near must believe “that he exists” (ὅτι ἔστιν hoti estin, literally “that he is”). In a world crowded with gods, this was not a philosophical abstraction but a commitment: that the God of Israel, the God who spoke to the fathers and has now spoken in his Son, really is—that he is there, living and present, not a projection of human hopes. God revealed himself to Moses with the words אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה ehyeh asher ehyeh, “I am who I am” (Exod 3:14), which the Septuagint rendered “I am the One who is.” Yet belief in God’s existence alone is not saving faith; “even the demons believe—and shudder” (Jas 2:19). So the preacher adds a second conviction.

Word Study: μισθαποδότης misthapodotēs

The one who draws near must believe that God “becomes a rewarder” of those who seek him. The noun misthapodotēs is found only here in the New Testament. It is a compound of misthos, “wages, reward,” and apodidōmi, “pay back, give what is due”: one who pays out a reward. Its cousin μισθαποδοσία misthapodosia, “recompense,” appears three times in Hebrews: of the just penalty that fell on disobedience (2:2), of the “great reward” that boldness has (10:35), and of the reward to which Moses looked (11:26). The verb γίνεται ginetai, “becomes, proves to be,” is present tense: God is not merely a rewarder in theory; he shows himself to be one, again and again, to those who seek him.

Faith, then, is not only the conviction that God exists but the conviction that he is good, that he responds to those who come to him, that seeking him is never in vain. This is the faith that moves people to act. No one sets out on a journey, as Abraham did, toward a God who may or may not be there and may or may not care. The verb for “seek out” (ἐκζητοῦσιν ekzētousin) is intensive: the prefix ek strengthens “seek” into “seek diligently, search out.” The Greek Old Testament often uses this verb for seeking the Lord, and the promise attached to such seeking is constant. Moses told Israel: וּבִקַּשְׁתֶּם מִשָּׁם אֶת־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ וּמָצָאתָ כִּי תִדְרְשֶׁנּוּ בְּכָל־לְבָבְךָ וּבְכָל־נַפְשֶׁךָ uviqqashtem misham et-YHWH elohekha umatsata ki tidreshennu bekhol-levavkha uvekhol-nafshekha, “From there you will seek the Lord your God, and you will find him, if you search for him with all your heart and with all your soul” (Deut 4:29; compare Jer 29:13). There is a sobering echo later in Hebrews: Esau “sought” the blessing “with tears” (12:17, the same verb), but too late. The time to seek God is while it is called “today” (3:13).

Is it unworthy to come to God for a reward? Some have thought so, imagining that the purest faith expects nothing. Scripture does not share that view. The reward God gives is not wages earned (Rom 4:4–5) but the generosity of a Father, and its heart is God himself. When God came to Abraham after his battle, he said, אַל־תִּירָא אַבְרָם אָנֹכִי מָגֵן לָךְ שְׂכָרְךָ הַרְבֵּה מְאֹד al-tira avram, anokhi magen lakh, sekharkha harbeh me’od, “Do not fear, Abram; I am your shield; your reward shall be very great” (Gen 15:1). To trust God as rewarder is to trust his character: that he is generous, faithful, and worth seeking. Augustine’s well-known observation applies here, that when God crowns our merits he is crowning his own gifts. The reward is grace from first to last, and faith honors God precisely by expecting good from him. For the hearers, tempted to think their sacrifices had been wasted (10:32–36), the verse was a reminder that God sees, and that no one who seeks him will be the loser.

11:7

By faith Noah, having been warned by God concerning things not yet seen, in reverent fear constructed an ark for the salvation of his household; through this he condemned the world and became heir of the righteousness that is according to faith.

The third example before the flood is Noah, and he illustrates the definition of 11:1 almost exactly. He acted on God’s word about “things not yet seen” (περὶ τῶν μηδέπω βλεπομένων peri tōn mēdepō blepomenōn), the same participle used in verse 1 for “things not seen,” now sharpened with “not yet” (mēdepō). When God spoke to him, there was no flood, no cloud on the horizon, nothing in the visible world to confirm the warning. There was only the word of God. Noah built on that word, in full view of an unbelieving world.

Word Study: χρηματισθείς chrēmatistheis

The verb χρηματίζω chrēmatizō was used in Greek for an official response, such as an oracle’s answer or a ruler’s decree. In the New Testament it regularly means “to be divinely instructed or warned”: the magi were “warned in a dream” not to return to Herod (Matt 2:12); Simeon had “been told by the Holy Spirit” he would see the Messiah (Luke 2:26). Hebrews uses it of Moses, who “was instructed” by God when about to build the tabernacle (8:5), and of God, “the one who warned them on earth” at Sinai (12:25). Here the aorist passive participle means “having been divinely warned.” Noah’s ark, like Moses’ tabernacle, was built according to a revelation from God (Gen 6:14–16).

The participle εὐλαβηθείς eulabētheis, “in reverent fear,” describes Noah’s response to the warning. The verb can mean simply “be cautious” or “be afraid,” but in Hebrews its noun is used of reverent, godly awe: Jesus “was heard because of his reverence” (5:7), and believers are to offer God acceptable worship “with reverence and awe” (12:28). Noah’s fear was not panic but the right response of a creature to the word of the Creator: he took God seriously. Faith and holy fear are not opposites. The one who truly believes God’s warnings will tremble at them and act.

His action was to construct an ark (κατεσκεύασεν κιβωτόν kateskeuasen kibōton). The verb κατασκευάζω kataskeuazō, “build, construct, furnish,” is the word Hebrews used in 3:3–4 of the builder of a house (“every house is built by someone, but the builder of all things is God”) and in 9:2, 6 of the preparing of the tabernacle. The Greek word for “ark,” κιβωτός kibōtos, “chest, box,” is the same word Hebrews uses for the ark of the covenant (9:4). In Hebrew, however, the two are different: the ark of the covenant is אֲרוֹן aron, while Noah’s ark is תֵּבָה tevah (Gen 6:14), a word the Old Testament uses elsewhere only for the little basket of papyrus in which the infant Moses was placed on the Nile (תֵּבַת גֹּמֶא tevat gome, Exod 2:3). In both stories God preserves the one through whom he will deliver his people, inside a sealed vessel floating on the waters of judgment.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 6:9, 22; 7:1

Hebrew (6:9): נֹחַ אִישׁ צַדִּיק תָּמִים הָיָה בְּדֹרֹתָיו אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים הִתְהַלֶּךְ־נֹחַ — noach ish tsaddiq tamim hayah bedorotav; et-ha’elohim hithallekh-noach, “Noah was a righteous man, blameless in his generations; Noah walked with God.”

Hebrew (6:22): וַיַּעַשׂ נֹחַ כְּכֹל אֲשֶׁר צִוָּה אֹתוֹ אֱלֹהִים כֵּן עָשָׂה — vayyaʿas noach kekhol asher tsivvah oto elohim, ken ʿasah, “And Noah did according to all that God commanded him; so he did.”

Hebrew (7:1): בֹּא־אַתָּה וְכָל־בֵּיתְךָ אֶל־הַתֵּבָה כִּי־אֹתְךָ רָאִיתִי צַדִּיק לְפָנַי בַּדּוֹר הַזֶּה — bo-attah vekhol-beitekha el-hatevah, ki-otekha ra’iti tsaddiq lefanai baddor hazzeh, “Go into the ark, you and all your household, for I have seen that you are righteous before me in this generation.”

Noah is the first person in the Bible called צַדִּיק tsaddiq, “righteous.” Yet the text says first that “Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord” (Gen 6:8, חֵן chen, “grace, favor”). God’s grace comes before Noah’s righteousness, and his righteousness shows itself in exact obedience (6:22). Like Enoch, he “walked with God.” Ezekiel later names Noah with Daniel and Job as models of righteousness (Ezek 14:14, 20).

The ark was built “for the salvation of his household” (εἰς σωτηρίαν τοῦ οἴκου αὐτοῦ eis sōtērian tou oikou autou). Both nouns are significant in Hebrews. “Salvation” is one of the letter’s great themes: Christ is the “source of eternal salvation” (5:9), and he will appear a second time “for salvation to those who eagerly await him” (9:28). “Household” recalls 3:6: “we are his house, if we hold fast our confidence.” Noah’s faith did not benefit Noah alone; his family was preserved because of it (Gen 7:1; 1 Pet 3:20). Faithful perseverance is never merely private.

“Through this he condemned the world.” Again the relative pronoun (δι’ ἧς di’ hēs) is feminine and could refer to faith or to the ark (kibōtos is also feminine). Either way, the sense is the same: by believing and obeying, Noah exposed the unbelief of his generation and showed that it was without excuse. He did not pronounce sentence on them as a judge; his life itself was the verdict. Jesus spoke of the men of Nineveh rising at the judgment to “condemn” the generation that refused his preaching (Matt 12:41), and Peter calls Noah “a herald of righteousness” to the ungodly world (2 Pet 2:5). The “world” (κόσμος kosmos) here is humanity organized in rebellion against God, the same world that, according to 11:38, was not worthy of the faithful.

Finally, Noah “became heir of the righteousness that is according to faith” (τῆς κατὰ πίστιν δικαιοσύνης ἐγένετο κληρονόμος tēs kata pistin dikaiosynēs egeneto klēronomos). The word “heir” (κληρονόμος klēronomos) belongs to the vocabulary of inheritance that runs through Hebrews: the Son is “heir of all things” (1:2), believers are “heirs of the promise” (6:17), and Abraham goes out to a place he will receive “as an inheritance” (11:8). An heir does not earn his inheritance; he receives it by right of relationship. Noah received righteousness in that way: not as a wage for building the ark, but as the inheritance that belongs to faith. The phrase “according to faith” recalls the Habakkuk text just quoted, “my righteous one shall live by faith” (10:38; Hab 2:4, where the Hebrew reads וְצַדִּיק בֶּאֱמוּנָתוֹ יִחְיֶה vetsaddiq be’emunato yichyeh, “but the righteous by his faithfulness shall live”). Noah is the first “righteous one” in Scripture, and he lived by faith.

Hebrews’ emphasis falls on righteousness as the approved standing and life of the one who trusts God, while Paul, who also speaks of “the righteousness of faith” (Rom 4:13; compare Phil 3:9), emphasizes righteousness as God’s gift of right standing to the ungodly. The two are not in conflict. Both teach that righteousness is received by faith and not achieved by human merit, and both teach that genuine faith shows itself in obedience. Noah was counted righteous because he trusted God’s word, and he built the ark because he trusted it.

The application to the hearers would not have been missed. Jesus himself compared the days before his coming to “the days of Noah” (Matt 24:37–39), when people went on eating and drinking until the flood swept them away. The hearers too had been “warned” about things not yet seen: “a fearful expectation of judgment” (10:27), the shaking of earth and heaven (12:26–27), the Lord’s return “in a very little while” (10:37). The world around them saw no sign of it. Faith, like Noah’s, would take the warning seriously, prepare accordingly, and keep building while others carried on as though nothing would ever change.
