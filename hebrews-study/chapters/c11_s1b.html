Faith before the Flood: Abel, Enoch, Noah (11:4–7)

11:4

By faith Abel offered to God a better sacrifice than Cain, through which he received testimony that he was righteous, God bearing witness over his gifts; and through it, though he died, he still speaks.

The catalogue of the elders begins at the beginning of human history outside Eden, with the first act of worship recorded in Scripture and the first death. The account in Genesis 4:3–5 is brief and leaves much unexplained. Hebrews reads it with one question in mind: what made the difference between the two brothers? Its answer is the chapter’s refrain: “by faith.”

Old Testament Text: Genesis 4:3–5

Hebrew (4:3–4a): וַיְהִי מִקֵּץ יָמִים וַיָּבֵא קַיִן מִפְּרִי הָאֲדָמָה מִנְחָה לַיהוָה׃ וְהֶבֶל הֵבִיא גַם־הוּא מִבְּכֹרוֹת צֹאנוֹ וּמֵחֶלְבֵהֶן — vayhi miqqets yamim vayyave qayin mipperi ha’adamah minchah laYHWH, vehevel hevi gam-hu mibbekhorot tso’no umechelvehen, “In the course of time Cain brought some of the fruit of the ground as an offering to the Lord, and Abel—he too brought some of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat portions.”

Hebrew (4:4b–5a): וַיִּשַׁע יְהוָה אֶל־הֶבֶל וְאֶל־מִנְחָתוֹ׃ וְאֶל־קַיִן וְאֶל־מִנְחָתוֹ לֹא שָׁעָה — vayyishaʿ YHWH el-hevel ve’el-minchato, ve’el-qayin ve’el-minchato lo shaʿah, “And the Lord looked with favor on Abel and on his offering, but on Cain and on his offering he did not look with favor.”

Three details in the Hebrew are worth noticing. First, both gifts are called מִנְחָה minchah, “gift, tribute, offering,” a word that in the later Law often names the grain offering (Lev 2). So Genesis does not present Cain’s offering as wrong merely because it came from the field. Second, Abel’s gift is described with care: “the firstborn” of the flock and “their fat portions” (חֵלֶב chelev), the choicest parts, which the Law would later reserve for God alone (“all fat is the Lord’s,” Lev 3:16). Cain’s is simply “some of the fruit of the ground,” not its firstfruits. Third, God regarded “Abel and his offering,” the person first and then the gift. The issue was not only what was offered but who offered it and how. God’s word to Cain confirms this: “If you do well, will there not be a lifting up?” (Gen 4:7).

Hebrews calls Abel’s sacrifice πλείονα pleiona, literally “more” or “greater,” the comparative of the word for “much.” It can describe quantity (a more abundant offering) or quality (a fuller, more excellent one); here quality is surely meant: a “better” sacrifice. The comparison is made with παρά para, “beyond, in comparison with,” the same construction Hebrews uses for its great comparisons of Christ with angels and with Moses (1:4; 3:3). The verb “offered” (προσήνεγκεν prosēnenken, from προσφέρω prospherō) is the regular word in Hebrews for priestly offering (5:1; 8:3; 9:14; 10:12).

Interpretive Options: Why Was Abel’s Sacrifice Better?

  • Because it was an animal sacrifice involving blood. Many interpreters have reasoned that Abel, by offering a slain animal, acknowledged his need of atonement, anticipating the principle that “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22). This idea fits the larger story of Scripture, but Genesis does not say that God had commanded blood offerings at this point, it calls both gifts by the same word, and Hebrews itself speaks of Abel’s “gifts” rather than of a sin offering. It cannot be the main point of the text.
  • Because of its quality. Abel brought the firstborn and the fat, the best he had; Cain brought a portion of his produce. Ancient readers already noticed the contrast; Philo of Alexandria drew attention to the fact that Abel’s offering is described as firstborn and choice, while Cain’s is not called first fruits. This is a genuine feature of the Hebrew text.
  • Because of the faith of the offerer. This is Hebrews’ explicit answer: “by faith Abel offered.” God regarded Abel first, then his gift. Cain’s life, by contrast, revealed an evil heart: John says that Cain “was of the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own works were evil and his brother’s righteous” (1 John 3:12). The Scriptures consistently teach that God weighs the worshiper before the worship: “the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the Lord” (Prov 15:8; compare Isa 1:11–17).

The second and third answers belong together. Abel’s faith showed itself in the quality of his gift: he trusted and honored God, and so he gave God the first and the best. Calvin argued along these lines that Abel’s offering was accepted because Abel himself had first been accepted through faith. The sacrifice was “better” because it was the expression of a heart that trusted God; that is what made it, in God’s eyes, “more.”

Through this faith (or through this sacrifice; the relative pronoun ἧς hēs, “which,” is feminine and could refer to either feminine noun, “faith” or “sacrifice,” but since the sacrifice was the expression of the faith the meaning is the same) Abel “received testimony that he was righteous” (ἐμαρτυρήθη εἶναι δίκαιος emartyrēthē einai dikaios). Here is the key verb of verse 2 again. Genesis does not use the word “righteous” of Abel; Hebrews draws it from God’s favorable regard. Jesus too spoke of “the blood of righteous Abel” (Matt 23:35), and Abel’s righteousness was a fixed point in Jewish and Christian memory. The phrase that follows explains how the testimony was given: μαρτυροῦντος ἐπὶ τοῖς δώροις αὐτοῦ τοῦ θεοῦ martyrountos epi tois dōrois autou tou theou, “God bearing witness over his gifts.” This is a genitive absolute, a Greek construction that adds an accompanying circumstance: as the sacrifice was offered, God himself gave his verdict on it. How God showed his favor Genesis does not say. Some ancient interpreters imagined fire falling from heaven to consume Abel’s offering, as later on Elijah’s (1 Kgs 18:38), but Scripture is silent, and Hebrews does not speculate. What matters is that God’s approval was given and recorded.

Then comes the striking final clause: “and through it, though he died, he still speaks” (καὶ δι’ αὐτῆς ἀποθανὼν ἔτι λαλεῖ kai di’ autēs apothanōn eti lalei). Abel is the first human being in the Bible to die, and he died because of his righteousness. His name in Hebrew, הֶבֶל hevel, means “breath” or “vapor,” the word Ecclesiastes uses for life’s fleeting emptiness. His life was as brief as his name. Yet “he still speaks.” The participle apothanōn, “having died,” is concessive (“although he died”), and the verb lalei, “speaks,” is present tense: his voice continues.

In what sense? Genesis records that after the murder God said to Cain, “The voice of your brother’s blood is crying out to me from the ground” (Gen 4:10). Abel’s blood spoke, calling for justice. Hebrews will return to that thought in 12:24, where believers have come “to the blood of sprinkling that speaks a better word than Abel.” Jewish tradition developed the image; in 1 Enoch 22 the spirit of Abel is pictured as still bringing complaint against Cain. But in 11:4 the emphasis is different: Abel speaks “through it,” that is, through his faith. Recorded in Scripture and attested by God, his faith still addresses every later generation. He is the first of the “cloud of witnesses” (12:1), and his message is that a life of faith may end in apparent defeat and still be crowned by God’s verdict.

For the hearers, who had suffered for their faith and might yet suffer more (12:4), the opening example is pointed. The very first person commended for faith in the Bible did not escape death because of it; he was killed because of it. Faith is not a guarantee of earthly safety. It is the way to please God, and God does not forget those who please him. A brief life, a violent death, no descendants, and yet the man still speaks, while the name of his murderer survives only as a warning (Jude 11).
