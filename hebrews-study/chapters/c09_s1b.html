9:6

These things having been set up in this way, the priests enter the first tent continually as they carry out their acts of worship,

Having described the sanctuary, the preacher turns to the worship carried on in it. The opening phrase, Τούτων δὲ οὕτως κατεσκευασμένων toutōn de houtōs kateskeuasmenōn, “these things having been set up in this way,” uses the perfect tense of the verb already used in 9:2. The perfect describes a completed action with continuing results: the tabernacle was built, and it stands so arranged. That arrangement—two rooms, one behind the other—determines how worship works, and how worship works will reveal something about access to God (9:8).

Verses 6–7 are built as a balanced pair, marked in Greek by μέν … δέ men … de (“on the one hand … on the other”):

  • into the first tent — continually — the priests (plural) — performing the services
  • into the second — once a year — only the high priest (singular) — not without blood

Every element of the second line is more restricted than the first. The contrast is the point.

“Continually” translates διὰ παντός dia pantos, literally “through all [time].” The ministry in the outer room was constant. Every morning and evening a priest entered to trim and light the lamps and to burn incense on the golden altar (Exod 30:7–8); every Sabbath the bread of the Presence was replaced (Lev 24:8). The Hebrew word for this regularity is תָּמִיד tamid, “continually,” used of the bread (Exod 25:30), the lamps (Lev 24:2), and the incense (Exod 30:8). In the Second Temple period the daily service was so central that a whole tractate of the Mishnah, Tamid, describes it, and Luke’s Gospel opens with the priest Zechariah chosen by lot to enter the sanctuary of the Lord and burn incense while the people prayed outside (Luke 1:8–10).

“Carry out their acts of worship” renders τὰς λατρείας ἐπιτελοῦντες tas latreias epitelountes. ἐπιτελέω epiteleō means “to accomplish, perform, complete.” It is used for carrying out religious rites, and it echoes 8:5, where Moses was about to “complete” the tabernacle. The present tenses (“enter,” “carrying out”) are vivid. The preacher may simply be describing the tabernacle’s regulations in a timeless present, as one does when explaining a set of rules, but the present tense also fits a time when these rites were still being performed in Jerusalem, before AD 70.

The constant coming and going of priests in the outer room is in one sense a beautiful picture: God was continually served, the light never went out, the bread was always before him, the incense rose morning and evening. But it is also a picture of limitation. The priests went in constantly, yet never went further. Their ministry was busy, but it always stopped at a curtain. That is the contrast the next verse will draw.

9:7

but into the second only the high priest enters, once a year, and not without blood, which he offers for himself and for the sins the people committed in ignorance.

Here the preacher summarizes Leviticus 16, the law of the Day of Atonement (Hebrew יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים yom hakkippurim; Lev 23:27), which fell on the tenth day of the seventh month. Every phrase is chosen with care.

“Into the second”—the inner room, the Holy of Holies. The Lord’s warning to Aaron after the death of his sons Nadab and Abihu set the tone:

Old Testament Text: Leviticus 16:2

Hebrew: דַּבֵּר אֶל־אַהֲרֹן אָחִיךָ וְאַל־יָבֹא בְכָל־עֵת אֶל־הַקֹּדֶשׁ מִבֵּית לַפָּרֹכֶת אֶל־פְּנֵי הַכַּפֹּרֶת אֲשֶׁר עַל־הָאָרֹן וְלֹא יָמוּת כִּי בֶּעָנָן אֵרָאֶה עַל־הַכַּפֹּרֶת — dabber el-aharon achikha ve’al-yavo vekhol-et el-haqqodesh mibbet lapparokhet el-pene hakkapporet asher al-ha’aron velo yamut ki be’anan era’eh al-hakkapporet, “Speak to Aaron your brother, that he not come at any time into the holy place inside the curtain, before the mercy seat that is on the ark, so that he not die; for in the cloud I will appear over the mercy seat.”

The phrase “so that he not die” recurs through the chapter (16:2, 13). Entry into God’s immediate presence was a matter of life and death.

“Only the high priest” (μόνος ὁ ἀρχιερεύς monos ho archiereus). Not the ordinary priests, not the Levites, not the people. Lev 16:17 adds that no one at all was to be in the tent of meeting from the time he went in to make atonement until he came out. He was utterly alone before God, representing the whole nation.

“Once a year” (ἅπαξ τοῦ ἐνιαυτοῦ hapax tou eniautou). Lev 16:34 says, לְכַפֵּר עַל־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מִכָּל־חַטֹּאתָם אַחַת בַּשָּׁנָה lekhapper al-bene yisra’el mikkol-chattotam achat bashshanah, “to make atonement for the sons of Israel for all their sins, once in the year.” In fact, on that one day the high priest went behind the curtain more than once: first with the incense, then with the blood of the bull for himself, then with the blood of the goat for the people (Lev 16:12–15). The Mishnah describes these entries and a fourth to retrieve the incense vessels (m. Yoma 5:1–4; 7:4). “Once a year” therefore means “on one day in the year.” The important word is ἅπαξ hapax, “once.” It will return as a key term: Christ entered “once for all” (9:12), appeared “once” (9:26), was offered “once” (9:28). The high priest’s “once” was repeated every year; Christ’s “once” is never repeated.

“Not without blood” (οὐ χωρὶς αἵματος ou chōris haimatos). The understatement (“not without”) is emphatic: it was impossible to enter any other way. The high priest sprinkled the blood of the bull and then of the goat “on the mercy seat and in front of the mercy seat” seven times (Lev 16:14–15). This phrase anticipates the principle of 9:22, “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.”

“Which he offers for himself and for the sins of the people”. The high priest first offered a bull “for himself and for his house” (Lev 16:6, 11), and only then the goat “for the people” (16:15). Hebrews has already made this point (5:3; 7:27): the Levitical high priest was himself a sinner who needed atonement before he could represent others. Jesus, by contrast, is “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” and had no need to offer for himself (7:26–27). The Mishnah preserves the high priest’s confession over his bull: he laid his hands on it and confessed his sins and those of his household, and when he pronounced the divine Name the people bowed and responded, “Blessed be the name of the glory of his kingdom forever and ever” (m. Yoma 3:8; 6:2). It also describes the elaborate preparation that preceded the day: the high priest was secluded for seven days beforehand, and a substitute was held ready in case he became ritually unfit (m. Yoma 1:1). The whole nation understood that much depended on one man entering and coming out alive.

Word Study: ἀγνόημα agnoēma — “sin of ignorance”

The word ἀγνοημάτων agnoēmatōn (from ἀγνόημα agnoēma, related to ἀγνοέω agnoeō, “to not know”) means a sin committed in ignorance or through error. It corresponds to the Hebrew שְׁגָגָה shegagah, “inadvertence, unintentional error,” the category of sin for which the Levitical sacrifices were provided: נֶפֶשׁ כִּי־תֶחֱטָא בִשְׁגָגָה nefesh ki-techeta vishgagah, “when a person sins unintentionally” (Lev 4:2; compare Num 15:27–28).

The law distinguished such sins from sins committed בְּיָד רָמָה beyad ramah, “with a high hand,” that is, in deliberate, defiant rebellion: “the person who acts with a high hand … blasphemes the Lord, and that person shall be cut off from among his people” (Num 15:30). For defiant sin the sacrificial system made no provision. The Day of Atonement did cover “all their sins” (Lev 16:16, 21, 30, 34), including “transgressions” (פְּשָׁעִים peshaim, 16:16, 21), so the preacher’s word is probably a broad description of the people’s sins viewed as committed in weakness and blindness rather than in settled rebellion. Hebrews has already described the high priest as one able to “deal gently with the ignorant and wayward” (τοῖς ἀγνοοῦσιν καὶ πλανωμένοις tois agnoousin kai planōmenois, 5:2). The same distinction lies behind the solemn warnings of 10:26 (“if we go on sinning deliberately … there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins”).

The preacher’s compact sentence reminds his hearers of the most solemn day in Israel’s year, a day on which the people fasted and “afflicted their souls” (Lev 16:29–31) while the high priest went in alone. Even at its height, the old covenant’s access to God was one man, one day, behind a curtain, under threat of death, with blood that had to be brought again next year. Everything in the coming paragraphs will reverse these restrictions.

9:8

By this the Holy Spirit is making clear that the way into the holy places has not yet been disclosed while the first tent still has standing.

This verse is the interpretive key to the paragraph. The preacher does not merely describe the tabernacle; he tells us what God meant by it.

“The Holy Spirit is making clear” (τοῦτο δηλοῦντος τοῦ πνεύματος τοῦ ἁγίου touto dēlountos tou pneumatos tou hagiou). δηλόω dēloō means “to make plain, reveal, indicate.” The present participle (“is making clear”) means the Spirit’s message is not a past event but an ongoing testimony, still speaking whenever Scripture is read. This matches Hebrews’ consistent view of Scripture. The Holy Spirit “says” the words of Psalm 95 (3:7) and “testifies” through Jeremiah 31 (10:15). Here the Spirit speaks not through a quoted sentence but through the God-designed arrangement of the tabernacle as Scripture records it. The layout itself was revelation. Moses built according to the pattern shown him on the mountain (8:5; Exod 25:40), so the pattern carried a divine meaning beyond what Moses himself may have understood (compare 1 Pet 1:10–12).

“The way into the holy places has not yet been disclosed” (μήπω πεφανερῶσθαι τὴν τῶν ἁγίων ὁδόν mēpō pephanerōsthai tēn tōn hagiōn hodon). Here τῶν ἁγίων tōn hagiōn, “of the holy places,” means the true sanctuary, God’s own presence, which the inner room symbolized (as in 8:2; 9:12, 24; 10:19). The perfect infinitive pephanerōsthai, “to have been made manifest,” is preceded by μήπω mēpō, “not yet.” The Spirit was saying, “not yet”—which implies, “but one day.” The tabernacle confessed its own provisional character. Its design pointed forward to a time when the way would be opened. That time has come: “we have boldness to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain” (10:19–20). Jesus himself is “the way” (John 14:6).

“While the first tent still has standing” (ἔτι τῆς πρώτης σκηνῆς ἐχούσης στάσιν eti tēs prōtēs skēnēs echousēs stasin). The noun στάσις stasis means “a standing, a place, a position”; “to have standing” means to exist, to have a recognized status or validity. The phrase is deliberately layered.

Interpretive Options: “The First Tent”

  • The outer room. In 9:2 and 9:6 “the first tent” clearly means the outer compartment. On this reading, the outer room itself, standing between the people and the inner sanctuary, is a barrier; as long as it stands, the way into the inner room is blocked.
  • The whole first-covenant sanctuary. “First” is also the word Hebrews uses for the first covenant (8:7, 13; 9:1, 15, 18). On this reading, “the first tent” means the earthly tabernacle as a whole, together with the system it represents; as long as it retains its standing as the God-appointed place of worship, the true way into God’s presence is not yet open.

The two are not really in competition. The preacher is moving from the spatial sense to the temporal and covenantal sense, which is exactly why he spoke of two “tents” instead of two rooms of one tent. The outer room, with its constant but limited ministry, becomes a symbol of the entire first-covenant age. Just as the outer room stood in front of the inner sanctuary, so the whole Mosaic order stood “in front of” the true access to God, which could come only when that order had served its purpose. Many commentators see this double reference. The next verse, which calls the first tent a “parable for the present time,” confirms the symbolic, temporal sense.

It is worth asking whether “has standing” implies that the earthly sanctuary still stood when Hebrews was written. The preacher is speaking of its God-given status rather than of buildings: once Christ entered the true sanctuary, the earthly one lost its “standing” in God’s purposes, even if its rituals continued for a while in Jerusalem. That is what 8:13 meant by “near to vanishing.” But the verse fits most naturally a setting in which those rituals were still visibly going on and still exerting a pull on the hearers.

For Christians this verse is a reminder of the privilege we take for granted. For more than a thousand years, the most faithful Israelite could never enter the place of God’s presence; even the high priest could do so only once a year, trembling. The arrangement was itself God’s message: “Not yet.” The gospel is God’s announcement: “Now.”

9:9

This is a parable for the present time, according to which gifts and sacrifices are offered that cannot make the worshiper perfect with respect to conscience,

“This is a parable” (ἥτις παραβολή hētis parabolē). The relative pronoun hētis (“which”) is feminine and refers back to “the first tent” (feminine) in 9:8, though it can include the whole arrangement just described. A παραβολή parabolē (literally “something placed beside” for comparison) is an illustration, a figure, a symbol that teaches by comparison. In the Gospels it is the word for Jesus’ parables; in Hebrews it appears again at 11:19, where Abraham received Isaac back from the dead “as a parable,” that is, figuratively. Calling the tabernacle a “parable” means that it was a God-given picture of spiritual realities, with meaning beyond itself. It was not an end but a pointer.

“For the present time” (εἰς τὸν καιρὸν τὸν ἐνεστηκότα eis ton kairon ton enestēkota). ἐνεστηκώς enestēkōs, the perfect participle of ἐνίστημι enistēmi, means “present, now here.” Which “present time” is meant?

Interpretive Options: “The Present Time”

  • The time of the old covenant. The tabernacle was a parable for the age in which it functioned, the period “until the time of setting things right” (9:10). On this view, “the present time” is the old age, contrasted with the coming “time of reformation.”
  • The time of the writer and readers. The tabernacle is a parable that speaks to “the present time,” the Christian era, which can now read its meaning in the light of Christ. On this view, “the present time” corresponds to the age in which Christ has come, and the parable was “for” it in the sense of pointing to it.

The grammar allows either. The flow of the sentence, which continues with gifts and sacrifices that “are offered” and cannot perfect the conscience, and which then speaks of regulations imposed “until the time of setting things right,” suggests that “the present time” is the age of the old order, which in the preacher’s day still overlapped with the new as the temple rituals continued. The tabernacle was a parable of and for that age, pointing to the limits of its own worship. Its lesson, however, is now read by Christians in the light of its fulfillment, so the second idea is not far away.

“According to which gifts and sacrifices are offered”. The phrase “gifts and sacrifices” (δῶρά τε καὶ θυσίαι dōra te kai thysiai) is Hebrews’ standard way of describing the whole range of offerings, bloodless and bloody (5:1; 8:3). “According to which” connects them to the “parable”: the offerings were part of the symbolic order and shared its limitations.

“That cannot make the worshiper perfect with respect to conscience” (μὴ δυνάμεναι κατὰ συνείδησιν τελειῶσαι τὸν λατρεύοντα mē dynamenai kata syneidēsin teleiōsai ton latreuonta). This is the heart of the matter.

Word Study: συνείδησις syneidēsis — “conscience”

συνείδησις syneidēsis is literally “knowing-with,” a co-knowledge, that is, one’s inner awareness of oneself, especially of one’s own guilt or innocence before God. The Old Testament has no single word for it; the Hebrew usually speaks of the “heart” (לֵב lev), as when “David’s heart struck him” after he cut Saul’s robe (1 Sam 24:5) or after he numbered the people (2 Sam 24:10). In Hebrews the conscience is the place where the burden of sin is felt, the inner barrier that keeps a person from drawing near to God with confidence. It appears five times: the old sacrifices could not perfect the worshiper as to conscience (9:9); Christ’s blood will purify our conscience (9:14); if the old sacrifices had worked, worshipers would no longer have “consciousness of sins” (10:2); believers draw near “with hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience” (10:22); and the preacher himself has “a good conscience” (13:18).

“Make perfect” (τελειόω teleioō) is one of the great words of Hebrews. It means to bring something to its intended goal or completion. Applied to worshipers, it means to bring them to the goal of worship: real, unhindered fellowship with God, with their sins truly dealt with. “The law made nothing perfect” (7:19); the Levitical priesthood could not bring perfection (7:11); the same sacrifices offered year after year “can never make perfect those who draw near” (10:1). But Christ, “by a single offering, has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14).

Why could the old offerings not reach the conscience? The answer of Hebrews is not that the Israelites lacked sincerity, nor that God did not truly forgive repentant Israelites. The Old Testament plainly says that he did: in the sin offering “the priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven” (Lev 4:31; 5:13), and David knew the blessedness of forgiveness (Ps 32:1–5; 51). The point is that the animal sacrifices themselves could not be the ground of that forgiveness: “it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (10:4). They were provisional and symbolic, effective at the level for which God appointed them (ritual cleansing and restored access to the community of worship, 9:13) and pointing to the reality that would actually remove guilt. Their constant repetition proved that they had not finished the work (10:1–3). A worshiper could go home from the Day of Atonement forgiven by God’s gracious word, but knowing that the same ritual would be needed next year, and that he still could not enter where God was. His conscience was not “perfected”—brought to settled rest in God’s presence.

The Mishnah itself shows awareness that rituals alone could not deal with the heart. It teaches that one who says, “I will sin, and the Day of Atonement will atone,” finds that the Day of Atonement does not atone, and that for offenses against a neighbor the day does not atone until he has made peace with his neighbor (m. Yoma 8:9). Thoughtful Jews knew that true atonement required something deeper than the ritual. Hebrews says what that something is: the blood of Christ.

9:10

since they deal only with foods and drinks and various washings—regulations for the flesh, imposed until the time of setting things right.

The sentence that began in 9:6 reaches its verdict. The old gifts and sacrifices were “only” (μόνον monon) concerned with external matters. The grammar is compressed: literally, “only upon foods and drinks and various washings.” The preposition ἐπί epi with the dative here means “in connection with, on the basis of”; the offerings belonged to a system of external regulations.

  • “Foods” (βρώμασιν brōmasin): the laws of clean and unclean food (Lev 11; Deut 14), and the rules about who could eat which portions of the sacrifices (Lev 6–7). Hebrews later warns against being carried away by teachings about “foods, which have not benefited those devoted to them” (13:9).
  • “Drinks” (πόμασιν pomasin): regulations such as the priests’ abstention from wine while on duty (Lev 10:9), the Nazirite vow (Num 6:3), drink offerings (Num 28:7), and the purity of liquids (Lev 11:34).
  • “Various washings” (διαφόροις βαπτισμοῖς diaphorois baptismois): the many ritual washings of the law. Priests washed hands and feet at the bronze basin before entering the tent or approaching the altar, “so that they not die” (Exod 30:18–21); the high priest bathed his whole body before and after the Day of Atonement rites (Lev 16:4, 24); people and objects that had become unclean were washed (Lev 15; Num 19:19). The Mishnah says that on the Day of Atonement the high priest immersed himself five times and washed his hands and feet ten times (m. Yoma 3:3). The word βαπτισμός baptismos here means ritual washings, not Christian baptism (compare Mark 7:4, where the same word is used of washing cups and pots; and Heb 6:2, “teaching about washings”).

“Regulations for the flesh” (δικαιώματα σαρκός dikaiōmata sarkos). The word dikaiōmata repeats the “regulations for worship” of 9:1, closing the paragraph like a bracket. “Flesh” (σάρξ sarx) here does not mean sinful human nature, as it often does in Paul. In Hebrews it means the outward, bodily, earthly sphere, in contrast to the conscience (9:13–14: the old rites sanctify “for the purity of the flesh,” but Christ’s blood purifies “the conscience”). The Levitical priesthood itself rested on “a law of fleshly commandment” (7:16), that is, on bodily descent from Levi. The old regulations dealt with the body: what it ate, what it drank, how it was washed, whether it had touched something unclean. They were good, God-given regulations, but they could not reach the inner person.

“Imposed until the time of setting things right” (μέχρι καιροῦ διορθώσεως ἐπικείμενα mechri kairou diorthōseōs epikeimena). ἐπίκειμαι epikeimai, “to lie upon, be imposed,” suggests something laid upon people as an obligation (compare Acts 15:10, 28, where the apostles refuse to lay a yoke or burden on Gentile believers).

Word Study: διόρθωσις diorthōsis — “setting things right, reformation”

διόρθωσις diorthōsis occurs only here in the New Testament. It comes from ὀρθός orthos, “straight, right,” with the prefix dia, “through.” It means “making straight, correction, restoration, reform.” In ordinary Greek it could be used of setting a broken limb straight, of correcting a text, or of reforming laws and civic affairs. Here it describes the decisive new order that God would bring, the time when what had been provisional would be replaced by what is final. Its closest equivalent in Hebrews is the “new covenant” of Jer 31, under which God writes his law on the heart and remembers sins no more (8:8–12). It corresponds to “the age to come” (6:5) and to “these last days” in which God has spoken by his Son (1:2).

The word “until” (μέχρι mechri) is decisive. The regulations of the first covenant were never meant to be permanent. They were given with a built-in expiration, like scaffolding around a building under construction or a tutor appointed for a child until he comes of age (Gal 3:23–25; 4:1–5). Paul speaks similarly of food laws and festivals as “a shadow of things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ” (Col 2:16–17). When the time of setting things right arrives, the regulations of the flesh have done their work. The next verse announces that the time has arrived: “But Christ, having come….”

This verse also offers a safeguard for Christians in every age. It is always tempting to make religion a matter of externals: what we eat or abstain from, rituals of washing, outward observances. Those things are not evil, and God once commanded many of them. But the conscience is not cleansed by externals. Only the blood of Christ reaches the heart (9:14; 10:22; 13:9).

For Reflection

  1. The preacher describes the tabernacle with reverence even while showing its limits. How does this model a right way of reading the Old Testament—neither dismissing it nor treating it as final?
  2. The arrangement of the tabernacle—two rooms, a curtain, one man, one day, blood—said “Not yet” about access to God. What does it mean for your prayer life that in Christ the answer is now “Come” (4:16; 10:19–22)?
  3. The jar of manna, the budding rod, and the tablets of the law were all memorials of Israel’s failures and God’s faithfulness, and they lay beneath the mercy seat. What failures of your own has God covered in Christ, and how does that shape the way you remember them?
  4. Hebrews says the old sacrifices could not perfect the worshiper “with respect to conscience.” Are there ways you try to quiet your conscience with outward religious activity rather than resting in what Christ has done?
  5. The old regulations were “imposed until the time of setting things right.” What does it mean to live in the time of fulfillment, and how should that affect the way Christians think about religious rules and rituals?
