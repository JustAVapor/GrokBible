4:15

For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been tempted in every respect in the same way as we are, yet without sin.

Verse 14 described the greatness of our high priest; verse 15 guards against a misunderstanding that greatness might create. A priest who has passed through the heavens and is the Son of God might seem too exalted to understand ordinary, struggling people. The preacher answers that objection with a double negative: “For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses.” This figure of speech, called litotes, affirms something strongly by denying its opposite. It means: we have a high priest who is fully able to sympathize. The double negative also suggests that the preacher is answering a real fear. Perhaps some of his hearers thought that a heavenly priest must be remote, and that the earthly priests in Jerusalem, men of flesh and blood, were nearer to them. The preacher replies that no one is nearer to them in their weakness than Jesus.

Word Study: συμπαθῆσαι sympathēsai

The verb συμπαθέω sympatheō is formed from syn, “with,” and the root path-, “suffer, feel.” It means “to suffer with, to feel with, to share the experience of another.” The English word “sympathy” comes from it, but the English word has often weakened to mean a polite feeling of pity from a distance. The Greek word is stronger. It describes fellow-feeling born of shared experience. Jewish writers in Greek used the related noun for the deep bond of feeling between brothers and of a mother for her suffering sons, as in 4 Maccabees. The only other New Testament use of the verb is in Hebrews 10:34, where the hearers “sympathized with” those in prison—not merely by feeling sorry for them but by visiting them and accepting the loss of their own property. Peter urges believers to be “sympathetic” (1 Pet 3:8). Sympathy in this biblical sense acts.

The object of Jesus’ sympathy is “our weaknesses” (ταῖς ἀσθενείαις ἡμῶν tais astheneiais hēmōn). The noun ἀσθένεια astheneia means “weakness, frailty, lack of strength,” and it can include sickness, mortality, and the vulnerability that makes us liable to fall into sin. It is not itself sin. Hebrews 5:2 says that every earthly high priest is “beset with weakness,” which is why he can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward. Jesus himself shared human weakness: Paul says he “was crucified in weakness” (2 Cor 13:4), and Hebrews describes his “loud cries and tears” in the days of his flesh (5:7). He knows the limits of a human body, the strain of hunger and fatigue, the pain of rejection, the terror of death (2:14–15). He does not sympathize with sin as such; he hates sin. But he sympathizes with the weakness that makes us vulnerable to it, and he is gentle toward the weak.

The ground of his sympathy is his own experience: he “has been tempted in every respect in the same way as we are.” The participle πεπειρασμένον pepeirasmenon, from πειράζω peirazō, is in the perfect tense. His temptations happened in the past, during his earthly life, but their effect continues: he is now, and always will be, the high priest who has been tempted. His experience of testing is a permanent part of who he is as our priest. (A few manuscripts have a slightly different form of the participle, but the meaning is unaffected.)

The verb peirazō covers both “test” and “tempt.” It can describe trials and pressures that test a person’s faithfulness, and it can describe enticements to do evil. In Jesus’ life the two belong together. He was tested by suffering, by hostility from sinners (12:3), by the agony of facing death (5:7), and by the pressure to turn aside from the path of obedience. The Gospels describe his temptation in the wilderness for forty days, where the devil urged him to use his power for himself, to test God, and to gain the kingdoms of the world by worshiping Satan (Matt 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13). Luke adds that the devil departed “until an opportune time” (Luke 4:13). Jesus later told his disciples, “You are those who have stayed with me in my trials” (Luke 22:28). In Gethsemane he prayed in anguish, “not my will, but yours be done” (Luke 22:42), and on the cross he was taunted, “If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross” (Matt 27:40). Hebrews 2:18 had already said, “because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted.”

There is a quiet but powerful connection with the preceding exposition. Psalm 95, quoted in 3:8–9, recalled “the day of testing in the wilderness, where your fathers tested me” (the noun peirasmos and the verb peirazō). Israel was tested in the wilderness and fell. Jesus was tested in the wilderness—and throughout his life—and did not fall. Where the people failed, their high priest stood firm. That is why he can lead them into the rest they could not enter.

The phrase “in every respect” (κατὰ πάντα kata panta) echoes 2:17, where Jesus “had to be made like his brothers in every respect.” It does not mean that Jesus faced every specific temptation that every person faces; he did not face the temptations peculiar to old age, or to marriage, or to a later culture. It means that he faced temptation in every area of human life and in its full range and depth—through desire, through fear, through suffering, through the offer of an easier way. The next phrase, καθ’ ὁμοιότητα kath’ homoiotēta, “according to likeness,” can mean “in the same way as we are” or “in keeping with his likeness to us.” Either way, the point is that his temptations were real human temptations, not a play performed for show. He was tested as a true man.

Then come the decisive words: “yet without sin” (χωρὶς ἁμαρτίας chōris hamartias). The preposition chōris means “apart from, without.” The phrase can be understood in two complementary ways. Primarily, it states the outcome: he was tempted in every way, yet he never sinned. He never yielded to any temptation, in thought, word, or deed. Secondarily, it may also suggest that his temptations did not arise from any sinful inclination within him. James explains that “each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire” (Jas 1:14). Our temptations are often fed by our own corruption. Jesus’ temptations came from outside him and from the real pressures of his human situation, not from an inner bent toward evil. Both senses are true, and both are taught elsewhere in Scripture.

Scripture on the Sinlessness of Jesus

The New Testament speaks with one voice. Hebrews describes him as “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26), who “offered himself without blemish to God” (9:14). Paul says God made him “who knew no sin” to be sin for us (2 Cor 5:21). John writes, “in him there is no sin” (1 John 3:5). Jesus himself challenged his opponents, “Which of you convicts me of sin?” (John 8:46).

Peter applies to Jesus the words of Isaiah’s suffering servant (1 Pet 2:22): עַל לֹא־חָמָס עָשָׂה וְלֹא מִרְמָה בְּפִיו ‘al lo-chamas ‘asah welo mirmah befiw, “although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth” (Isa 53:9). In Isaiah the servant’s innocence is what makes his suffering for others so remarkable: he bears the sins of many though he has none of his own (Isa 53:4–6, 11–12). Hebrews draws the same conclusion. Because Jesus is sinless, he does not need to offer sacrifice first for his own sins, as the Levitical high priests did (7:27); he can offer himself wholly for others.

The combination of real temptation and complete sinlessness has raised a question that Christians have long discussed: could Jesus have sinned? This question must be handled with care, because Scripture does not address it directly, and it is easy to say more than the text warrants.

Interpretive Options: Could Jesus have sinned?

  • Impeccability: he was not able to sin. This has been the dominant view in the church’s history. Its defenders point out that the one who was tempted is the eternal Son of God (1:2–3, 8), and that “God cannot be tempted with evil” (Jas 1:13). The human nature of Jesus was united to the person of the Son, so that every act of Jesus was the act of that person, who is “the same yesterday and today and forever” (13:8). God’s saving purpose, foretold by the prophets, could not fail: of his servant God said, לֹא יִכְהֶה וְלֹא יָרוּץ lo yikhheh welo yaruts, “he will not grow faint, nor be crushed” until he has established justice (Isa 42:4).
  • Peccability: he was able not to sin, but could have sinned. Some theologians have argued that unless sin was a real possibility, Jesus’ temptations could not have been genuine, and his victory would not truly be an example for us. They stress his full humanity and the language of Hebrews about his learning obedience through suffering (5:8).

Weighing these views from Scripture, two things must be held without compromise. First, the temptations were real. Hebrews says he “suffered when tempted” (2:18) and offered prayers “with loud cries and tears” (5:7). Any view that turns his temptations into play-acting contradicts the text. Second, he was without sin—not by chance, but as the holy Son of God (7:26). Any view that makes his sinlessness uncertain or precarious sits uneasily with his identity as the Son who is the same forever and with the certainty of God’s promises concerning him.

The peccability view rests on an assumption that Scripture does not teach: that a temptation is real only if yielding to it is possible. The reality of temptation lies in the pressure and pull that are actually felt, not in the possibility of failure. A great army may truly assault a fortress that cannot be taken; the assault is no less real because the walls hold. B. F. Westcott made a related and important observation: sympathy with the sinner in his trial does not depend on having sinned, but on having felt the full strength of the temptation; and only one who never yields knows the full force of temptation, since the one who yields stops short of feeling its whole weight. On this reading, Jesus’ sinlessness does not make his temptations lighter than ours, but heavier. He endured temptation to its limit and did not give way.

The impeccability view therefore coheres best with Scripture’s teaching about who Jesus is, provided it is held in a way that fully honors the reality of his human testing. Beyond this, believers should be content to confess what Scripture says—truly tempted, yet without sin—and not press curiosity into areas where the text is silent.

Why does this matter for the hearers? Because it means their high priest is perfectly fitted to help them. A priest who had sinned would need forgiveness himself, and his sympathy might shade into excuse. A priest who had never been tempted could not sympathize at all. Jesus is neither. He knows from the inside the full weight of temptation, and he knows from the inside how to overcome it. His sympathy is not indulgent; it does not tell us that sin does not matter. It is the compassion of one who has fought the same battle and won, and who stands ready to help us fight it too (2:18). When believers come to him in weakness, they meet neither a harsh judge nor a fellow failure, but a victorious brother and a merciful priest.

4:16

Let us then draw near with boldness to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace for help in time of need.

The section reaches its climax in an invitation: “Let us then draw near” (προσερχώμεθα οὖν proserchōmetha oun). The word “then” (oun) draws the conclusion from verses 14–15. Since we have such a high priest—great, exalted, yet sympathetic and tested—we can come to God. The verb is in the present tense: “let us keep on drawing near,” making it a habit of life, not a single occasion.

Word Study: προσέρχομαι proserchomai

The verb means “to come toward, approach, draw near.” In ordinary Greek it can be used of any approach, but in the Greek Old Testament it often has a sacred sense: priests drawing near to the altar or the people approaching God in worship. It frequently translates Hebrew qarav, “to draw near,” the root of qorban, “offering” (literally, “something brought near”). When Aaron began his ministry, Moses said to him, קְרַב אֶל־הַמִּזְבֵּחַ qerav el-hammizbeach, “draw near to the altar,” and make atonement for yourself and for the people (Lev 9:7).

In Hebrews the word becomes a key term for the privilege of believers. Jesus “is able to save completely those who draw near to God through him” (7:25). The old sacrifices could never perfect “those who draw near” (10:1), but now “let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith” (10:22). “Whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists” (11:6). And the climactic contrast of the sermon is between Sinai, to which Israel “drew near” in terror, and Mount Zion, to which believers “have drawn near” in joy (12:18, 22). What was once the special privilege of priests is now the privilege of the whole people of God through Jesus.

They are to come “with boldness” (μετὰ παρρησίας meta parrēsias). The noun παρρησία parrēsia originally meant the freedom of speech enjoyed by a citizen in the assembly of a Greek city—the right to say what one thinks openly. It came to mean frankness, confidence, and freedom of access, as when a trusted friend of a king may speak freely in his presence. In Hebrews it is both an inner confidence and an objective right of access. The hearers are urged to “hold fast our boldness” (3:6) and not to “throw away your boldness” (10:35), and they are told that they have “boldness to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus” (10:19). Boldness is not presumption or casualness. It is the confidence of those who have been given the right to come, because the way has been opened by the blood of their high priest.

The contrast with the old covenant is striking. In Leviticus the Lord commanded, וְאַל־יָבֹא בְכָל־עֵת אֶל־הַקֹּדֶשׁ … כִּי בֶּעָנָן אֵרָאֶה עַל־הַכַּפֹּרֶת we’al-yavo vekhol-‘et el-haqqodesh … ki be‘anan era’eh ‘al-hakkapporet, “he [Aaron] must not come at any time into the holy place … for I appear in the cloud over the mercy seat” (Lev 16:2). Only the high priest, only once a year, only with blood and a cloud of incense could approach, and no one else could even be present in the tent while he did so (Lev 16:17). Now all believers—not once a year, but continually, and especially “in time of need”—are invited to draw near to God himself.

They draw near “to the throne of grace” (τῷ θρόνῳ τῆς χάριτος tō thronō tēs charitos). A throne is the seat of a king, the place of authority and judgment. Hebrews has already spoken of God’s throne and of the Son seated at his right hand (1:3, 8, 13), and it will say that Jesus “sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens” (8:1) and “of the throne of God” (12:2). After the warning of 4:12–13, one might expect the throne to be a place of terror, where every creature stands naked before the Judge. But this throne is characterized by grace. The genitive “of grace” describes the throne’s nature: it is the throne from which grace is given.

Old Testament Background: The Mercy Seat as God’s Throne

For hearers who knew the tabernacle, “the throne of grace” would recall the ark of the covenant and its golden cover, the כַּפֹּרֶת kapporet, traditionally translated “mercy seat.” God commanded Moses, וְעָשִׂיתָ כַפֹּרֶת זָהָב טָהוֹר we‘asita khapporet zahav tahor, “you shall make a mercy seat of pure gold” (Exod 25:17), with two cherubim of gold overshadowing it with their wings. The word kapporet is related to the verb kipper, “to make atonement,” and it was upon this cover that the high priest sprinkled the blood of the sin offering on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:14–15). The Greek Old Testament calls it the ἱλαστήριον hilastērion, the “place of atonement,” and Hebrews uses that word in 9:5.

The mercy seat was also the place of God’s royal presence. God told Moses, וְנוֹעַדְתִּי לְךָ שָׁם וְדִבַּרְתִּי אִתְּךָ מֵעַל הַכַּפֹּרֶת מִבֵּין שְׁנֵי הַכְּרֻבִים weno‘adti lekha sham wedibbarti ittekha me‘al hakkapporet mibbein shenei hakkeruvim, “there I will meet with you, and I will speak with you from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim” (Exod 25:22). Israel praised the Lord as the one יֹשֵׁב הַכְּרֻבִים yoshev hakkeruvim, “enthroned above the cherubim” (2 Sam 6:2; Ps 80:2 [English 80:1]); “The Lord reigns … he is enthroned above the cherubim” (Ps 99:1). The ark and its cover were thus the earthly footstool of the heavenly King’s throne, and at the same time the place where atonement was made. Royal authority and atoning mercy met at the same spot.

Hebrews 4:16 brings these ideas together. The throne of God in heaven, of which the mercy seat was a copy, is now a “throne of grace” because atonement has been made by the blood of the great high priest. The place of judgment has become the place of mercy—not because God’s holiness has been relaxed, but because his justice has been satisfied in the sacrifice of his Son (2:17; 9:11–14).

The purpose of drawing near is stated in two parallel clauses: “so that we may receive mercy and find grace.” Mercy (ἔλεος eleos) is God’s compassion toward the wretched and guilty; grace (χάρις charis) is his free, undeserved favor and the strength it supplies. Many interpreters have distinguished them helpfully: we receive mercy for our failures and sins, and we find grace for the help and strength we need to stand. Both describe the character of the God who revealed himself to Moses as אֵל רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן el rachum wechannun, “a God merciful and gracious” (Exod 34:6). In 2:17 Jesus became a “merciful” high priest; here mercy flows from the throne where he ministers.

The phrase “find grace” has a Hebrew ring. The Old Testament often speaks of “finding favor [chen] in the eyes of” someone. “Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord” (וְנֹחַ מָצָא חֵן בְּעֵינֵי יְהוָה wenoach matsa chen be‘einei YHWH, Gen 6:8). Moses pleaded with God on the basis of having found favor in his sight, and God answered, “I will do this very thing that you have spoken, for you have found favor in my eyes, and I know you by name” (Exod 33:17). In that same conversation God promised, “My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest” (Exod 33:14). The preacher’s hearers, wandering like Israel between redemption and rest, are invited to find favor in God’s eyes as Moses did—not through their own merit, but through their high priest.

David deSilva and others have shown how the language of “grace” would have sounded in the social world of the first century. Charis was the word for the generous favor of a patron or benefactor toward a client, and for the gratitude the client returned. People in need often approached a powerful patron through a broker—a friend of the patron who could gain them a hearing. Hebrews presents God as the supreme Benefactor, seated on a throne whose very character is generosity, and Jesus as the one who gives his people access to him. Unlike human patrons, this King does not tire of petitioners or favor only the powerful. His throne is a throne of grace.

The final phrase specifies the kind of grace they will find: “for help in time of need” (εἰς εὔκαιρον βοήθειαν eis eukairon boētheian). The adjective εὔκαιρος eukairos is formed from eu, “well,” and kairos, “time, the right moment.” It means “well-timed, opportune, coming at the right time.” The help God gives is timely help—neither too early nor too late, but suited to the moment of need. The noun βοήθεια boētheia, “help,” recalls 2:18, where Jesus “is able to help [boēthēsai] those who are being tempted,” and it anticipates 13:6, “The Lord is my helper; I will not fear.” The need in view is especially the moment of temptation and trial. When the pressure to drift, to harden the heart, or to shrink back is strongest, that is the time to draw near.

The Psalms often praise God as the helper who is present in the time of trouble. Psalm 46:2 (English 46:1) says, אֱלֹהִים לָנוּ מַחֲסֶה וָעֹז עֶזְרָה בְצָרוֹת נִמְצָא מְאֹד elohim lanu machaseh wa‘oz, ‘ezrah vetsarot nimtsa me’od, “God is our refuge and strength, a help in troubles, very readily found.” Psalm 9:10 (English 9:9) calls the Lord מִשְׂגָּב לְעִתּוֹת בַּצָּרָה misgav le‘ittot batsarah, “a stronghold for times of trouble,” and the Greek version of that verse speaks of the Lord as a helper at the opportune times of affliction, using a noun related to eukairos. The God of the psalms, who was found as a present help by Israel in its trials, is the God who now offers timely help through Jesus.

With this invitation, chapter 4 ends where it began—with the people of God on the way to his rest—but the mood has changed. The chapter opened with “let us fear” and closes with “let us draw near.” The fear was right: the word of God is a sharp sword, and all things lie open to the eyes of the one to whom we must give account. But the same God has given a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, who has been tempted as we are without sin, and who has turned the throne of judgment into a throne of grace. The preacher will spend the following chapters explaining how this priesthood works: who appointed Jesus (5:5–6), what order he belongs to (7:1–28), what covenant he mediates (8:1–13), and what sacrifice he offered (9:1–10:18). But the invitation comes first. Before the explanation, the call: come near, come boldly, come now, in your time of need.
