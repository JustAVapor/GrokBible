Hebrews 9

The Blood of the Eternal Covenant

Hebrews 9 stands at the center of the central section of the sermon. Chapter 8 announced the “main point” (8:1): we have a high priest who sits at the right hand of the Majesty in the heavens and ministers in the true tabernacle, the mediator of a better covenant promised through Jeremiah. Chapter 9 now shows what that ministry actually is. Having explained where Christ serves (the heavenly sanctuary) and under what arrangement (the new covenant), the preacher turns to how he serves: by entering God’s presence once for all through his own blood. Chapter 10 will then draw the conclusions about the single, sufficient sacrifice and turn them into the great exhortation, “Let us draw near” (10:19–25).

The chapter falls into two halves, joined by a strong “but” at 9:11. Verses 1–10 describe the earthly sanctuary of the first covenant: its two compartments, its furnishings, its daily priestly service in the outer tent, and the single annual entry of the high priest into the inner room on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16). The preacher is not belittling the tabernacle. He describes it with obvious reverence, naming the gold, the ark, the cherubim “of glory.” But he reads the very layout of the building as a message from the Holy Spirit: as long as the first tent stands, the way into God’s presence has not yet been opened. The tabernacle was a “parable” (9:9), a God-given picture that could cleanse the body for worship but could not cleanse the conscience.

Verses 11–28 set Christ over against that whole order. He has come as high priest of the good things now arrived; he passed through a greater tabernacle not made with hands; he entered, not with the blood of goats and calves, but through his own blood, once for all, having secured an eternal redemption (9:11–12). If animal blood and the ashes of a red heifer could restore Israelites to ritual purity, “how much more” will the blood of Christ, who offered himself through the eternal Spirit, cleanse our conscience to serve the living God (9:13–14)? The preacher then explains why a death was necessary to bring in the new covenant, using a play on the Greek word διαθήκη diathēkē, which can mean both “covenant” and “will” (9:15–17), and recalls how Moses inaugurated the Sinai covenant with sprinkled blood (9:18–22). The chapter closes with a soaring summary: Christ has entered heaven itself to appear before God for us; he has been manifested once at the climax of the ages to abolish sin; and he will appear a second time, not to deal with sin again, but to bring salvation to those who are eagerly waiting for him (9:23–28).

Several key words carry the argument and are worth watching:

  • Blood (αἷμα haima) is concentrated in this chapter as in hardly any other passage of the New Testament (9:7, 12 [twice], 13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 [twice, counting the compound “blood-shedding”], 25). In Scripture, blood is life given up in death (Lev 17:11).
  • Once (ἅπαξ hapax) and once for all (ἐφάπαξ ephapax) set the high priest’s yearly entry (9:7) against Christ’s single, final entry (9:12, 26, 27, 28).
  • Conscience (συνείδησις syneidēsis, 9:9, 14) names the inner place that the old rites could not reach and Christ’s blood does reach.
  • Purify (καθαρίζω katharizō, 9:14, 22, 23) and sprinkle (ῥαντίζω rhantizō, 9:13, 19, 21) come from the world of Israel’s purity laws.
  • Eternal (αἰώνιος aiōnios): eternal redemption (9:12), the eternal Spirit (9:14), the eternal inheritance (9:15). What Christ does belongs to the age to come and cannot wear out.
  • Hands: the earthly sanctuary was “made with hands” (χειροποίητος cheiropoiētos, 9:11, 24); Christ’s is not.

The reader who does not know the Levitical system well will find this chapter easier if Leviticus 16 (the Day of Atonement), Numbers 19 (the red heifer), and Exodus 24 (the covenant ceremony at Sinai) are kept open alongside it. The preacher assumes his hearers know these passages intimately. His purpose is not antiquarian. A congregation tempted to drift back toward the visible, familiar machinery of the old order needed to see that every part of that machinery pointed beyond itself, and that the reality it pointed to has now arrived in Jesus. To go back would be to trade the substance for the shadow, the open way for the closed curtain, a cleansed conscience for a cleansed body. The chapter ends, fittingly, not with a backward look but with a forward one: Christians are those who “eagerly await him.”
