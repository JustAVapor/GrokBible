From Milk to Solid Food (5:11–14)

This section (5:11–6:12) continues in chapter 6.

Translation

11About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become sluggish in hearing. 12For indeed, though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the first elementary principles of the oracles of God; and you have become people who need milk, not solid food. 13For everyone who lives on milk is unacquainted with the word of righteousness, for he is an infant. 14But solid food is for the mature, for those whose faculties have been trained by practice to distinguish good and evil.

Setting and Structure

The author has just reached the edge of his great theme: Christ, “designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek” (5:10). We expect him to plunge straight into it. Instead he stops and turns to his hearers. What follows, from 5:11 to 6:20, is the longest exhortation in the sermon before the warnings of chapter 10. Only at 6:20, with almost the same words that ended 5:10, does he return to Melchizedek, and chapter 7 finally gives the promised exposition.

Why the interruption? Because the author is a pastor, not just a teacher. He knows that truth about Christ cannot be poured into ears that are not listening. Before he can give his hearers solid food, he must wake them up. The passage moves in four stages:

  • 5:11–14: Rebuke. You have become sluggish in hearing. You ought to be teachers, but you need milk again, like infants.
  • 6:1–3: Resolve. Therefore let us leave the elementary teaching and be carried on to maturity.
  • 6:4–8: Warning. Those who have tasted the heavenly gift and then fall away cannot be restored to repentance.
  • 6:9–12: Reassurance. But we are convinced of better things concerning you. Do not become sluggish, but imitate those who inherit the promises through faith and patience.

The word “sluggish” (νωθροί nōthroi) appears at the start (5:11) and at the end (6:12) of this unit. This framing device, called an inclusio, marks 5:11–6:12 as a single block of exhortation. Then 6:13–20 forms a bridge back to the main argument through God’s oath to Abraham, which prepares for the oath of Psalm 110:4.

The four verses of our passage are built on an extended image drawn from the ancient world of child-rearing and education. The vocabulary moves from the classroom (“teachers,” “teach,” “elementary principles”) to the nursery (“milk,” “solid food,” “infant”) to the gymnasium (“trained,” “practice”). Each picture makes the same point. The readers are behaving like children when they should be adults. They are still learning the alphabet when they ought to be reading, still drinking milk when they ought to be eating meat, still unpracticed when they ought to be skilled.

Historical Background: Milk, Solid Food, and Ancient Education

The contrast between milk for infants and solid food for adults was a well-worn image in ancient moral teaching. Greek philosophers used it to distinguish beginners from advanced students. Epictetus, the Stoic teacher of the late first century, rebukes his students: are you not willing, now at last, to be weaned like children and to take more solid food (Discourses 2.16.39)? Philo of Alexandria compares the preliminary school subjects, the general education given to youths, to milk, and the higher teaching of virtue and wisdom to the solid food of grown men (On Agriculture 9). The image also appears in the New Testament. Paul tells the Corinthians, “I fed you with milk, not solid food, for you were not yet able to receive it. Indeed, even now you are not yet able” (1 Cor 3:2). Peter uses milk in a positive sense: “like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow up to salvation” (1 Pet 2:2).

In Greek education a child was a νήπιος nēpios, an infant or minor, and the goal of education was to become τέλειος teleios, a grown man, mature and complete. Both words appear in our passage (5:13–14). Education was expected to progress. A student who after years of instruction still needed the alphabet would be a source of shame to himself and his teachers.

This sharp language is also part of a deliberate rhetorical strategy. In the honor-conscious culture of the ancient Mediterranean, a speaker could stir his hearers to action by making them ashamed of their present condition. David deSilva has shown how Hebrews uses honor and shame in this way throughout. The author is not mocking his readers. He wants them to feel how unworthy their sluggishness is of their calling, so that they will shake it off. Notice that he immediately softens the blow with “let us” in 6:1, and with warm reassurance in 6:9–10. A good shepherd sometimes has to speak hard words, but he speaks them in love.

Finally, there is a tension in the passage that careful readers notice. If the hearers really are infants who can only take milk, why does the author go on to give them the solid food of chapter 7? The answer seems to be that the rebuke is meant to provoke, not to describe their capacity once and for all. He tells them what they have become in order to rouse them out of it. And he trusts that the solid food itself, the glorious truth of Christ’s eternal priesthood, will help them grow. In 6:1 he does not say “let us go back to the basics.” He says, “let us be carried on to maturity.”

Verse by Verse

5:11

About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become sluggish in hearing.

About this. The opening words Περὶ οὗ Peri hou can be translated “about whom” or “about which.” The relative pronoun οὗ hou may be masculine or neuter. If masculine, it could refer to Melchizedek, the last person named, or to Christ as high priest after Melchizedek’s order. If neuter, it would refer to the whole subject just introduced. In practice the meaning is much the same. What the author has “much to say” about is Christ’s Melchizedekian priesthood, which he will expound in chapter 7. The translation “about this” keeps the reference open.

We have much to say. Literally, πολὺς ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος polys hēmin ho logos means “the word for us is much,” that is, “we have a great deal to say.” The “we” is the author speaking of himself, a common literary style (compare 2:5; 6:9, 11). He is not apologizing for a long sermon to come. He is announcing that the subject is rich and deep.

Hard to explain. The adjective δυσερμήνευτος dysermēneutos is found only here in the Bible. It combines the prefix dys-, “difficult, hard” (as in English “dysfunction”), with the root of ἑρμηνεύω hermēneuō, “interpret, explain” (from which we get “hermeneutics”). With the infinitive λέγειν legein, “to say,” the phrase means “hard to explain in words.” Why is it hard? Partly because the subject itself is profound. The Melchizedek priesthood requires careful reading of the Old Testament and demands that the hearer think beyond the familiar Levitical categories. But the author immediately locates the main difficulty elsewhere: not in the subject, but in the listeners.

Since you have become sluggish in hearing. The conjunction ἐπεί epei, “since, because,” gives the reason the explanation will be difficult. The verb γεγόνατε gegonate, “you have become,” is in the perfect tense. The perfect tense describes a past action whose results continue into the present. The readers were not always like this. They had once been eager, as 10:32–34 recalls: “you endured a great contest of sufferings … you joyfully accepted the plundering of your property.” But somewhere along the way they became sluggish, and they remain so. The problem is not a lack of intelligence. It is a spiritual decline.

Word Study: νωθρός nōthros

The adjective means “sluggish, slow, lazy, dull.” It was used of animals that moved slowly, of minds that grasped things slowly, and of people who were slack in their duties. Sirach uses it when he warns against being sluggish and slack in one’s deeds (Sir 4:29). In the New Testament the word occurs only here and in 6:12, where the author says, “so that you may not become sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience inherit the promises.” The two occurrences frame the whole exhortation.

The phrase “in hearing” (ταῖς ἀκοαῖς tais akoais, literally “in the ears” or “in your hearings”) shows what kind of sluggishness the author has in mind. It is a dullness of reception. The word of God comes to them, but it does not penetrate. This is serious in a sermon that has placed such stress on hearing: “we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard” (2:1); “Today, if you hear his voice” (3:7, 15; 4:7); “the word they heard did not benefit them, because it was not united by faith with those who heard” (4:2). Sluggish hearing is the first step on the road to drifting (2:1) and hardening (3:13).

There is a link, too, with the verses just before. Christ “learned obedience” (ὑπακοή hypakoē, 5:8), and he is the source of salvation to those who “obey” (ὑπακούω hypakouō, 5:9). Both words come from the root “hear.” The readers, by contrast, have become dull in “hearing” (ἀκοή akoē). The contrast is pointed. The Servant of the Lord had an ear opened by God morning by morning, and he did not turn back (Isa 50:4–5). These believers are letting their ears grow heavy. Since true obedience begins with true hearing, sluggish ears are a threat to the obedience by which salvation is received.

Many pastors will recognize this condition. It is possible to sit under the preaching of God’s word for years and absorb very little, not because the preaching is poor but because the heart has become inattentive. The cure is not merely more information. It is a renewed earnestness, a hunger for God that makes us strain to hear.

5:12

For indeed, though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the first elementary principles of the oracles of God; and you have become people who need milk, not solid food.

The author now explains (“for indeed,” καὶ γάρ kai gar) what he means by “sluggish in hearing.” He draws a contrast between what they ought to be and what they have become.

You ought to be teachers. The participle ὀφείλοντες opheilontes, “being obligated, owing it,” uses the same verb as 5:3, where the high priest “is obligated” to offer for himself. There is a moral duty here. “By this time” (διὰ τὸν χρόνον dia ton chronon, literally “because of the time”) means that, given how long they have been believers, they should have advanced far enough to instruct others. The author does not mean that every member should hold the office of teacher. James warns, “Not many of you should become teachers” (Jas 3:1). He means that mature believers should be able to explain the faith, encourage the weak, and correct error. Paul was confident that the Roman Christians were “able to instruct one another” (Rom 15:14). He urged the Colossians to be “teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom” (Col 3:16). Hebrews itself calls the whole congregation to “exhort one another every day” (3:13) and to “stir up one another to love and good works” (10:24). Christians are meant to grow from being taught to teaching.

You need someone to teach you again. Instead, they are going backward. The word πάλιν palin, “again,” stings. They need to go back to the beginning.

Textual Note: “Someone” or “What”?

The earliest Greek manuscripts were written without accent marks. The word τινα tina can therefore be read in two ways. Accented as τινά tina (as in the SBL Greek text used for this study), it is the indefinite pronoun “someone”: “you need someone to teach you the elementary principles.” Accented as τίνα tina, it is the interrogative “what”: “you need to be taught what the elementary principles are.” Both readings are grammatically possible, and the difference in meaning is small. The first stresses their need for a teacher, the second their ignorance of the basics. Most modern editions and translations prefer “someone.” Either way, the rebuke is the same.

The first elementary principles of the oracles of God. The phrase τὰ στοιχεῖα τῆς ἀρχῆς τῶν λογίων τοῦ θεοῦ ta stoicheia tēs archēs tōn logiōn tou theou piles up words that emphasize how basic this teaching is.

  • στοιχεῖα stoicheia (from στοιχεῖον stoicheion) means “elements,” the basic components of something. It was used for the letters of the alphabet, the basic parts of a subject, and the physical elements of the world. In Galatians 4:3, 9 and Colossians 2:8, 20 Paul uses it for the “elemental things” of the old order. Here it means the ABCs of the faith.
  • τῆς ἀρχῆς tēs archēs, “of the beginning,” adds that these are the very first lessons. A literal rendering would be “the ABCs of the beginning.” The same word returns in 6:1: “the word of the beginning of Christ,” that is, the elementary teaching about Christ.
  • τῶν λογίων τοῦ θεοῦ tōn logiōn tou theou, “of the oracles of God.” The word λόγιον logion means a divine utterance, an oracle. In the Greek Old Testament it often translates אִמְרָה ’imrah, “word, saying.” For example, Psalm 12 says: אִמֲרוֹת יְהוָה אֲמָרוֹת טְהֹרוֹת ’imarot YHWH ’amarot tehorot, “The words of the Lord are pure words” (Ps 12:7 in Hebrew; 12:6 in English). Paul says that the Jews were “entrusted with the oracles of God” (Rom 3:2), meaning the Old Testament Scriptures. Stephen calls the law given at Sinai “living oracles” (Acts 7:38). Here the phrase probably means God’s revelation as a whole, the Old Testament Scriptures as now understood in the light of Christ.

The author spells out the content of these “first principles” in 6:1–2: repentance from dead works, faith toward God, teaching about washings, laying on of hands, resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment. Most of these were basic matters that Jewish believers would have shared with their synagogue background. The readers were in danger of retreating to these foundations, which Judaism and Christianity held in common, and stopping there. They were not moving on to the distinctively Christian truth that Christ is the eternal high priest whose sacrifice has fulfilled and replaced the old covenant system.

You have become people who need milk. The second γεγόνατε gegonate, “you have become,” echoes the first in 5:11. Again the perfect tense points to a decline. They have regressed. “Milk” (γάλακτος galaktos, from γάλα gala) stands for the elementary teaching just described. “Solid food” (στερεᾶς τροφῆς stereas trophēs) stands for the deeper teaching the author wants to give them. The adjective στερεός stereos means “firm, solid, hard.”

The Old Testament also uses weaning as a picture of readiness to learn. In Isaiah 28:9 the scoffers of Jerusalem mock the prophet: אֶת־מִי יוֹרֶה דֵעָה … גְּמוּלֵי מֵחָלָב עַתִּיקֵי מִשָּׁדָיִם ’et-mi yoreh de‘ah … gemuley mechalav ‘attiqey mishshadayim, “Whom will he teach knowledge? … Those weaned from milk, those taken from the breast?” They resented being treated like small children. The readers of Hebrews have the opposite problem. They need to be treated like small children, and they ought to be ashamed of it.

Milk is not bad. Infants need it, and the elementary truths of the faith are precious and must never be abandoned (6:1 speaks of leaving them behind as a foundation is left behind when one builds on it, not as rubbish is left behind). But milk is meant to be a stage. A child who is still on milk at ten years old is not healthy. The same is true of a believer who never moves beyond the first lessons.

5:13

For everyone who lives on milk is unacquainted with the word of righteousness, for he is an infant.

Verses 13–14 explain the contrast between milk and solid food, one verse for each. Verse 13 describes the infant. Verse 14 describes the mature.

Everyone who lives on milk. The participle μετέχων metechōn, from μετέχω metechō, means “sharing in, partaking of.” “Lives on milk” gives the sense: milk is his diet. There may be a quiet irony here. Earlier the author called his readers “partakers (μέτοχοι metochoi) of a heavenly calling” (3:1) and “partakers of Christ” (3:14), using a closely related word. Those who are partakers of Christ should not be content to partake of nothing but milk.

Unacquainted with the word of righteousness. The adjective ἄπειρος apeiros means “inexperienced, unskilled, unacquainted with.” It is formed from the negative prefix a- and πεῖρα peira, “trial, experience.” (The same root lies behind the word “tested” in 2:18 and 4:15.) The infant is not simply ignorant of certain facts. He lacks experience. He has not yet used and tested the word of righteousness in real life. This prepares for verse 14, where the mature are those who have been trained “by practice.”

Interpretive Options: “The Word of Righteousness”

  • The message about righteousness. Some, especially in the Reformation tradition, understand the phrase as the gospel message of the righteousness God provides in Christ. On this reading the infant has not grasped the full doctrine of salvation.
  • Moral instruction. Others take “righteousness” in its ethical sense, as teaching about right conduct. The infant lacks the moral understanding to know right from wrong. This fits the reference to distinguishing good and evil in 5:14.
  • Mature or competent speech. Some interpreters suggest that the phrase is roughly idiomatic: an infant cannot yet understand or use ordinary, correct speech. On this view the point is simply that a baby cannot follow adult conversation.
  • Teaching about Christ as the righteous king-priest. Others note that the solid food the author is about to serve concerns Melchizedek, whose name means “king of righteousness” (7:2). The “word of righteousness” would then be the advanced teaching about Christ’s priesthood.

The context suggests that these views need not be sharply separated. The “word of righteousness” is the mature teaching of God’s word, as opposed to the milk of first principles. It concerns both the righteousness that Christ, our righteous king-priest, provides, and the righteous living that flows from it. The infant is unacquainted with it, not because it is hidden from him, but because he has not yet grown into it by experience. Verse 14 confirms that the author is thinking of a teaching that must be practiced, not merely heard.

For he is an infant. The final clause explains the infant’s inexperience. νήπιος nēpios means a small child, an infant, someone not yet of age. In Greek law it could also describe a minor who had not yet come into his inheritance (compare Gal 4:1–3). Paul uses the same contrast between infants and the mature. “Brothers, I could not speak to you as spiritual people, but as people of the flesh, as infants (nēpioi) in Christ” (1 Cor 3:1). “When I was a child (nēpios), I spoke like a child … when I became a man, I put away childish things” (1 Cor 13:11). The goal of Christian ministry is “that we may no longer be infants (nēpioi), tossed back and forth by the waves and carried about by every wind of teaching” (Eph 4:14). That last text names the danger of remaining an infant. Those who are not grounded in mature understanding are easily swept away. Hebrews warns, “Do not be carried away by various and strange teachings” (13:9).

The Old Testament describes small children as those who do not yet know good and evil. Moses told the wilderness generation that their little ones, אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יָדְעוּ הַיּוֹם טוֹב וָרָע ’asher lo’-yade‘u hayyom tov vara‘, “who today do not know good and evil,” would enter the land (Deut 1:39). This provides a bridge to the next verse. To be an infant is to lack the capacity to discern good and evil. To be mature is to have it.

5:14

But solid food is for the mature, for those whose faculties have been trained by practice to distinguish good and evil.

Solid food is for the mature. The Greek puts the key word first: τελείων δέ ἐστιν ἡ στερεὰ τροφή teleiōn de estin hē sterea trophē, “of the mature is solid food,” meaning it belongs to them. The word τέλειος teleios means “complete, full-grown, mature,” the opposite of nēpios, “infant.” It is related to τελειόω teleioō, “make perfect,” used of Christ in 5:9, and to τελειότης teleiotēs, “maturity,” in 6:1. This word family is one of the threads that binds Hebrews together. Christ was brought to the goal of his calling through suffering (2:10; 5:9). Believers are called to grow toward their own goal of maturity (6:1). And through his sacrifice Christ “has perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (10:14). Maturity in this passage does not mean sinless perfection. It means being a grown-up believer, rather than a spiritual infant.

Those whose faculties have been trained. The rest of the verse describes the mature. Each word deserves attention.

Word Study: Training for Discernment

αἰσθητήρια aisthētēria (from αἰσθητήριον aisthētērion) occurs only here in the New Testament. It means the organs of perception, the senses, and by extension the faculties of the mind that perceive and judge. It is related to αἴσθησις aisthēsis, “perception, insight” (Phil 1:9), and to our word “aesthetic.” The author pictures the believer’s spiritual senses, the capacities that taste, see, and judge what is spiritually good or bad.

γεγυμνασμένα gegymnasmena, “trained,” is the perfect passive participle of γυμνάζω gymnazō, “to exercise, train.” The word comes from the gymnasium (γυμνάσιον gymnasion), the training ground found in every Greek city, where young men exercised to prepare for athletic contests and for life as citizens. The perfect tense describes a lasting condition produced by past training. Their senses are in a state of being trained. Hebrews uses the same verb in 12:11: discipline “yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.” Notice the link with “righteousness” there and in 5:13. Paul uses it too: “train yourself for godliness” (1 Tim 4:7).

ἕξις hexis, translated “practice,” also occurs only here in the New Testament. It comes from ἔχω echō, “have, hold,” and in Greek philosophy it meant a settled state or disposition, a habit formed by repeated action. Aristotle taught that virtues are acquired by practice: we become just by doing just acts, and brave by doing brave acts (Nicomachean Ethics 2.1). The phrase διὰ τὴν ἕξιν dia tēn hexin could be translated “because of their [mature] condition” or “by constant use.” Either way, it describes a skill that has become second nature through repeated exercise.

The picture is clear. Spiritual maturity is not simply a matter of accumulating knowledge. It is the result of training. Just as an athlete’s body becomes strong and responsive through daily exercise, so the believer’s capacity for spiritual discernment grows strong through repeated use. Every time we hear God’s word and put it into practice, every time we choose good over evil in the light of Scripture, our spiritual senses are exercised and sharpened. This matches Jesus’ teaching: the one who hears his words and does them is like a wise man who built his house on the rock (Matt 7:24). It also matches the experience of Christ himself, who “learned obedience from the things he suffered” (5:8). The Son was trained for his office through obedience under pressure. His people are trained for maturity in the same way.

To distinguish good and evil. The phrase πρὸς διάκρισιν καλοῦ τε καὶ κακοῦ pros diakrisin kalou te kai kakou states the goal of the training. διάκρισις diakrisis means “discernment, the ability to distinguish.” Paul uses it of “distinguishing between spirits” (1 Cor 12:10). The mature believer can tell the difference between good and evil, truth and error, what pleases God and what does not.

Old Testament Background: Knowing Good and Evil

The phrase “good and evil” has deep Old Testament roots. Small children are described as those who do not yet know good and evil (Deut 1:39, discussed at 5:13). Isaiah speaks of the child Immanuel, who will eat curds and honey לְדַעְתּוֹ מָאוֹס בָּרָע וּבָחוֹר בַּטּוֹב leda‘to ma’os bara‘ uvachor battov, “when he knows to refuse the evil and choose the good” (Isa 7:15). The ability to discern good and evil was the mark of a wise ruler. The woman of Tekoa told David that “my lord the king is like the angel of God, לִשְׁמֹעַ הַטּוֹב וְהָרָע lishmoa‘ hattov vehara‘, to hear the good and the evil” (2 Sam 14:17). Note “to hear,” which links discernment with the hearing of 5:11. Solomon asked God for “an understanding heart … to discern between good and evil” (1 Kgs 3:9). At the other end of life, the aged Barzillai lamented that at eighty years old he could no longer discern between good and bad, or taste what he ate and drank (2 Sam 19:36 in Hebrew; 19:35 in English). Remarkably, in that text discernment is tied to taste, just as in Hebrews 5:14 it is tied to food.

There is a contrast with the beginning of the Bible as well. In Eden, Adam and Eve reached for “the knowledge of good and evil” (Gen 2:17) by disobeying God and taking it for themselves. The maturity Hebrews describes comes by the opposite route. It is not grasped in defiance of God’s word but received through patient, obedient training in it. True discernment is the fruit of submission, not autonomy.

For the readers of Hebrews, discernment was urgently needed. They were tempted to drift away from Christ back to the familiar securities of the old covenant. They needed to see clearly that this would not be a safe return to their roots but a deadly turning away from the living God (3:12). They were exposed to “various and strange teachings” about foods and rituals (13:9). Only a congregation with trained spiritual senses could tell the difference between the shadow and the reality, between the copy and the true sanctuary, between the old covenant that was “becoming obsolete” (8:13) and the new covenant in Christ’s blood.

This verse also explains why the author does not simply go back to the milk of the first principles. Maturity is not attained by repeating the same elementary lessons. It comes by being fed solid food and putting it to use. So in 6:1 the author will urge his hearers to press on. Then in chapter 7 he will set before them the solid food of Christ’s eternal priesthood, and he will trust that, as they chew on it and live by it, their senses will be trained and they will grow up into Christ.

The pastoral lesson for every generation is plain. God does not intend his people to remain infants. He provides his word as food. He provides the trials and choices of daily life as the training ground. He provides a high priest who walked the path of obedience before us and who now helps us along it. Spiritual growth requires hearing the word with attention, receiving its deeper truths, and exercising ourselves in obedience until discernment becomes second nature. The alternative, as the next chapter will show, is not a safe and stable infancy. It is the danger of falling away.
