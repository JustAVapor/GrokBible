Press On to Maturity (6:1–12)

This section began at 5:11.

Translation

1Therefore, leaving behind the elementary word about Christ, let us be carried on to maturity, not laying again a foundation of repentance from dead works and of faith toward God, 2of teaching about washings and laying on of hands, of resurrection of the dead and of eternal judgment. 3And this we will do, if indeed God permits.

4For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift and have become sharers of the Holy Spirit, 5and have tasted that the word of God is good, and the powers of the age to come, 6and then have fallen away—it is impossible to renew them again to repentance, since they are crucifying the Son of God again for themselves and holding him up to public contempt.

7For land that drinks the rain that often comes upon it, and brings forth vegetation useful to those for whose sake it is also farmed, receives a share of blessing from God; 8but if it bears thorns and thistles, it is worthless and near to a curse, and its end is to be burned.

9But concerning you, beloved, we are persuaded of better things—things that belong to salvation—even though we speak in this way. 10For God is not unjust, so as to forget your work and the love that you showed toward his name, in having served the saints and in serving them still. 11And we long for each one of you to show the same earnestness toward the full assurance of the hope until the end, 12so that you may not become sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises.

Setting and Structure

This section is the second half of a single exhortation that began at 5:11. There the preacher had interrupted his discussion of Jesus as “a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek” (5:10) to confront a problem: his hearers had “become sluggish in hearing” (5:11). They ought by now to be teachers, yet they need someone to teach them again “the elementary principles of the oracles of God”; they need milk rather than solid food (5:12–14). The natural expectation, after such a rebuke, would be a remedial lesson in the basics. Instead, the author does the opposite: “Therefore … let us be carried on to maturity” (6:1). He will not lay the foundation again. The reason he gives is the warning of 6:4–8, and the encouragement that tempers it is 6:9–12.

The unit is carefully framed. The word νωθροί nōthroi, “sluggish,” appears at its beginning (5:11, “you have become sluggish in hearing”) and at its end (6:12, “so that you may not become sluggish”). Rhetoricians call such a frame an inclusio; it tells the reader that everything in between belongs together. The structure of 6:1–12 then unfolds in four steps:

  1. The resolve (6:1–3). Leave the elementary teaching and be carried on to maturity; a list of six foundation stones that need not be laid again; “this we will do, if God permits.”
  2. The warning (6:4–6). One long Greek sentence: it is impossible to renew to repentance those who, having shared richly in the blessings of God, have fallen away, because they are crucifying the Son of God again.
  3. The parable (6:7–8). Two kinds of land receive the same rain; one yields a crop and receives blessing, the other yields thorns and is burned.
  4. The reassurance and the appeal (6:9–12). “But we are persuaded of better things concerning you”; God will not forget your love; show the same earnestness to the end; imitate those who inherit the promises.

This pattern—sharp warning followed by warm confidence—is characteristic of Hebrews. The same sequence occurs in 10:26–39, where the terrifying words about “no longer a sacrifice for sins” (10:26) are followed by “remember the former days” (10:32) and “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39). The author is not lurching between moods. He is a pastor who knows that drifting people need to be startled awake and that frightened people need to be anchored. He does both, deliberately, in the same breath.

The historical setting sharpens the urgency. The hearers were, in all likelihood, Jewish believers in Jesus (with some Gentiles among them), probably in or near Rome, who had once endured public abuse, imprisonment, and the plundering of their property with joy (10:32–34). Now, perhaps a decade or more later, the pressure had shifted. Their enthusiasm had cooled; some were neglecting to meet together (10:25); the synagogue, with its legal recognition in the Roman world, its ancient rites, and its visible temple in Jerusalem, offered a safer and more respectable home than a despised sect gathered around a crucified man. A drift back toward the old covenant’s securities would not have felt like apostasy to them. It would have felt like returning to common ground. The author’s list of “foundation” teachings in 6:1–2 is significant here, for almost all of them could be affirmed in a synagogue. The danger was not that the hearers would deny God, resurrection, or judgment; it was that they would settle for these things without the Son, and so lose everything.

The background of the wilderness generation, which the author expounded at length in 3:7–4:13, remains in view. Israel at Kadesh-barnea had seen God’s signs, eaten bread from heaven, been led by the cloud and fire, heard the good report of the land, and even tasted its fruit; yet they refused to enter, and when they afterward tried to undo their refusal, it was too late (Num 14). This story, already the backbone of chapters 3 and 4, supplies the pattern behind the warning of 6:4–6, as we shall see.

Finally, the passage must be read in its larger flow. The author’s solution to the hearers’ immaturity is not less teaching but deeper teaching—the “solid food” of Christ’s Melchizedekian priesthood (chapters 7–10). And his ground of confidence for them is not their own steadiness but God’s oath and Christ’s entry into the sanctuary on their behalf (6:13–20). The warning of 6:4–8 must therefore be heard neither as the whole message nor as a footnote to be explained away. It is one indispensable part of a sermon that aims to bring its hearers safely home.

Verse by Verse

6:1

Therefore, leaving behind the elementary word about Christ, let us be carried on to maturity, not laying again a foundation of repentance from dead works and of faith toward God,

The opening word, Διό Dio, “therefore,” is surprising. After telling his hearers that they need milk (5:12–13), the author draws a conclusion that seems to run the other way: therefore let us move on to solid food. The logic, however, is sound. The previous verse defined the “mature” as those whose faculties have been trained by practice (5:14). Training happens by exercise, not by endlessly repeating the first lesson. A child who is never given anything but milk will remain a child. So the preacher, including himself in the summons (“let us”), resolves to lead them forward. There is also a deeper reason, which 6:4–6 will make explicit: if any of his hearers were to fall away from Christ, going back over the foundation would not help them, for there is no other foundation to lay.

The phrase rendered “the elementary word about Christ” is in Greek τὸν τῆς ἀρχῆς τοῦ Χριστοῦ λόγον ton tēs archēs tou Christou logon, literally “the word of the beginning of Christ.” It deliberately echoes 5:12, “the elementary principles (literally, ‘the elements of the beginning’) of the oracles of God.” The genitive “of Christ” most naturally means “about Christ”—the first instruction concerning him that the hearers received when they came to faith. The participle ἀφέντες aphentes, from ἀφίημι aphiēmi, “leave, let go, leave behind,” does not mean “abandon” or “repudiate.” A builder who “leaves” the foundation to raise the walls has not rejected the foundation; he is building on it. Calvin stresses this very point: the first principles are not to be forsaken but built upon, so that the believer advances beyond them without ever departing from them. The foundation stays where it is; it simply ceases to be the place where one stands still.

The main verb is striking: φερώμεθα pherōmetha, from φέρω pherō, “carry, bear.” It is a present subjunctive used as an exhortation (“let us …”), and its form is passive (Greek uses the same form for middle and passive in the present, but the passive sense is the natural one here). Many English versions render it “let us go on” or “let us press on,” which captures the urgency but loses the voice. Literally, it means “let us be carried.” The same verb is used of a ship driven along by the wind (Acts 27:15, 17, “we were being carried along”), and of the prophets who spoke “being carried along by the Holy Spirit” (2 Pet 1:21). In Hebrews itself, the Son is the one who “carries all things by the word of his power” (1:3). Westcott and others draw out the implication: maturity is not something the believer manufactures by sheer effort; it is a movement into which he yields himself, borne along by a power not his own. The verb holds together human responsibility (the subjunctive is an exhortation: let us!) and divine agency (the voice is passive: let us be carried). Verse 3 will make the divine side explicit: “if God permits.”

Word Study: τελειότης teleiotēs

The goal of being carried is τελειότης teleiotēs, “maturity” or “completeness.” It belongs to a word family that runs through the whole letter: τέλειος teleios, “mature/perfect” (5:14; 9:11), and τελειόω teleioō, “make perfect, bring to perfection,” used of Christ (2:10; 5:9; 7:28), of believers (10:14; 11:40; 12:23), and of what the law could not do (7:19; 9:9; 10:1). In 5:14 the word described the adult who can eat solid food, in contrast to the infant. Here “maturity” is first of all the maturity of understanding that can receive the teaching about Christ’s priesthood. But in Hebrews intellectual maturity is never detached from spiritual reality: the author wants hearers whose grasp of Christ’s high priesthood will make them draw near to God with boldness (4:16; 10:19–22) and hold fast their confession (4:14; 10:23). The maturity in view is the believer’s share in the “perfection” that Christ has achieved for his people.

The author then explains what leaving the elementary word means: “not laying again a foundation.” The participle καταβαλλόμενοι kataballomenoi, “laying down,” was used in the building trade for laying foundations, and θεμέλιος themelios is the ordinary word for a foundation. The word “again” (πάλιν palin) is important; it recurs in 6:6 (“renew again”). A foundation is laid once. A building whose foundation is constantly being torn up and relaid never rises.

Six items follow, arranged (as most commentators recognize) in three pairs. The first pair names the two sides of conversion: “repentance from dead works and faith toward God.” μετάνοια metanoia, “repentance,” means a change of mind that issues in a change of direction—turning away from sin. The phrase “dead works” (νεκρῶν ἔργων nekrōn ergōn) appears again at 9:14, where the blood of Christ “will cleanse our conscience from dead works to serve the living God.” Some have taken “dead works” to mean the rituals of the Levitical law, now obsolete. But the contrast in 9:14 is with serving “the living God,” and the conscience needs cleansing from them—which suggests sinful works, deeds that belong to the realm of death and lead to death (compare Eph 2:1, “dead in your trespasses and sins”). Repentance from dead works, then, is turning away from the whole life of sin. Its positive counterpart is “faith toward God” (πίστεως ἐπὶ θεόν pisteōs epi theon), trust directed to God and resting on him. These two belong together in the apostolic preaching: “repentance toward God and faith toward our Lord Jesus” (Acts 20:21); “repent and believe in the gospel” (Mark 1:15).

It is worth noticing what this first pair does not say. It does not mention Christ by name, his death, or his priesthood. Repentance and faith in God were proclaimed by the prophets and taught in the synagogue. Many commentators, F. F. Bruce among them, have observed that nearly every item in this list could have been affirmed by a devout Jew. That observation will become important in 6:2.

6:2

of teaching about washings and laying on of hands, of resurrection of the dead and of eternal judgment.

The remaining four items complete the foundation. The second pair—“washings and laying on of hands”—concerns rites of initiation into the believing community; the third pair—“resurrection of the dead and eternal judgment”—concerns the last things. The whole list thus moves from the beginning of the Christian life (conversion), through entrance into the community (washing and the laying on of hands), to the end of all things (resurrection and judgment).

Textual Note

The text followed here reads διδαχήν didachēn, “teaching,” in the accusative case, the reading of the early papyrus P46 and Codex Vaticanus. Most other manuscripts read διδαχῆς didachēs, in the genitive, which matches the surrounding genitives (“a foundation of repentance … of faith … of teaching”). The genitive is the easier reading and may be a scribal smoothing; the accusative would stand in apposition to “foundation” (“a foundation … namely, teaching about washings …”). Either way the sense is nearly the same: these matters are foundational instruction. Metzger’s committee judged the question finely balanced.

The word translated “washings” is βαπτισμῶν baptismōn, the genitive plural of βαπτισμός baptismos. Two details matter. First, it is plural: not “baptism” but “washings.” Second, it is not the word the New Testament ordinarily uses for Christian baptism, which is βάπτισμα baptisma (for example, Rom 6:4; Eph 4:5; 1 Pet 3:21). Where baptismos appears elsewhere in the New Testament, it refers to Jewish ritual washings: “washings of cups and pitchers and copper vessels” (Mark 7:4), and in Hebrews itself, the Levitical regulations concerning “food and drink and various washings” (διαφόροις βαπτισμοῖς diaphorois baptismois, 9:10). The historian Josephus, writing in Greek near the end of the first century, uses the same word for the baptism of John the Baptist (Ant. 18.117).

Historical Background: Washings in the First-Century Jewish World

A Jewish believer in the first century lived among many kinds of ritual washing. The law of Moses prescribed washings for priests (Exod 29:4; 30:17–21; Lev 16:4, 24) and for the cleansing of various impurities (Lev 14–15; Num 19). By the Second Temple period, stepped immersion pools (Hebrew miqvaot) were common in Judea and Galilee; archaeologists have found many in Jerusalem near the temple mount. The community behind the Dead Sea Scrolls practiced frequent purifying washings, while insisting (1QS 3) that water could not cleanse one who refused to turn from sin. John the Baptist proclaimed “a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins” (Mark 1:4), and in Acts we meet disciples who knew only John’s baptism and needed further instruction (Acts 18:25; 19:1–5). Whether Gentile converts to Judaism were already being immersed in the first century is debated; the evidence for proselyte baptism comes mostly from later rabbinic sources, and it is best not to build on it.

In such a world, a new Christian would need to be taught the difference between these washings and baptism into the name of Jesus—what each signified, and why Christian baptism is not one more purification to be repeated but the once-for-all sign of union with Christ. This is very likely what “teaching about washings” means: elementary instruction that set Christian baptism in relation to the other washings of Jewish life.

The “laying on of hands” (ἐπιθέσεώς τε χειρῶν epitheseōs te cheirōn) also had deep roots in Israel. The worshiper laid his hand on the head of a sacrificial animal (Lev 1:4), the high priest laid both hands on the scapegoat while confessing Israel’s sins (Lev 16:21), and Moses laid his hands on Joshua to commission him (Num 27:18–23). In the early church the gesture accompanied the giving of the Spirit (Acts 8:17; 19:6), commissioning for service (Acts 6:6; 13:3; 1 Tim 4:14), and healing (Mark 16:18 in the longer ending; Acts 28:8). Paired here with washings, it most likely refers to the laying on of hands that in the apostolic church often accompanied baptism, associated with prayer for the gift of the Spirit (Acts 19:5–6). This connection becomes significant in 6:4, where the author speaks of those who “have become sharers of the Holy Spirit.”

The final pair, “resurrection of the dead and eternal judgment,” names the great hope and the great accountability that frame human life. Both are Old Testament teaching. Daniel had foretold that “many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproach and everlasting contempt” (Dan 12:2). The Pharisees held to the resurrection against the Sadducees (Acts 23:6–8). Hebrews will return to both themes: Abraham reasoned that God could raise the dead (11:19); women received back their dead by resurrection, and others refused release “that they might obtain a better resurrection” (11:35); and “it is appointed for human beings to die once, and after this, judgment” (9:27; compare 10:27, 30–31; 12:23). The adjective “eternal” (αἰώνιος aiōnios) is a favorite of the author’s: eternal salvation (5:9), eternal redemption (9:12), eternal Spirit (9:14), eternal inheritance (9:15), eternal covenant (13:20). Here it marks a judgment whose verdict is final and unending.

Looking back over all six, we can see why the author calls them a foundation and why he will not lay it again. They are true, necessary, and permanent. But as F. F. Bruce and others have noted, every one of them had a recognizable counterpart in the Judaism the hearers had known. A Jewish Christian under pressure could retreat to these teachings—repentance, faith in God, ritual washings, the laying on of hands, resurrection, judgment—and still feel at home in the synagogue, without any open denial. What would be lost in such a retreat is precisely what makes the foundation Christian: Christ himself, his once-for-all sacrifice, and his present priestly ministry. That is why the author’s cure for spiritual infancy is not a review of the common ground but an exposition of what is distinctively and decisively new.

6:3

And this we will do, if indeed God permits.

“This” refers back to the resolve of 6:1: to be carried on to maturity rather than lay the foundation again. The author is confident—“we will do” (ποιήσομεν poiēsomen, future indicative)—but he places that confidence entirely within the will of God: ἐάνπερ ἐπιτρέπῃ ὁ θεός eanper epitrepē ho theos, “if indeed God permits.” The intensified conditional particle eanper (“if indeed, if only”) appears also at 3:6 and 3:14 in some manuscripts and in the best text of 3:14, where it attaches the reality of belonging to Christ to holding fast to the end.

Textual Note

A number of manuscripts read ποιήσωμεν poiēsōmen, “let us do this,” a subjunctive differing from the future indicative by a single vowel (ο/ω, which sounded alike in later Greek). The future, “we will do,” is better supported and fits the confident tone. The meaning differs only slightly: a resolve rather than an exhortation.

On the surface the phrase resembles the pious conditions with which New Testament writers qualify their plans: “I will return to you, if God wills” (Acts 18:21); “I hope to remain with you for some time, if the Lord permits” (1 Cor 16:7, which uses the same verb, ἐπιτρέπω epitrepō); “if the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that” (Jas 4:15). It reminds us that spiritual progress, like every other human undertaking, depends upon God. That is the same truth embedded in the passive verb of 6:1: “let us be carried.” Growth in grace is God’s work before it is ours (Phil 2:12–13).

But the phrase is not merely conventional, for the very next word is γάρ gar, “for”: “if God permits. For it is impossible …” The connection is sobering. The author has just said that he and his hearers will move forward if God permits; now he explains that there is a case in which God does not permit it. For some who have shared in the blessings of the gospel and then turned away, there will be no going forward and no starting over. The condition “if God permits” thus hints that progress toward maturity is not something anyone can take for granted, as though it could be postponed indefinitely and picked up at leisure. Delay in responding to God is dangerous. As the author warned from Psalm 95, the time for hearing is “today” (3:7, 13, 15; 4:7); hearts harden under the deceitfulness of sin (3:13). The repetition of “today” in Hebrews is the positive face of what “if God permits” expresses negatively: the opportunity to grow is a gift, and gifts can be withdrawn from those who despise them.

Calvin draws a pastoral lesson that fits the context: progress in faith is a work of God’s grace, so the teacher must labor and the hearers must strive, yet neither should imagine that success lies in their own power. The verse is thus a hinge. It looks back to the resolve to advance and forward to the warning about those for whom advance has become impossible.

6:4

For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift and have become sharers of the Holy Spirit,

We come now to the passage that has been called the most difficult and most debated in the letter. Before we can weigh the interpretations, we must see exactly what the Greek says, for the structure of the sentence carries much of its meaning.

The Shape of the Greek Sentence (6:4–6)

Verses 4–6 form a single sentence. Stripped to its skeleton, it reads: Impossible … those who [five descriptions] … again to renew to repentance, [two further descriptions]. In order:

  • Ἀδύνατον γάρ Adynaton gar, “For [it is] impossible.” The word stands first for emphasis. The verb it governs does not appear until verse 6.
  • τοὺς tous, “those who …”—a single definite article that governs all five following participles. Grammatically, the same people are in view throughout; the article ties the descriptions together into one portrait.
  • Five aorist participles (the aorist presents an action as a whole, a completed event): (1) φωτισθέντας phōtisthentas, “having been enlightened”; (2) γευσαμένους geusamenous, “having tasted” the heavenly gift; (3) μετόχους γενηθέντας metochous genēthentas, “having become sharers” of the Holy Spirit; (4) γευσαμένους geusamenous, “having tasted” the good word of God and the powers of the age to come; (5) παραπεσόντας parapesontas, “and having fallen away.”
  • The main infinitive: πάλιν ἀνακαινίζειν εἰς μετάνοιαν palin anakainizein eis metanoian, “to renew again to repentance.”
  • Two present participles (the present presents an action as ongoing): ἀνασταυροῦντας anastaurountas, “crucifying again,” and παραδειγματίζοντας paradeigmatizontas, “holding up to contempt.”

Three observations follow. First, the fifth participle is joined to the first four by a simple “and” (καί kai); it is not grammatically marked as a condition (“if they fall away”), although English translations often render it that way. The author describes people who have experienced these things and have fallen away. Second, the change from aorist to present participles marks a shift from what these people experienced to what they are now doing. Third, the emphatic “impossible” at the head, separated from its infinitive by the whole list, creates suspense: the hearer waits through the long catalog of blessings before learning what is impossible, and the weight of everything they have received falls on the final, terrible verdict.

“Impossible.” The adjective ἀδύνατος adynatos appears four times in Hebrews, and each time it is absolute: it is impossible for God to lie (6:18); it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins (10:4); without faith it is impossible to please God (11:6). The author does not use the word loosely to mean “very difficult.” Attempts to soften it to “hard” or “rare” do not respect the author’s usage. Whatever 6:4–6 means, it means that something cannot happen.

“Once enlightened.” The first description is τοὺς ἅπαξ φωτισθέντας tous hapax phōtisthentas, “those who have once been enlightened.” The verb φωτίζω phōtizō, “give light, enlighten,” is used again in 10:32: “remember the former days in which, after you were enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings.” There it plainly refers to the hearers’ coming to the knowledge of the gospel. Elsewhere in the New Testament, God “has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ” (2 Cor 4:6), and Paul prays that the eyes of the Ephesians’ hearts may be “enlightened” (Eph 1:18). By the middle of the second century, Christian writers called baptism “enlightenment”: Justin Martyr says that the washing of baptism is called φωτισμός phōtismos, “illumination,” because those who learn these things are enlightened in understanding (1 Apology 61). Some commentators think Hebrews already reflects that usage, especially since “washings” and “laying on of hands” were just mentioned. It is safer to say that the enlightenment in view is the reception of the light of the gospel, publicly sealed in baptism. The word ἅπαξ hapax, “once,” is significant. In Hebrews it carries the note of something decisive and unrepeatable: Christ appeared “once” at the end of the ages to put away sin (9:26), was offered “once” (9:28), and the worshipers, once cleansed, would no longer have consciousness of sins (10:2). Enlightenment of this kind is not a passing glimmer; it is a decisive event that cannot be gone through a second time.

“Tasted the heavenly gift.” The verb γεύομαι geuomai means “taste,” and figuratively “experience, come to know.” Some interpreters (John Owen, for example) have argued that “tasting” implies something less than eating—a sampling rather than a full meal. But Hebrews itself uses the same verb in 2:9, where Jesus by the grace of God “tasted death for everyone.” Jesus did not merely sample death; he experienced it fully. The word, then, points to real experience. Whether that experience was saving is a separate question, to which we will return; but the word itself does not imply a mere superficial contact. “The heavenly gift” (τῆς δωρεᾶς τῆς ἐπουρανίου tēs dōreas tēs epouraniou) is not further defined. Some take it as the Holy Spirit (as in Acts 2:38; 8:20; 10:45, where the Spirit is “the gift”), but the Spirit is named separately in the next phrase. Others think of the Lord’s Supper or of salvation generally. In view of the wilderness background, it is suggestive that Israel was fed with “bread from heaven” (Exod 16:4; Ps 78:24; compare John 6:31–35). Most likely the phrase gathers up all the grace of God given from heaven in the gospel: forgiveness, adoption, and fellowship with God. The word “heavenly” is a keynote of Hebrews—a heavenly calling (3:1), a heavenly sanctuary (8:5; 9:23), a heavenly country (11:16), a heavenly Jerusalem (12:22).

“Become sharers of the Holy Spirit.” The phrase μετόχους γενηθέντας πνεύματος ἁγίου metochous genēthentas pneumatos hagiou uses a key word of the letter. μέτοχος metochos means “sharer, partner, participant.” The hearers are “sharers in a heavenly calling” (3:1); the Son is anointed above his “companions” (1:9); and—crucially—“we have become sharers of Christ, if indeed we hold our original confidence firm to the end” (3:14). Believers also become “sharers” in God’s discipline (12:8). To share in the Holy Spirit is to participate in the Spirit’s presence and work in the community. The hearers had witnessed and experienced the Spirit’s activity: God bore witness to the gospel “by signs and wonders and various miracles and distributions of the Holy Spirit according to his will” (2:4). Coming just after the mention of the laying on of hands (6:2), this phrase likely includes the visible gifts of the Spirit that accompanied the gospel in the early church.

Hebrews 3:14 deserves special attention here, because the same author uses the same word for “sharers” with a perfect-tense verb: “we have become sharers of Christ, if we hold fast to the end.” The condition is not “we will become sharers if we hold fast,” but “we have already become sharers—as will be shown if we hold fast.” In the author’s own thinking, perseverance is the evidence of a reality that already exists. We will need this verse when we weigh the interpretations of 6:4–6.

Historical Background: Kadesh-barnea and the Generation That Turned Back

Hebrews has already devoted a long exposition (3:7–4:13) to the generation that came out of Egypt and failed to enter the land. The decisive moment came at Kadesh-barnea (Num 13–14). Several interpreters have noticed that the descriptions in 6:4–6 correspond closely to the experiences of that generation. They were enlightened: the pillar of fire was given “to give them light on the way” (Exod 13:21; Neh 9:12). They tasted the heavenly gift: they ate bread from heaven (Exod 16:4). They shared in the Holy Spirit: God put his Spirit upon the seventy elders (Num 11:17, 25), and Isaiah remembers that the Lord “put his Holy Spirit in their midst” and that they “grieved his Holy Spirit” (Isa 63:10–11). They tasted the goodness of God’s word and the powers of the age to come: the spies brought back the fruit of the land and reported that it was “exceedingly good” (Num 13:23–27; 14:7), and the people had seen God’s mighty signs.

Yet at Kadesh they refused to go up. The Lord’s verdict is recorded in Numbers 14:22:

Hebrew: כִּי כָל־הָאֲנָשִׁים הָרֹאִים אֶת־כְּבֹדִי וְאֶת־אֹתֹתַי אֲשֶׁר־עָשִׂיתִי בְמִצְרַיִם וּבַמִּדְבָּר וַיְנַסּוּ אֹתִי זֶה עֶשֶׂר פְּעָמִים וְלֹא שָׁמְעוּ בְּקוֹלִי — “For all the men who have seen my glory and my signs that I did in Egypt and in the wilderness, and yet have tested me these ten times (vaynassu oti zeh eser peamim) and have not listened to my voice …” The next verse completes the oath: they “shall not see the land that I swore to give to their fathers” (Num 14:23).

Then comes the detail that most closely illuminates “it is impossible to renew them again to repentance.” The next morning, the people changed their minds. “Here we are; we will go up to the place that the Lord has promised, for we have sinned” (כִּי חָטָאנוּ ki chatanu, Num 14:40). But Moses told them it would not succeed, for the Lord was not with them. They went up anyway—the Hebrew says וַיַּעְפִּלוּ vayyapilu, “they presumed” (Num 14:44)—and were struck down. Their regret was real, but the door had closed; God had sworn. The generation that had seen everything was not permitted to enter. This is the very pattern Hebrews has been pressing on the hearers since 3:7, and it stands behind the words “if God permits” (6:3) and “impossible to renew again to repentance” (6:6). Esau, who “found no place for repentance, though he sought it with tears” (12:17), will furnish a second example of the same tragedy.

6:5

and have tasted that the word of God is good, and the powers of the age to come,

The fourth description repeats the verb “taste,” γευσαμένους geusamenous, with two objects. The first is καλὸν … θεοῦ ῥῆμα kalon … theou rhēma. The adjective “good” stands first and apart from its noun, and the most natural rendering is “tasted that the word of God is good,” or “tasted the goodness of God’s word.” The phrase recalls Psalm 34:8, “Taste and see that the Lord is good,” which Peter applies to believers who have “tasted that the Lord is good” (1 Pet 2:3). It also recalls the report of Joshua and Caleb that the land was “exceedingly good” (Num 14:7) and the frequent Old Testament expression “the good word” of God’s promise (for example, Josh 21:45; 23:15, where God’s “good word” to Israel did not fail). The noun ῥῆμα rhēma, “word, utterance,” is used in Hebrews of the Son’s powerful word that upholds all things (1:3) and of God’s word that formed the ages (11:3). These people had heard God speak in the gospel and had found it sweet.

A small grammatical detail has attracted comment. In 6:4 the verb “taste” takes its object in the genitive case (“tasted of the heavenly gift”), while here it takes the accusative (“tasted the word … the powers”). Some older interpreters suggested that the genitive implies tasting a part, the accusative tasting the whole, but Greek usage does not support so precise a distinction, and most modern commentators regard the change as stylistic variation. The point is the same in both verses: real experience.

The second object is “the powers of the age to come” (δυνάμεις τε μέλλοντος αἰῶνος dynameis te mellontos aiōnos). Jewish teaching distinguished “this age” from “the age to come,” the age of the Messiah and of the resurrection (compare Matt 12:32; Eph 1:21). The New Testament announces that in Christ the age to come has broken into the present. Hebrews says that God has spoken to us “in these last days” by his Son (1:2), that Christ appeared “at the consummation of the ages” (9:26), and that the subject of the letter is “the world to come” (2:5). The “powers” (dynameis) are almost certainly the miracles that accompanied the preaching of the gospel; the same word appears in 2:4, where God bore witness “with signs and wonders and various powers (ποικίλαις δυνάμεσιν poikilais dynamesin).” The hearers had seen the future age at work in the present—healings, deliverances, the gifts of the Spirit. They had stood at the edge of the promised land and tasted its fruit.

Taken together, verses 4 and 5 present an impressive portrait. These are not outsiders who once attended a meeting. They had received the light of the gospel, experienced God’s heavenly gift, participated in the Spirit’s work, found God’s word good, and witnessed the powers of the coming age. Every interpretation of this passage must reckon honestly with the strength of this language. The author describes people who belonged, by every outward measure and by real experience, to the community of the new covenant. It is precisely this that makes what follows so terrible.

Yet it is equally important to notice what the author does not say. He does not say that these people were “called,” “sanctified,” “justified,” or “perfected forever by a single offering” (10:14). He does not say that they had “faith” or “love,” or that God had written his law on their hearts (8:10; 10:16). He does not mention that their sins were forgiven or that they were “made sharers of Christ” in the sense of 3:14. Some interpreters, notably John Owen, have built a great deal on these silences, arguing that the author carefully chose descriptions that fall short of saving grace. Others regard this as an argument from silence that reads later theological categories into a rhetorical list. We need not decide yet. It is enough to see that the author’s language, while strong, is the language of experience and participation; whether that experience was saving is a question the author himself will address in 6:9, where he contrasts these things with “better things—things that belong to salvation.”

Pastorally, these two verses are a sobering reminder that privileges are not the same as perseverance. Israel had the cloud, the manna, the water from the rock, and the law from Sinai; “nevertheless, with most of them God was not pleased, for they were laid low in the wilderness” (1 Cor 10:5). Paul draws from that history the same lesson Hebrews draws: “let the one who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall” (1 Cor 10:12). The greater the light received, the more serious the turning from it.
