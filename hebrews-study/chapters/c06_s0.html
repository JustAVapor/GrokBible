Hebrews 6

Press On: The Anchor of the Soul

Hebrews 6 stands in the middle of a deliberate detour. At 5:10 the preacher had just announced his great theme—Jesus “designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek”—and then, before he could develop it, he stopped. “About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become sluggish in hearing” (5:11). What follows, from 5:11 to 6:20, is the longest exhortation in the sermon so far. It is not a digression in the sense of a wandering thought; it is a pastoral intervention. The author will not feed the “solid food” of chapter 7 to hearers who are drifting, so he first rouses them, warns them, comforts them, and then fastens their hope to the very place where the Melchizedek argument will take them: the inner sanctuary, “behind the curtain,” where Jesus has entered as forerunner (6:19–20). The last words of the chapter—“a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”—pick up exactly where 5:10 left off. The whole chapter is a bridge built out of warning and promise.

The chapter falls into two movements. The first (6:1–12) completes the rebuke that began at 5:11. Having told the hearers that they are acting like infants who still need milk, the author surprisingly refuses to go back over the basics: “leaving the elementary word about Christ, let us be carried on to maturity” (6:1). He lists six foundational teachings (6:1–2) that need not be laid again, and then explains why with the most solemn warning in the New Testament (6:4–8): it is impossible to renew to repentance those who have shared in the blessings of the new covenant and then have fallen away, since they are crucifying the Son of God again for themselves. An agricultural parable drives the point home: land drenched by God’s rain must bear useful crops or face the farmer’s fire (6:7–8). Yet the warning is immediately followed by one of the tenderest passages in the letter. “But concerning you, beloved, we are persuaded of better things, things that belong to salvation” (6:9). God has not forgotten their love and service (6:10); the author longs for each of them to persevere with the same earnestness to the end, imitating those who “through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:11–12).

The second movement (6:13–20) supplies the ground for that hope. Abraham is the great example of faith and patience. God made him a promise and, because he had no one greater to swear by, swore by himself (Gen 22:16–17). Abraham waited and obtained what was promised. Among human beings an oath ends all dispute; God, wishing to show the heirs of the promise how unchangeable his purpose is, added his oath to his word, so that by these two unchangeable things—in which it is impossible for God to lie—those who have fled to him for refuge might have strong encouragement. That hope is “an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast,” which reaches into the heavenly Holy of Holies where Jesus has gone ahead of us.

The two movements must be read together. The same word “impossible” (ἀδύνατον adynaton) governs both halves: it is impossible to renew the apostate (6:4–6), and it is impossible for God to lie (6:18). The first “impossible” closes every back door; the second bars every door against fear. The preacher holds them in tension on purpose. He wants his hearers to feel the full weight of what it would mean to turn away from Christ, and he wants them to rest the full weight of their souls on God’s oath. Neither truth is allowed to swallow the other.

Several words recur and repay attention. Maturity (τελειότης teleiotēs, 6:1) continues the “mature” of 5:14 and the larger theme of “perfection” that runs through the book. Sluggish (νωθρός nōthros) brackets the whole exhortation (5:11; 6:12). Promise (ἐπαγγελία epangelia) appears four times in 6:12–17 and links the passage to the rest promised in chapter 4 and to the heroes of chapter 11. Hope (ἐλπίς elpis) runs from 6:11 through 6:18–19. Once (ἅπαξ hapax, 6:4) introduces a word that will later describe Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (9:26–28). And the language of the sanctuary—“behind the curtain,” “forerunner,” “high priest”—prepares for the great central exposition of chapters 7–10.

Readers should watch for three things. First, notice how carefully the author distinguishes between his warning and his diagnosis: he speaks “in this way” (6:9) precisely because he believes better things of them. Second, notice that his remedy for sluggishness is not a review of the basics but more of Christ—deeper teaching about Jesus’ priesthood. Third, notice that the chapter’s final picture is not of a struggling believer clinging to God, but of God’s oath and Christ’s entry holding the believer fast. The passage on falling away has troubled many tender consciences and been argued over for centuries; we will give it the careful attention it deserves, weighing the major interpretations by the Greek text, by the flow of the author’s argument, and by the whole testimony of Scripture.
