13:7

Remember your leaders, who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.

The second paragraph opens with another call to “remember” (μνημονεύετε mnēmoneuete). In 13:3 the readers were to remember the prisoners. Now they are to remember their leaders. The word is τῶν ἡγουμένων ὑμῶν tōn hēgoumenōn hymōn, “your leaders,” a participle of ἡγέομαι hēgeomai, “to lead, to guide.” Hebrews uses this general term rather than the more specific “elders” or “overseers.” It appears three times in this chapter (13:7, 17, 24) and nowhere else in Hebrews. Luke uses it of Judas and Silas, “leading men among the brothers” (Acts 15:22). Jesus used it when he said that among his disciples “the leader” must be “as one who serves” (Luke 22:26). Later, the Roman church used the same word for its leaders (1 Clem 1:3), which fits the likelihood that Hebrews was sent to Rome (see on 13:24).

The leaders in this verse belong to the past. The aorist ἐλάλησαν elalēsan, “they spoke,” looks back on a completed ministry, and the call to consider “the outcome” of their lives shows that their lives have ended. They are probably the founders of the congregation, the first preachers who brought the gospel to the readers. In 2:3–4 the author spoke of the salvation “confirmed to us by those who heard” the Lord. These may be the same people or their associates. Their defining work was that they “spoke to you the word of God.” This is the theme with which Hebrews opened: “God … has spoken to us in his Son” (1:1–2). The same God continued to speak through faithful messengers. The word of God that is “living and active” (4:12) reached the readers through human lips.

The readers are to do two things. First, ἀναθεωροῦντες τὴν ἔκβασιν τῆς ἀναστροφῆς anatheōrountes tēn ekbasin tēs anastrophēs, “considering the outcome of their way of life.” The verb anatheōreō means “look carefully, observe again and again.” ἔκβασις ekbasis means literally “a way out,” and so “end, result, outcome.” Paul uses it of God’s providing “the way out” in temptation (1 Cor 10:13). ἀναστροφή anastrophē means “conduct, manner of life,” a favorite word of Peter (1 Pet 1:15, 18; 2:12; 3:1–2, 16). The readers are to look at how these leaders’ lives ended.

Interpretive Options: What Was the “Outcome”?

  • Martyrdom. Some interpreters think the leaders died for their faith, so that the “outcome” of their lives was a martyr’s death. This would give the call to imitate their faith special force. If the letter went to Rome, some have thought of Peter and Paul.
  • A faithful death, by whatever means. Others think the point is simply that the leaders persevered to the end and died in faith, whether by violence or by natural causes. Their lives ended well.

The text does not say that the leaders were killed, and 12:4 (“you have not yet resisted to the point of blood”) shows that the congregation itself had not yet lost members to execution. That does not rule out martyrdom for leaders who died elsewhere, but it advises caution. The emphasis of the verse is on the faith that carried them to the end, not on how they died. The best parallel is 11:13: “These all died in faith, not having received the promises.” The leaders join the “great cloud of witnesses” (12:1), and like the heroes of chapter 11 they demonstrate that faith endures. The application to Peter and Paul is possible, but it cannot be proven.

Second, μιμεῖσθε τὴν πίστιν mimeisthe tēn pistin, “imitate their faith.” The readers are to imitate their faith, not their personal style or every detail of their lives. Earlier the author urged them to be “imitators (μιμηταί mimētai) of those who through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12). Chapter 11 provided the great examples from Israel’s history. Here the examples are closer to home: people they knew, who taught them and lived among them. Faith is learned partly by watching people who have it. Paul could say, “Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ” (1 Cor 11:1), and Peter called elders to be “examples to the flock” (1 Pet 5:3). Leaders who are now gone still serve the church through the memory of their faithfulness.

The next verse explains why this memory is not mere nostalgia. The leaders have died, but the Christ they preached has not changed.

13:8

Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever.

The verse stands without any connecting word, like a confession or a line of a hymn. It may have been familiar to the readers from worship. The Greek order is striking: Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς ἐχθὲς καὶ σήμερον ὁ αὐτός, καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας Iēsous Christos echthes kai sēmeron ho autos, kai eis tous aiōnas, “Jesus Christ yesterday and today the same, and unto the ages.” There is no verb. The full double name, “Jesus Christ,” appears only three times in Hebrews (10:10; 13:8; 13:21). It brings together the human name (Jesus, the one who suffered, 2:9) and the messianic title (Christ, the anointed King and Priest, 5:5). The one who lived, suffered, and died as a man is the same one who reigns now and will reign forever.

The verse connects backward and forward. Backward: the leaders who preached to them have died (13:7), but the Christ they preached remains. Their faith is worth imitating because its object never changes. Forward: because Christ is the same, the readers must not be carried away by “various and strange teachings” (13:9). A changing message would imply a changing Christ. Doctrine must not shift because its subject does not.

Word Study: ὁ αὐτός ho autos, “the same”

With the article, αὐτός autos means “the same, the identical one.” Hebrews used the same expression in chapter 1, in one of the most important quotations in the book. Applying Psalm 102 to the Son, the author wrote: “They will perish, but you remain … they will be changed, but you are the same (σὺ δὲ ὁ αὐτὸς εἶ sy de ho autos ei), and your years will not fail” (1:11–12). In the psalm these words are addressed to the God of Israel, the creator who laid the foundations of the earth. Hebrews applies them directly to the Son. The Hebrew of Psalm 102 reads וְאַתָּה־הוּא וּשְׁנוֹתֶיךָ לֹא יִתָּמּוּ ve-attah-hu u-shenotekha lo yittammu, “But you are he, and your years will not come to an end” (Ps 102:28 Hebrew; 102:27 English). The phrase attah hu, “you are he,” is close to the way God speaks of himself in Isaiah: “I am he” (Isa 41:4; 43:10; 46:4). It expresses his eternal, unchanging identity. In Malachi, God says: כִּי אֲנִי יְהוָה לֹא שָׁנִיתִי ki ani YHWH lo shaniti, “For I the Lord do not change” (Mal 3:6).

So Hebrews 13:8 is more than a statement that Jesus is reliable. It echoes the words with which the sermon began, ascribing to the Son the unchangeable being of God. Chapter 1 began with the Son who laid the foundations of the earth and outlasts all creation. The chapter that closes the sermon returns to the same truth: the Son is “the same.”

The three time words fit the argument of Hebrews.

  • “Yesterday” (ἐχθές echthes). This points to the past, especially the past in which the Son “in the days of his flesh” offered prayers with loud cries and tears (5:7), endured the cross (12:2), and was preached by the readers’ first leaders (13:7). It may also recall his eternal past. He is the one through whom God made the ages (1:2), whose life has “neither beginning of days nor end of life” (7:3).
  • “Today” (σήμερον sēmeron). This word carries special weight in Hebrews. It is the “today” of Psalm 95, the day of God’s voice and the day of decision (3:7, 13, 15; 4:7): “exhort one another every day, as long as it is called ‘Today’” (3:13). It is also the “today” of Psalm 2:7, “You are my Son; today I have begotten you” (1:5; 5:5). The Christ who speaks “today” is the same Christ who acted “yesterday.” He now “appears in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24).
  • “Forever” (εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας eis tous aiōnas). This echoes the repeated “forever” of his priesthood: “You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek” (5:6; 6:20; 7:17, 21). “He remains forever” (7:24). He “always lives to make intercession” (7:25). The same phrase closes the doxology of 13:21.

In what sense is Christ “the same”? The Scriptures show that the Son truly entered history and that in his human nature he experienced change. He was “made for a little while lower than the angels” (2:9). He “learned obedience through what he suffered” (5:8). He was “made perfect” (5:9) and then exalted to God’s right hand (1:3). Hebrews 13:8 does not deny any of this. Its point is that through every stage of his work he remains the same person, with the same divine identity, the same faithfulness, the same saving purpose, and the same sympathy with his people. The incarnation did not make him someone else. The cross and the exaltation did not change who he is. As man he grew and suffered. As the eternal Son he is the One who said “Here I am, I have come to do your will” (10:7), and who will “appear a second time … to save those who are eagerly waiting for him” (9:28). Calvin reads the verse chiefly as a statement about Christ as his people have known him in every age, the constant foundation of the church, rather than as an abstract proof of his eternity. That reading rightly keeps the context in view. But the echo of 1:12 shows that the constancy of Christ rests on his being.

This truth also ties together the generations of God’s people. The first readers’ leaders trusted Jesus Christ; the readers trust the same Jesus Christ; believers today trust him still. The heroes of chapter 11, who “greeted the promises from afar” (11:13), were looking toward him. Moses bore “the reproach of Christ” (11:26). The church in every century does not stand on the changing words of its teachers, however faithful. It stands on the unchanging Christ whom they preached.

For the readers this was deeply practical. Their leaders had died. Their community was under pressure. New teachings were attractive. The old system of temple and priesthood looked solid and lasting. To all of this the author says: everything else may shift, but Jesus Christ does not. Earthly priests “were prevented by death from continuing” (7:23), but he continues. Earthly cities do not last (13:14), but he does. Earthly heavens and earth will be rolled up like a garment (1:12), but he is the same.

13:9

Do not be carried away by various and strange teachings; for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods, which have not benefited those who walk in them.

Because Christ does not change, the readers must not be moved. διδαχαῖς ποικίλαις καὶ ξέναις μὴ παραφέρεσθε didachais poikilais kai xenais mē parapheresthe, “by teachings various and strange do not be carried away.” The verb παραφέρω parapherō means “carry aside, sweep away.” Jude uses it of clouds “carried along by winds” (Jude 12). The picture is like that of 2:1, where the readers were warned not to “drift away” like a ship carried past its harbor. Paul uses similar language of believers “tossed to and fro and carried about by every wind of teaching” (Eph 4:14). A heart that is not anchored is easily carried off. Hebrews has offered an anchor: the hope “sure and steadfast” that enters behind the curtain where Jesus has gone (6:19–20).

The teachings are described in two ways. ποικίλος poikilos means “many-colored, varied, diverse.” False teaching takes many forms, while the truth about Christ is one and the same. ξένος xenos means “strange, foreign.” This is a deliberate echo of 13:2. The readers are to welcome strangers (philoxenia) but not strange teachings (xenai). The open home must not become an open door for doctrines foreign to the gospel. The traveling visitor who was to be welcomed might bring with him something that should be refused (compare 2 John 10).

The positive principle follows: καλὸν γὰρ χάριτι βεβαιοῦσθαι τὴν καρδίαν, οὐ βρώμασιν kalon gar chariti bebaiousthai tēn kardian, ou brōmasin, “for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods.” βεβαιόω bebaioō means “make firm, confirm, establish.” Hebrews uses related words several times. The message about salvation “was confirmed” to us (2:3). We must hold our original confidence “firm” to the end (3:14). God’s promise is an anchor “firm” and sure (6:19). The question is what makes the inner person stable. The answer is grace, God’s free favor given through Christ. The readers are to “approach the throne of grace with boldness, to receive mercy and find grace” (4:16). They must take care that no one “fall short of the grace of God” (12:15). They are to “have grace, through which we may worship God in a way pleasing to him” (12:28). The heart is made firm by what God gives, not by what people eat.

The last clause gives a verdict on the foods: ἐν οἷς οὐκ ὠφελήθησαν οἱ περιπατοῦντες en hois ouk ōphelēthēsan hoi peripatountes, “in which those who walk have not been benefited.” To “walk” in something is a Hebrew way of speaking about conduct, as when one walks in God’s statutes. Those who made food regulations the path of their lives did not gain what they sought. The same judgment was made of the old cult in 9:9–10. Its gifts and sacrifices “cannot perfect the worshiper as to the conscience, since they deal only with foods (βρώμασιν brōmasin) and drinks and various washings, regulations for the flesh imposed until the time of reformation.” The word is the same. Hebrews 7:18 makes a similar point: the former commandment was set aside “because of its weakness and uselessness” (ἀνωφελές anōpheles, built from the same root as “benefited” here).

Interpretive Options: What “Foods” Are Meant?

  • Jewish food laws in general. On this view the “foods” are the distinctions between clean and unclean food in Leviticus 11. The readers were tempted to return to keeping these laws as a means of standing before God. In support: 9:10 lists “foods and drinks” among the regulations of the old covenant; Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19); and the early church debated food laws often (Acts 10:9–16; 15:20; Rom 14; Gal 2:11–14).
  • Meals connected with sacrifice and the altar. On this view the “foods” are meals in which participants shared in the sacrifices. Examples are the peace offerings, part of which the worshiper ate (Lev 7:11–21), and the Passover meal. The idea would be that such meals gave participants a share in the altar and its benefits. Some recent commentators suggest that Jewish communities outside the land held festive meals that strengthened the community’s connection with the temple. This view fits well with the next verse, which speaks of an “altar” and of “eating.” Paul assumes that those who eat the sacrifices are “partners in the altar” (1 Cor 10:18).
  • Pagan sacrificial meals or ascetic food rules. Some compare 1 Corinthians 8–10 (meat sacrificed to idols) or the ascetic rules of Colossians 2:16–23 and 1 Timothy 4:3. But nothing in Hebrews points toward pagan worship, and the rest of the passage is entirely about the Levitical cult.

The first two views overlap and need not be sharply separated. The context points strongly toward Jewish practice, since the following verses speak of “those who serve the tabernacle,” the high priest, and the sanctuary. The closest connection is with 13:10: “We have an altar from which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat.” The word “for” in 13:11 ties the argument together. The most likely meaning is that the readers were attracted to the ritual meals and food rules of the old covenant, especially meals that offered a sense of participation in the temple’s sacrifices, as a way of strengthening their relationship with God. The author does not deny that such practices had their place in God’s former order (9:10). He denies that they can strengthen the heart. That can be done only by grace, which comes through the one sacrifice of Christ. Whatever the exact practice was, his principle applies to every attempt to find spiritual security through religious rules about what one eats or does not eat. Paul says the same: “the kingdom of God is not food and drink, but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit” (Rom 14:17), and “food will not commend us to God” (1 Cor 8:8).

There is also an echo here of Esau, who “for a single meal (βρώσεως brōseōs) sold his birthright” (12:16). Esau traded the inheritance of the firstborn for food and later could not get it back. The readers must not trade the grace of God for food of any kind.

13:10

We have an altar from which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat.

Against the “foods” of 13:9 the author sets a bold claim: ἔχομεν θυσιαστήριον echomen thysiastērion, “We have an altar.” The verb comes first. Hebrews often uses “we have” to sum up what believers possess in Christ. “We have such a high priest” (8:1). “We have this hope as an anchor of the soul” (6:19). “Having a great high priest” (4:14). “Having boldness to enter the sanctuary … and having a great priest over the house of God” (10:19, 21). The readers might feel that by leaving the temple’s rituals behind they had lost something solid: priests, altar, sacrifices, sacred meals. The author answers that they have lost nothing. “We too have an altar,” and a better one.

θυσιαστήριον thysiastērion, “altar,” literally means “place of sacrifice.” Hebrews used the word once before, of the tribe of Judah, from which “no one has ever served at the altar” (7:13). Jesus came from that tribe. On the old covenant’s terms, he had no right to serve at the altar. Now the author says that believers have an altar from which the old covenant’s priests have no right to eat. The roles have been reversed.

The second half of the verse is ἐξ οὗ φαγεῖν οὐκ ἔχουσιν ἐξουσίαν οἱ τῇ σκηνῇ λατρεύοντες ex hou phagein ouk echousin exousian hoi tē skēnē latreuontes, “from which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat.” ἐξουσία exousia means “right, authority.” “Those who serve the tabernacle” could mean the Levitical priests. More broadly, it could mean all who worship according to the old covenant system, since the verb λατρεύω latreuō is used in Hebrews of worshipers generally (9:9; 10:2) as well as of priests (8:5). Hebrews speaks of the “tabernacle” rather than the temple, because its argument is built on the Mosaic pattern described in the Scriptures (8:5; 9:1–10).

Historical Background: Eating from the Altar

Under the law of Moses, many sacrifices were partly eaten. In the peace offerings, the fat was burned on the altar, a portion went to the priest, and the worshiper and his family ate the rest in a joyful meal before the Lord (Lev 7:11–36; Deut 12:7). The priests lived from the portions of the sacrifices assigned to them. “Those who serve at the altar share in the sacrificial offerings,” Paul notes (1 Cor 9:13). Even an ordinary sin offering was eaten by the priests. “The priest who offers it for sin shall eat it; in a holy place it shall be eaten, in the court of the tent of meeting” (Lev 6:26; Hebrew 6:19).

There was one important exception. When the blood of a sin offering was brought inside the sanctuary, no one could eat its flesh: וְכָל־חַטָּאת אֲשֶׁר יוּבָא מִדָּמָהּ אֶל־אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד לְכַפֵּר בַּקֹּדֶשׁ לֹא תֵאָכֵל בָּאֵשׁ תִּשָּׂרֵף — ve-khol-chatta’t asher yuva middamah el-ohel mo‘ed lekhapper baqqodesh lo te’akhel; ba’esh tissaref, “But any sin offering from which blood is brought into the tent of meeting to make atonement in the holy place shall not be eaten; it shall be burned with fire” (Lev 6:30; Hebrew 6:23). The chief examples were the sin offerings of the Day of Atonement, whose blood the high priest carried into the Most Holy Place (Lev 16:14–15, 27). On the holiest day of Israel’s year, the most important sacrifice could not be eaten by anyone, not even the high priest. That is the background of 13:11, which follows directly.

What is the altar from which believers eat?

Interpretive Options: “We Have an Altar”

  • The Lord’s Supper or Communion table. Many Roman Catholic interpreters, and some others, see here a reference to the Eucharist. On this reading Christians have a sacred meal (the Supper), which those who still worship by the old covenant may not share. Some take this further, understanding the Supper as a sacrifice offered on an altar by priests.
  • The cross, or Christ’s sacrifice itself. Most Reformation and modern Protestant commentators, including Calvin, Owen, Bruce, and Hughes, understand the altar as the sacrifice of Christ, offered once for all on the cross. To “eat” from this altar means to receive by faith the benefits of his sacrifice: forgiveness, cleansing, and access to God. Some prefer to say that the altar is Christ himself.
  • The heavenly altar. Others connect it to the heavenly sanctuary where Christ ministers (8:1–2; 9:24), seeing the altar as part of the true tabernacle in heaven.

The context of Hebrews strongly favors the second view.

First, the verse that follows explains the altar by the Day of Atonement sacrifice (13:11, “for”). It then says that Jesus “suffered outside the gate” to sanctify the people “through his own blood” (13:12). The altar is identified by the sacrifice offered on it, and the sacrifice is Jesus’ death.

Second, Hebrews insists that Christ’s sacrifice was offered “once for all” (7:27; 9:12, 26, 28; 10:10). “Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin” (10:18). After offering “one sacrifice for sins for all time,” Christ “sat down” (10:12). The repeated standing of the priests at the altar is contrasted with his finished work (10:11–12). An altar on which Christ is offered again, or re-presented as a sacrifice by a Christian priesthood, would contradict the central argument of the book. The only sacrifices Hebrews gives Christians to offer are praise and doing good (13:15–16).

Third, the contrast is with “foods” that do not benefit (13:9). It would be strange for the author to warn against seeking strength in sacred foods and then point the readers to a different sacred food as the source of their strength. The point is that the heart is strengthened “by grace,” that is, by Christ’s sacrifice received in faith.

Fourth, Hebrews never mentions the Lord’s Supper directly. Some see an allusion in 6:4 (“tasted the heavenly gift”) or in 9:20 (“the blood of the covenant,” compare Mark 14:24). If so, these allusions are indirect, and they do not show that the author has the Supper in view here.

None of this lessens the Lord’s Supper. Scripture teaches that in the Supper believers have a real “participation in the blood of Christ” and “in the body of Christ” (1 Cor 10:16). The Supper is the visible sign by which the church proclaims and receives the benefits of Christ’s death (1 Cor 11:26). But what it signifies and gives is a share in the one sacrifice of the cross. The table is not a second altar. It is the place where the church remembers and receives the fruit of the one altar. The heavenly view is not far from this either, since the sacrifice offered on earth is presented in the heavenly sanctuary (9:24). The altar of 13:10 is best understood as Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice, from which believers “eat” by faith.

Why do “those who serve the tabernacle” have no right to eat from it? On one level, those who continue to rely on the old system, as though Christ had not come, cut themselves off from his sacrifice (compare 10:26–29; Gal 5:2–4). But the next verse shows that the author also has a typological point. Under the law itself, no one could eat the flesh of the Day of Atonement sacrifice. The old system itself excluded its own worshipers from the greatest of its offerings. It had nothing to “eat” from its highest act of atonement. That was the limit of the old covenant. The new covenant has no such limit. Believers now feed by faith on the true atoning sacrifice, which the priests of the old order could not touch. Jesus spoke of this in his own way: “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you … whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst” (John 6:53, 35).

13:11

For the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the sanctuary by the high priest as an offering for sin are burned outside the camp.

The “for” (γάρ gar) explains 13:10. The author returns one last time to the Day of Atonement, the ceremony he expounded in chapter 9. ὧν γὰρ εἰσφέρεται ζῴων τὸ αἷμα περὶ ἁμαρτίας εἰς τὰ ἅγια διὰ τοῦ ἀρχιερέως, τούτων τὰ σώματα κατακαίεται ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς hōn gar eispheretai zōōn to haima peri hamartias eis ta hagia dia tou archiereōs, toutōn ta sōmata katakaietai exō tēs parembolēs, “for of those animals whose blood is brought in for sin into the sanctuary through the high priest, of these the bodies are burned outside the camp.”

Several details matter. περὶ ἁμαρτίας peri hamartias, “for sin,” is the standard Greek phrase for the sin offering. Hebrews used it in quoting Psalm 40 (10:6, 8) and in saying that there is “no longer any offering for sin” (10:18). τὰ ἅγια ta hagia, “the sanctuary,” here means the Most Holy Place, as in 9:12, 24–25. The verbs are in the present tense: “is brought,” “are burned.” The author describes the ritual as prescribed in the law and, it seems, still practiced in his day. This is one of several signs that the temple was still standing when he wrote.

Old Testament Text: Leviticus 16:27

Hebrew: וְאֵת פַּר הַחַטָּאת וְאֵת שְׂעִיר הַחַטָּאת אֲשֶׁר הוּבָא אֶת־דָּמָם לְכַפֵּר בַּקֹּדֶשׁ יוֹצִיא אֶל־מִחוּץ לַמַּחֲנֶה וְשָׂרְפוּ בָאֵשׁ אֶת־עֹרֹתָם וְאֶת־בְּשָׂרָם וְאֶת־פִּרְשָׁם — ve’et par hachatta’t ve’et se‘ir hachatta’t asher huva et-damam lekhapper baqqodesh yotsi el-michuts lammachaneh, ve-sarfu va’esh et-‘orotam ve’et-besaram ve’et-pirsham, “And the bull of the sin offering and the goat of the sin offering, whose blood was brought in to make atonement in the holy place, shall be carried out to the outside of the camp; and they shall burn with fire their skins and their flesh and their dung.”

The phrase מִחוּץ לַמַּחֲנֶה michuts lammachaneh, “outside the camp,” is exactly what Hebrews renders as ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς exō tēs parembolēs. The same rule applied to the sin offering for the high priest and for the whole congregation on other occasions (Lev 4:11–12, 21). It was also applied at the ordination of the priests (Exod 29:14). Hebrews speaks only of the bodies of the animals. The Hebrew details (skin, flesh, dung) make clear that the whole carcass was destroyed. It was not eaten by anyone.

In the wilderness, the “camp” (מַחֲנֶה machaneh; Greek παρεμβολή parembolē) was Israel’s encampment around the tabernacle (Num 2). It was the community of God’s people, the area of ritual cleanness, with the sanctuary at its center. Outside the camp was the place of the unclean. People with skin diseases lived there (Lev 13:46; Num 5:2–4). Ashes from the altar were carried there (Lev 4:12; 6:11). Executions took place there: the blasphemer (Lev 24:14) and the Sabbath-breaker (Num 15:35–36) were stoned outside the camp. The red heifer whose ashes made the water of purification was slaughtered there (Num 19:3, 9; compare Heb 9:13). Once Israel was settled, “the camp” came to correspond to Jerusalem and its holy area. In the Second Temple period, the remains of the Day of Atonement sacrifices were carried out of the city and burned at a designated place outside it.

So the sin offering of the Day of Atonement had a double destiny. Its blood went into the very presence of God in the Most Holy Place. Its body went outside the camp, to the place of uncleanness, and was burned. The holiest sacrifice of Israel’s year ended up in the place furthest from holiness. The next verse shows how this pattern was fulfilled in Jesus.

13:12

Therefore Jesus also, in order to sanctify the people through his own blood, suffered outside the gate.

διὸ καὶ Ἰησοῦς dio kai Iēsous, “therefore Jesus also.” The “therefore” presents Jesus’ death as the fulfillment of the pattern just described. The Day of Atonement sacrifice had two parts: blood inside, body outside. Hebrews 9 showed Jesus entering the true sanctuary “through his own blood” (9:12). Now 13:12 shows the other part. His body suffered “outside the gate.” He fulfilled the sin offering completely. His blood was presented in heaven, and his body was put to death outside the holy city as one rejected and unclean.

The purpose was ἵνα ἁγιάσῃ διὰ τοῦ ἰδίου αἵματος τὸν λαόν hina hagiasē dia tou idiou haimatos ton laon, “in order to sanctify the people through his own blood.” Each word echoes the earlier argument. ἁγιάζω hagiazō, “sanctify, make holy,” describes what Christ’s sacrifice does: it cleanses and sets apart God’s people for him, so that they may come near. “By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10). “By a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14). “He who sanctifies and those who are sanctified are all from one” (2:11). διὰ τοῦ ἰδίου αἵματος dia tou idiou haimatos, “through his own blood,” repeats 9:12 exactly. Unlike the high priest, who entered “with blood not his own” (9:25), Jesus offered himself. τὸν λαόν ton laon, “the people,” is the covenant people, those for whom he “made propitiation” (2:17). The paradox is clear. The one who was cast out as unclean is the one who makes his people holy.

ἔξω τῆς πύλης ἔπαθεν exō tēs pylēs epathen, “outside the gate he suffered.” The author changes “camp” to “gate.” In the wilderness it was a camp; in Jerusalem it was a city with gates. ἔπαθεν epathen, “he suffered,” is Hebrews’ characteristic way of describing Christ’s death. He was crowned with glory “because of the suffering of death” (2:9). He “suffered when tempted” (2:18). He “learned obedience through what he suffered” (5:8). He would have had to “suffer repeatedly” if his sacrifice were like the old ones (9:26).

Historical Background: Crucifixion Outside the Gate

The Gospels agree that Jesus was crucified outside Jerusalem. John says that Jesus went out carrying his own cross to the place called Golgotha, and that “the place where Jesus was crucified was near the city” (John 19:17, 20). Matthew and Mark describe the soldiers leading him out, and Simon of Cyrene being forced to carry the cross as they went out (Matt 27:31–32; Mark 15:20–21). Both Roman and Jewish practice placed executions outside the city. The Romans crucified people along busy roads near city walls, so that the punishment would be seen and would deter others. The law of Moses required that executions take place outside the camp (Lev 24:14; Num 15:35–36). Naboth was taken outside the city to be stoned (1 Kgs 21:13), and so was Stephen (Acts 7:58). The traditional site of Golgotha, now under the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, lay just outside the city wall in Jesus’ day. It was enclosed within the city only when a new wall was built some years later, under Herod Agrippa I in the early 40s AD.

The location was a matter of shame as well as geography. To be taken outside the city to die was to be cut off from the community, treated as accursed. “Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree” (Gal 3:13, quoting Deut 21:23). Jesus himself told a parable about tenants who took the owner’s son, “threw him out of the vineyard, and killed him” (Matt 21:39).

The fact that Jesus died outside the gate was not an accident of Roman custom. It was the fulfillment of the Day of Atonement pattern. The sin offering whose blood atoned in the sanctuary was burned outside the camp. In the same way Jesus, whose blood opened the way into the presence of God, was put out of the city to die. In the eyes of Jerusalem’s religious leaders, he was rejected and unclean. In God’s purpose, he was the sin offering that sanctifies the people. The place of rejection became the place of sanctification. This turns the old order upside down. Holiness is no longer found by staying inside the sacred precinct. It is found with Jesus, outside.
