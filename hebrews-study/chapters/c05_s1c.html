5:8

Although he was a Son, he learned obedience from the things he suffered;

This short verse is one of the most profound statements about Christ in the New Testament. It is also one of the most easily misunderstood.

Although he was a Son. The concessive conjunction καίπερ kaiper, “although, even though,” signals that what follows is surprising. The participle ὤν ōn, “being,” is present tense: he was, and continued to be, the Son all through his suffering. Notice that “Son” (υἱός huios) has no article in Greek. The author used the same style in 1:2, where God “has spoken to us in a Son” (ἐν υἱῷ en huiō). This stresses the quality of the person. He is one who is Son in the unique sense described in chapter 1: heir of all things, agent of creation, the radiance of God’s glory, the exact imprint of his nature (1:2–3). That such a one should have to learn anything, much less learn through suffering, is astonishing. The ordinary assumption would be that a son of that rank is exempt from hardship. Royal sons were pampered, not tested. But this Son was not exempt.

There is a contrast here with chapter 12. There the author tells his readers that God disciplines them because they are sons: “It is for discipline that you endure. God is treating you as sons. For what son is there whom a father does not discipline?” (12:7). In 5:8 Christ learns through suffering although he is the Son. He did not need correction. Yet he walked the path of suffering that his brothers and sisters must walk. In doing so he blazed the trail they follow. He is the “pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2).

Word Study: ἔμαθεν ἀφ’ ὧν ἔπαθεν emathen aph’ hōn epathen

The Greek contains a memorable play on sounds. ἔμαθεν emathen, “he learned” (from μανθάνω manthanō), and ἔπαθεν epathen, “he suffered” (from πάσχω paschō), differ by a single letter. The jingle was proverbial in Greek literature. The playwright Aeschylus speaks of Zeus establishing the law of “learning by suffering” (πάθει μάθος pathei mathos, Agamemnon 177). The historian Herodotus has the defeated king Croesus say that his sufferings had become his lessons (Histories 1.207). An English reader might capture it with “he learned through what he endured,” or “his pains were his teachers.”

In Greek usage, however, the proverb usually described a fool or a wrongdoer who learns his lesson the hard way, through punishment. The author takes this common saying and transforms it. Christ did not learn by being punished for folly. He learned obedience, the willing submission of his will to the Father’s, by experiencing what that obedience cost.

He learned obedience. What does it mean that the sinless Son “learned obedience”? It cannot mean that he was once disobedient and gradually learned to stop disobeying. Hebrews insists that he was “without sin” (4:15), “holy, innocent, undefiled” (7:26), and that he offered himself “without blemish” to God (9:14). At every moment of his life Jesus did the Father’s will perfectly. His own testimony was, “I always do the things that are pleasing to him” (John 8:29).

Rather, the verse speaks of learning by experience. There is a difference between knowing what obedience is and having actually obeyed under pressure, in pain, at great cost. A soldier may know his duty perfectly, but he learns what courage is only in battle. In the same way, the Son entered into the actual experience of obeying God as a human being, in a fallen world, under temptation and suffering, all the way to death. Each new trial called for obedience at a deeper level, and each time he gave it. Some interpreters suggest that the article in Greek, τὴν ὑπακοήν tēn hypakoēn, “the obedience,” may point to the particular obedience his mission required, the obedience that took him to the cross. Paul describes it in similar terms: “he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross” (Phil 2:8). “Through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous” (Rom 5:19).

The Gospels confirm that Jesus truly grew as a human being. As a boy he “increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man” (Luke 2:52). As the eternal Son, sharing the divine nature, he needed to learn nothing. But in the nature he took on, a true human mind and will, he learned, grew, and was tested. We must not divide the one person of Christ. Yet we must also not deny the reality of his humanity, which Hebrews stresses more strongly than any other New Testament book (2:14–18; 4:15; 5:7–8).

Old Testament Background: The Servant Who Was Taught

The third Servant Song of Isaiah gives a striking Old Testament picture of a servant who learns obedience through suffering: אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה פָּתַח־לִי אֹזֶן וְאָנֹכִי לֹא מָרִיתִי אָחוֹר לֹא נְסוּגֹתִי ’Adonay YHWH patach-li ’ozen, ve’anokhi lo’ mariti, ’achor lo’ nesugoti, “The Lord God has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious; I did not turn backward” (Isa 50:5). The previous verse says that morning by morning God awakens his ear “to hear as those who are taught” (כַּלִּמּוּדִים kallimmudim, Isa 50:4). The next verse shows where that obedience led: “I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I did not hide my face from disgrace and spitting” (Isa 50:6). Here is a servant who is taught, who listens, who does not turn back, and whose obedience leads straight into suffering. The Servant’s “open ear” is the very opposite of the “sluggish hearing” the author is about to rebuke in his readers (Heb 5:11).

Why this matters. The author has a pastoral purpose. His readers were suffering (10:32–34; 12:4–11), and they were tempted to interpret their suffering as a sign that something had gone wrong, that God had abandoned them, or that following Jesus was not worth the cost. The author shows them that suffering was the very path the Son himself walked. Suffering did not contradict his sonship. It was the arena in which his sonship was proved and his obedience perfected. If that was true of him, it will be true of them. And it means that the one to whom they pray knows from experience what obedience under suffering demands. When we struggle to obey God in hard circumstances, we come to a high priest who has done exactly that, perfectly, and who is able to help those who are being tested (2:18).

5:9

and having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him,

Now the long sentence reaches its goal. The Son who prayed, suffered, and learned obedience has been “made perfect,” and he has “become” the source of eternal salvation. This verse matches 5:1 in the chiastic pattern. The high priest was appointed to deal with sins on behalf of men. Christ has done that, and the result is not a temporary covering but eternal salvation.

Word Study: τελειόω teleioō

The verb means “to bring to completion, to bring to the goal, to make perfect.” It is built on τέλος telos, “end, goal.” Hebrews uses it nine times. Three times it is used of Christ: God made the pioneer of salvation “perfect through sufferings” (2:10); Christ, “having been made perfect,” became the source of salvation (5:9); the oath appoints “a Son made perfect forever” (7:28). Elsewhere it is used of what the law could not do (7:19; 9:9; 10:1) and of what Christ’s sacrifice has done for believers (10:14; 11:40; 12:23).

When used of Christ, “made perfect” cannot mean moral improvement, as if he were once flawed and then corrected. He was always sinless (4:15; 7:26). Instead the word speaks of vocational perfection, being brought to full qualification for the office he was to hold. A newly appointed doctor who has finished her training is “perfected” as a physician, not because she was morally deficient before, but because she is now fully equipped for her calling. Christ was made perfect as Savior and high priest. That came about through the whole course of his obedience, suffering, death, resurrection, and exaltation. The aorist participle τελειωθείς teleiōtheis, “having been made perfect,” views this process as complete. The passive voice implies that God is the one who perfected him (as in 2:10).

Old Testament Background: “Filling the Hand”

There may be a further, priestly nuance in the word. In the Law of Moses the ordination of a priest is called “filling the hand.” God commanded Moses concerning Aaron and his sons: וְהָיְתָה לָהֶם כְּהֻנָּה לְחֻקַּת עוֹלָם וּמִלֵּאתָ יַד־אַהֲרֹן וְיַד־בָּנָיו vehayetah lahem kehunnah lechuqqat ‘olam umille’ta yad-Aharon veyad-banav, “and the priesthood shall be theirs by a perpetual statute; and you shall fill the hand of Aaron and the hand of his sons” (Exod 29:9). The seven-day ordination ceremony was called מִלֻּאִים millu’im, “fillings” (Lev 8:33). The high priest who served on the Day of Atonement was the one “whom they anoint and whose hand they fill to serve as priest in his father’s place” (Lev 16:32).

The Greek Old Testament regularly translated “fill the hand” with the verb teleioō: literally, “perfect the hands” (for example, Exod 29:9; Lev 16:32). Readers who knew their Greek Bible would therefore hear in “made perfect” an echo of priestly consecration. Christ was “perfected,” that is, consecrated and installed as high priest, through his suffering. This nuance should not be pressed too far, since Hebrews uses the verb more broadly, and never with the word “hands.” But it fits the context well. The very next verse speaks of Christ’s designation as high priest.

He became the source of eternal salvation. The verb ἐγένετο egeneto, “he became,” indicates a new status entered at a point in time. The Son was always the eternal Son. But through his suffering and exaltation he became something he had not been before: the one through whom eternal salvation actually flows to sinners.

The word αἴτιος aitios occurs only here in the New Testament. It means “the one responsible for, the cause of, the source of.” In Hellenistic Greek the phrase “cause of salvation” was used of people who rescued others from danger, such as a general who saved a city or a benefactor who delivered a community. The word presents Christ as the one who brings salvation about. He is not just a channel or example of salvation. He is its cause and author. Compare 2:10, where he is “the pioneer of their salvation,” and 12:2, “the pioneer and perfecter of faith.”

Eternal salvation. The adjective αἰώνιος aiōnios, “eternal,” is a favorite word in Hebrews. Christ obtained “eternal redemption” (9:12), offered himself “through the eternal Spirit” (9:14), so that those called might receive “the promise of the eternal inheritance” (9:15). His blood is “the blood of the eternal covenant” (13:20). The contrast is with the Levitical priests, whose offerings had to be repeated year after year because they could never make the worshipers perfect (10:1–4). Christ’s salvation does not wear out. It belongs to the age to come and lasts forever, because the one who secured it is “a priest forever” (5:6) and “always lives to intercede” (7:25).

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 45:17

Hebrew: יִשְׂרָאֵל נוֹשַׁע בַּיהוָה תְּשׁוּעַת עוֹלָמִים לֹא־תֵבֹשׁוּ וְלֹא־תִכָּלְמוּ עַד־עוֹלְמֵי עַד — Yisra’el nosha‘ baYHWH teshu‘at ‘olamim; lo’-tevoshu velo’-tikkalemu ‘ad-‘olmey ‘ad, “Israel is saved by the Lord with an everlasting salvation; you shall not be put to shame or disgraced to the ages of eternity.”

This is the Old Testament passage that most closely anticipates the phrase “eternal salvation.” The Greek translation of Isaiah renders teshu‘at ‘olamim with the same words Hebrews uses here. Isaiah promised that the Lord himself would save his people with a salvation that would never end and never leave them ashamed. Hebrews announces that this promise has been fulfilled through the Son, the high priest whom God appointed.

To all who obey him. The phrase πᾶσιν τοῖς ὑπακούουσιν αὐτῷ pasin tois hypakouousin autō uses the verb ὑπακούω hypakouō, “obey,” which is closely related to ὑπακοή hypakoē, “obedience,” in 5:8. The parallel is deliberate. Christ learned obedience to the Father. He saves those who give obedience to him. Both words are built on ἀκούω akouō, “hear.” In biblical thought, to obey is to hear in the full sense: to listen and respond. This ties the verse to the great refrain of chapters 3–4: “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts” (3:7, 15; 4:7). It also prepares for the rebuke in 5:11: “you have become sluggish in hearing.”

Does this mean salvation is earned by obedience? Not at all. The whole argument of Hebrews is that salvation is the gift of God, secured entirely by the priestly work of Christ (9:12, 14, 26–28; 10:10, 14). But Hebrews also insists that genuine faith shows itself in obedience. Indeed, the author treats unbelief and disobedience as two sides of the same failure. The wilderness generation could not enter God’s rest “because of unbelief” (3:19). In the same breath they are described as “those who were disobedient” (3:18; 4:6). Paul speaks of “the obedience of faith” (Rom 1:5; 16:26). Obedience here is not a set of works that earn salvation. It is the trusting response of those who hear and heed the Son. It is the opposite of the drifting, hardening, and drawing back that the author warns against.

The word “all” (πᾶσιν pasin) is also significant. The Levitical high priest represented one nation. Christ is the source of salvation to all who obey him, without ethnic or social distinction. Those who belong to him are defined not by descent from Abraham or by their place in the old covenant, but by their relationship to the Son.

5:10

having been designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek.

The long sentence that began in 5:7 ends with God’s formal pronouncement. The participle προσαγορευθείς prosagoreutheis is the aorist passive of προσαγορεύω prosagoreuō, a verb that occurs only here in the New Testament. It means “to address, greet, hail,” or “to call by name, designate formally.” In ordinary Greek it could describe saluting someone with a title, or officially naming someone to a position. Here it pictures God addressing the Son with his title: “High Priest according to the order of Melchizedek.” The passive voice and the phrase “by God” (ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ hypo tou theou) bring us back to 5:4. Aaron was “called by God.” So was Christ, and his calling was publicly proclaimed by God himself.

When was he designated? The aorist participle is closely tied to “having been made perfect” and “he became” in 5:9. The most natural reading is that the designation took place when the Son had completed his suffering and been exalted. God spoke the words of Psalm 110:4 to the risen and ascended Christ, just as he spoke Psalm 2:7 (5:5) and Psalm 110:1 (1:13). This is consistent with Hebrews as a whole, which locates Christ’s high-priestly ministry chiefly in heaven: “we have such a high priest, one who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens, a minister in the holy places” (8:1–2). This does not mean that Christ was not acting as priest in his death. His self-offering on the cross was the decisive priestly act (7:27; 9:14, 26). But his public installation in the office, and his entrance into the heavenly sanctuary, followed his suffering. His sacrifice was offered on earth. Its blood was presented in heaven (9:11–12, 24).

High priest. Psalm 110:4 calls the king a “priest” (ἱερεύς hiereus). Here the author says “high priest” (ἀρχιερεύς archiereus). This is not careless quotation. It is interpretation. The priest-king of Psalm 110 is the supreme priest of God Most High, with no one above him. Within the framework of Israel’s worship, which the author has been describing since 5:1, that makes him the high priest. In fact he is the “great high priest” (4:14), the one to whom all the Levitical high priests pointed.

According to the order of Melchizedek. For the second time in five verses Melchizedek is named. The repetition is intentional. The author is announcing the theme of the next major section of his sermon. He has said that a high priest must be called by God “just as Aaron was” (5:4). Now he says that Christ’s calling is “according to the order of Melchizedek.” That is a different order from Aaron’s. It is older, since Melchizedek met Abraham centuries before Levi was born. It is royal, since Melchizedek was king of Salem. And it is eternal, since the psalm says “forever.” In chapter 7 the author will show why this matters. If perfection had been possible through the Levitical priesthood, why would God have spoken of another priest arising “according to the order of Melchizedek” and not “according to the order of Aaron” (7:11)? A change of priesthood means a change of law and of covenant (7:12, 22).

But before he can expound this, the author must deal with a problem in his hearers. They are not ready for it. Verse 11 begins, “About this we have much to say.” The phrase “according to the order of Melchizedek” will hang in the air through the exhortation of 5:11–6:20, and it will be picked up again in nearly the same words at 6:20, where the exposition resumes. The reader is left with a sense of anticipation, which is precisely what the author intends. The fullest truths about Christ are still ahead, and the readers must be roused from their sluggishness to receive them.

Looking back over 5:1–10, we can see how thoroughly Christ fulfills the office of high priest. He was taken from among men, sharing our flesh and blood (2:14). He was appointed on behalf of men, and he deals with sins. He knows weakness, not by sinning but by suffering, and so he can sympathize with us. He did not take the honor to himself but was called by God, as Aaron was, yet to a higher and eternal order. In every respect he is what a high priest should be, and in every respect he surpasses all who went before him. The appeal of 4:14–16 therefore stands on solid ground. “Let us hold fast our confession. … Let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace.”

For Reflection

  1. A high priest must be “taken from among men” (5:1) and “clothed with weakness” (5:2). How does Christ’s full sharing in our human condition change the way you approach God when you feel weak, ignorant, or wayward?
  2. The Law distinguished sins of ignorance and weakness from high-handed defiance (Num 15:27–31). How does that distinction help you understand both the gentleness of Christ toward stumbling believers and the seriousness of the warnings that follow in Hebrews 6 and 10?
  3. Christ “did not glorify himself” (5:5). Where are you tempted to seize honor, position, or recognition rather than receive whatever God gives? What would it look like to follow Christ’s example in your work, family, or church?
  4. Jesus prayed “with a loud cry and tears” and “was heard,” yet he was not spared the cross (5:7). How does this shape your expectations when you pray in suffering? What does it mean to be heard by the God who is “able to save out of death”?
  5. “Although he was a Son, he learned obedience from the things he suffered” (5:8). What hardships in your life might God be using to teach you obedience, not as punishment but as training? How does Christ’s own path encourage you?
  6. Christ is “the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him” (5:9). How do faith and obedience relate in your own walk with Christ? Where is he calling you to listen and respond more fully?
