Melchizedek, Priest of God Most High (7:1–10)

Translation

1For this Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of God Most High, who met Abraham as he was returning from the slaughter of the kings and blessed him, 2to whom Abraham also apportioned a tenth of everything—he is, first, by the translation of his name, “king of righteousness,” and then also king of Salem, that is, “king of peace”; 3without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but made like the Son of God—he remains a priest perpetually.

4Now consider how great this man was, to whom Abraham, the patriarch, gave a tenth from the choicest spoils. 5And those of the sons of Levi who receive the priestly office have a commandment according to the law to collect a tenth from the people, that is, from their brothers, even though these have come out of the loins of Abraham. 6But the one who does not trace his descent from them has collected a tenth from Abraham and has blessed the one who had the promises. 7Now it is beyond all dispute that the lesser is blessed by the greater. 8And here, men who die receive tithes; but there, one of whom it is testified that he lives. 9And, so to speak, through Abraham even Levi, the one who receives tithes, has paid a tithe, 10for he was still in the loins of his father when Melchizedek met him.

Setting and Structure

The preacher has just told his hearers that Jesus entered “behind the curtain” as forerunner on our behalf, “having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (6:20). The little word “For” (γάρ gar) at the start of 7:1 ties what follows directly to that statement. Chapter 7 is the explanation of 6:20: who is this Melchizedek, and what does it mean to be a priest “according to his order”?

The first paragraph (7:1–10) is framed by an inclusio, a pair of matching bookends: it opens with Melchizedek “who met Abraham” (ὁ συναντήσας ho synantēsas, 7:1) and closes with “when Melchizedek met him” (συνήντησεν synēntēsen, 7:10). Inside the frame there are two parts. Verses 1–3 are a single long sentence that summarizes Genesis 14:17–20 and interprets it; its main clause is held back to the very end: “this Melchizedek … remains a priest perpetually.” Verses 4–10 then argue from the Genesis story to Melchizedek’s greatness, using two facts: Abraham gave him a tenth (7:4–6a, 8–10), and he blessed Abraham (7:6b–7). Each fact establishes that Melchizedek stands above Abraham, and therefore above the Levitical priesthood that descended from Abraham.

The author reasons from Scripture in a way his Jewish-Christian hearers would have recognized and respected. Three features of his method should be noted at the outset.

First, he pays attention to names. In the Hebrew Bible names often carry meaning, and Jewish interpreters of the period (Philo and Josephus both do so in the case of Melchizedek) regularly translated them. The author does the same: Melchizedek means “king of righteousness,” and Salem means “peace” (7:2).

Second, he pays attention to what Scripture does not say. Genesis is a book of genealogies. Almost every significant figure is introduced with a father, and the deaths of the patriarchs are carefully recorded (Gen 5; 11; 25:7–8; 35:28–29; 50:26). Melchizedek appears without any of this—no father, no mother, no line of descent, no birth, no death. For a priest this silence is remarkable, because in Israel priesthood depended entirely on descent. Jewish interpreters of the period could treat the omissions of Scripture as meaningful, and the author does so here; but he uses the silence not to make claims about Melchizedek’s biology but to show how Scripture has portrayed him: as a figure fitted to foreshadow the eternal Son.

Third, he reads the Law in light of the Psalms. Psalm 110:4 names Melchizedek and attaches to his order the word “forever.” The author reads Genesis 14 with that psalm in his ear. That is why he can say that Melchizedek “remains a priest perpetually” (7:3) and that “it is testified that he lives” (7:8). The testimony is that of Scripture.

Historical Background: Melchizedek in Jewish Tradition

Melchizedek fascinated Jewish writers in the centuries around the time of Hebrews, and some knowledge of these traditions helps us see what the author does—and does not—say.

Josephus (late first century AD) treats Melchizedek as a historical Canaanite ruler. He explains that the name means “righteous king,” says that he was the first priest of God, that he built the first temple at the city then called Salem, later Jerusalem (J.W. 6.438), and that he generously supplied Abraham’s army with provisions (Ant. 1.179–181).

Philo of Alexandria (died around AD 50) also translates the name as “righteous king” and Salem as “peace,” but his main interest is allegorical: Melchizedek becomes a figure of the divine reason or Logos, the “priest” who gives the soul joy and gladness (Allegorical Interpretation 3.79–82).

The Dead Sea Scrolls contain a fragmentary text known as 11QMelchizedek (11Q13). There Melchizedek appears as an exalted heavenly being who, in the final “jubilee,” proclaims release to the captives, atones for the sons of light, and executes God’s judgment on Belial and his spirits. The text applies to Melchizedek words that the Psalms use of God (Ps 82:1, “God stands in the divine council”), and it weaves together Leviticus 25, Isaiah 52:7, and Isaiah 61:1–2.

Later rabbinic tradition (the Targums on Genesis and the Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 32b) identified Melchizedek with Shem, Noah’s son, who according to the chronology of Genesis 11 would still have been alive in Abraham’s day. The Talmud even says the priesthood was taken from Shem and given to Abraham because Melchizedek blessed Abraham before blessing God. These sources are later than Hebrews and probably reflect, in part, a reaction against Christian use of Melchizedek.

Against this background the restraint of Hebrews is striking. The author never allegorizes Melchizedek as Philo does, never makes him an angelic redeemer as 11QMelchizedek does, and never identifies him with Shem. He sticks to what Genesis says, adds the testimony of Psalm 110, and lets the portrait point beyond itself to Christ. The Jewish traditions show only that the author’s readers would not have found it strange to take Melchizedek seriously; they are background, not the key to his meaning.

Verse by Verse

7:1

For this Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of God Most High, who met Abraham as he was returning from the slaughter of the kings and blessed him,

The author begins by summarizing the Genesis account in a series of descriptive phrases, all hanging on the subject “this Melchizedek” (οὗτος … ὁ Μελχισέδεκ houtos … ho Melchisedek). The demonstrative “this” points back to the Melchizedek named in Psalm 110:4 and quoted at 6:20: the figure the psalm mentions is the one whom Genesis describes. Every detail in this verse comes straight from Genesis 14:17–20, and the author’s Greek follows closely the wording of the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) there.

Four things are said about him. He is “king of Salem.” He is “priest of God Most High.” He “met Abraham as he was returning from the slaughter of the kings.” And he “blessed him.” The next verse adds the fifth fact: Abraham gave him a tenth.

The setting in Genesis 14 is a war. Four kings from the east, led by Chedorlaomer of Elam, had subdued the cities of the plain and, after a rebellion, returned to plunder them, carrying off Lot, Abraham’s nephew, from Sodom (Gen 14:1–12). Abraham armed 318 trained men of his household, pursued the invaders as far as Dan and beyond Damascus, defeated them in a night attack, and brought back the captives and the goods (14:13–16). The Greek word for “slaughter” here, κοπή kopē, is the same word the Septuagint uses at Genesis 14:17, rendering the Hebrew “from striking down” (מֵהַכּוֹת mehakkot). It was a decisive military victory.

On Abraham’s return, two kings come out to meet him. The king of Sodom comes to bargain (14:17, 21). But between the arrival of the king of Sodom and his speech, Genesis inserts the brief, almost abrupt, appearance of Melchizedek (14:18–20). The contrast is deliberate. The king of Sodom offers Abraham the goods; Abraham refuses them, swearing by “the Lord, God Most High,” that he will take nothing from him (14:22–23). Melchizedek offers Abraham bread, wine, and a blessing, and Abraham gives him a tenth. One king Abraham treats as a spiritual superior; the other he will not be indebted to.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 14:18–20

Hebrew: וּמַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק מֶלֶךְ שָׁלֵם הוֹצִיא לֶחֶם וָיָיִן וְהוּא כֹהֵן לְאֵל עֶלְיוֹן׃ וַיְבָרְכֵהוּ וַיֹּאמַר בָּרוּךְ אַבְרָם לְאֵל עֶלְיוֹן קֹנֵה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ׃ וּבָרוּךְ אֵל עֶלְיוֹן אֲשֶׁר־מִגֵּן צָרֶיךָ בְּיָדֶךָ וַיִּתֶּן־לוֹ מַעֲשֵׂר מִכֹּל׃

Transliteration: umalki-tsedeq melek shalem hotsi’ lechem vayayin vehu’ kohen le’el ’elyon. vayvarekehu vayyo’mar baruk ’avram le’el ’elyon qoneh shamayim va’arets. uvaruk ’el ’elyon ’asher-miggen tsareka beyadeka vayyitten-lo ma‘aser mikkol.

Translation: “And Melchizedek, king of Salem, brought out bread and wine; and he was priest of God Most High. And he blessed him and said, ‘Blessed be Abram by God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth; and blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand.’ And he gave him a tenth of everything.”

Several features of the Hebrew matter for Hebrews 7:

  • “God Most High” is אֵל עֶלְיוֹן ’El ‘Elyon. El is the common Semitic word for God; ‘Elyon means “the Highest.” The title stresses God’s supremacy over all powers. Abraham immediately identifies this God with the Lord: “I have lifted my hand to the Lord, God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth” (Gen 14:22). Melchizedek is therefore not presented as a priest of some Canaanite deity but as a genuine priest of the one true God, outside the family of Abraham. The Greek τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ὑψίστου tou theou tou hypsistou, “of God the Most High,” translates it exactly.
  • “Creator of heaven and earth” renders קֹנֵה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ qoneh shamayim va’arets. The verb qanah can mean “create” or “acquire, possess”; either way the God whom Melchizedek serves owns all things. He is the universal God, not a local one.
  • “He was priest” (וְהוּא כֹהֵן vehu’ kohen) is the first occurrence of the word kohen, “priest,” in the Bible. The first priest Scripture names is not Aaron or Levi but Melchizedek.
  • “He gave him a tenth of everything” (וַיִּתֶּן־לוֹ מַעֲשֵׂר מִכֹּל vayyitten-lo ma‘aser mikkol). The Hebrew does not name the subject; grammatically, “he” could be either man. The context, however, makes it clear: Melchizedek has just blessed Abraham and praised God for giving Abraham victory, and it is Abraham who returns with spoils. Hebrews names Abraham explicitly (7:2, 4), and that is the natural reading of the passage.

Hebrews does not mention the bread and wine. Many later Christian writers saw in them a foreshadowing of the Lord’s Supper, and some have spoken of Melchizedek’s “sacrifice” of bread and wine. But Genesis does not say Melchizedek offered anything to God; it says he “brought out” (הוֹצִיא hotsi’) bread and wine—the ordinary language of hospitality, refreshment for weary soldiers (compare Josephus’s understanding, mentioned above). Hebrews, which is deeply interested in sacrifice, passes over the bread and wine entirely. Its interest lies in the two priestly acts that establish rank: the blessing and the tithe. We should follow the inspired author’s emphasis rather than build on a detail he chose not to use.

The two participles that describe Melchizedek’s action are both aorist (a simple past): he “met” Abraham and “blessed” him. These are historical events. The author treats Genesis 14 as history, and Melchizedek as a real king of a real city at a real moment in Abraham’s life. That observation will be important when we come to the remarkable language of 7:3.

Where was Salem? The only other biblical use of the name places it in parallel with Zion: “His shelter was in Salem, and his dwelling place in Zion” (Ps 76:3 [English 76:2]; Hebrew וַיְהִי בְשָׁלֵם סֻכּוֹ וּמְעוֹנָתוֹ בְצִיּוֹן vayhi veshalem sukko ume‘onato vetsiyyon). Genesis 14:17 also locates the meeting at “the Valley of Shaveh, that is, the King’s Valley,” which 2 Samuel 18:18 places near Jerusalem. Josephus and the Genesis Apocryphon from Qumran likewise identify Salem with Jerusalem. The identification matters for the theology of the Bible: the city where Melchizedek was king and priest became the city of David, the city of Psalm 110 (“The Lord will send forth from Zion the scepter of your strength,” Ps 110:2). A Davidic king enthroned in Zion is told that he is a priest after the order of the ancient priest-king of that same city. There is a striking parallel in Joshua 10:1, where a later king of Jerusalem bears the similar name Adoni-tsedeq (אֲדֹנִי־צֶדֶק Adoni-tsedeq, “my lord is righteousness” or “lord of righteousness”), suggesting that “righteousness” was associated with Jerusalem’s kingship from ancient times.

7:2

to whom Abraham also apportioned a tenth of everything—he is, first, by the translation of his name, “king of righteousness,” and then also king of Salem, that is, “king of peace”;

The first half of the verse completes the summary of Genesis 14. The verb is unusual: Abraham “apportioned” or “divided off” (ἐμέρισεν emerisen, from μερίζω merizō) a tenth. The word pictures Abraham separating a portion out of the whole and assigning it to Melchizedek—a deliberate, measured act of acknowledgment. “A tenth” (δεκάτη dekatē) is the tithe. In the ancient Near East, giving a tenth to a king or to a sanctuary was a recognized way of honoring a superior or a deity. Abraham’s tithe will be the basis of the argument in 7:4–10; here the author simply records it.

Then the author turns to interpretation, signaled by the word ἑρμηνευόμενος hermēneuomenos, “being translated” or “interpreted” (the root of our word “hermeneutics”). He reads two titles out of Melchizedek’s name and city.

“King of righteousness.” The Hebrew name מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק Malki-tsedeq is made of two words: melek, “king,” and tsedeq, “righteousness.” The “-i” ending on malki can mean “my” (“my king is righteousness”), but in old Hebrew names it is often simply a connecting vowel joining two words, giving “king of righteousness.” Both Josephus and Philo understood the name as “righteous king,” and the author’s interpretation is the natural one. The Hebrew word tsedeq is not merely a legal quality; it describes the right ordering of relationships under God, the kind of rule the coming Davidic king would exercise: “Behold, the days are coming, declares the Lord, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he will reign as king and act wisely and do justice and righteousness in the land” (Jer 23:5; Hebrew וַהֲקִמֹתִי לְדָוִד צֶמַח צַדִּיק וּמָלַךְ מֶלֶךְ vahaqimoti ledavid tsemach tsaddiq umalak melek, “I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he will reign as king”).

“King of peace.” Salem (שָׁלֵם shalem) shares its root with שָׁלוֹם shalom, “peace.” So the king of Salem is the “king of peace.” Again, shalom is far more than the absence of war; it is wholeness, well-being, and harmony under God. Isaiah’s promised child is called “Prince of Peace” (שַׂר־שָׁלוֹם sar-shalom, Isa 9:5 [English 9:6]), and Zechariah foresees a figure who will be both king and priest, with “a counsel of peace” between the two roles (Zech 6:13).

The order matters, and the author marks it: “first … king of righteousness, and then also … king of peace.” In the Bible’s theology, peace follows righteousness; it does not come at its expense. “The work of righteousness will be peace” (Isa 32:17). “Righteousness and peace have kissed” (Ps 85:11 [English 85:10]). Paul states the gospel order: “having been justified by faith, we have peace with God” (Rom 5:1). The author does not develop these titles further—he does not say in so many words that Jesus is the king of righteousness and peace—but he has already applied to the Son the words of Psalm 45: “You have loved righteousness and hated lawlessness” (1:9), and later he will speak of “the God of peace who brought up from the dead … our Lord Jesus” (13:20) and of “the peaceful fruit of righteousness” (12:11). For hearers who knew those passages, the titles of Melchizedek are full of Christ.

Notice too that Melchizedek is both king and priest. In Israel those offices were kept strictly apart. Kings came from Judah, priests from Levi, and when King Uzziah presumed to burn incense in the temple, he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). Melchizedek unites the two offices in one person, and so does the figure of Psalm 110, who sits at God’s right hand as king (110:1–2) and is sworn in as priest (110:4). That union of offices is one of the things that makes Melchizedek a fitting pattern of Christ.

7:3

without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but made like the Son of God—he remains a priest perpetually.

This is one of the most striking—and most debated—verses in Hebrews. It contains three rare adjectives, two negative phrases about time, a crucial participle, and finally the main verb of the whole sentence that began in 7:1.

“Without father, without mother, without genealogy.” The three Greek words all begin with the negative prefix a- (like English “a-” in “atypical”): ἀπάτωρ apatōr, “fatherless”; ἀμήτωρ amētōr, “motherless”; ἀγενεαλόγητος agenealogētos, “without a recorded genealogy.” None of these words occurs anywhere else in the New Testament. In ordinary Greek, apatōr and amētōr could describe an orphan, a child of unknown or disreputable parentage, or, in religious language, a god who had no parent. The third word is probably the key to the other two: agenealogētos means “without a genealogy,” that is, without a recorded line of descent. The author will use the related verb in 7:6: Melchizedek is the one who “does not trace his descent” (μὴ γενεαλογούμενος mē genealogoumenos) from Levi. The point is not that Melchizedek was literally born without parents, but that Scripture introduces him without them.

Why would that matter? Because for a priest in Israel, genealogy was everything. Priesthood belonged by law to the descendants of Aaron (Exod 28:1; 29:9; Num 3:10), and the high priest was required to marry within strict limits so that his line would remain pure (Lev 21:13–15). After the exile, men who claimed to be priests but “searched for their registration among those enrolled in the genealogies” and “were not found” were “excluded from the priesthood as unclean,” and were forbidden to eat the most holy food until a priest could arise to consult the Urim and Thummim (Ezra 2:61–63; Neh 7:63–65). Josephus, himself a priest, proudly records his own priestly descent from public registers, and states that priests took great care to preserve their genealogies, even after wars (Against Apion 1.30–36; Life 1–6). In that world, a priest “without genealogy” was a contradiction in terms—unless his priesthood rested on a completely different basis than descent. That is exactly the author’s point, and he will make it explicit in 7:6 and 7:16: Melchizedek’s priesthood, and the priesthood of Jesus, does not depend on “a law of a fleshly commandment,” that is, on physical descent.

“Having neither beginning of days nor end of life.” Genesis records neither Melchizedek’s birth nor his death. He steps onto the stage of Scripture fully formed as king and priest, and he steps off it without any notice of his passing. In a book that carefully records the lifespans and deaths of the patriarchs (Gen 5:5, 8, 11; 9:29; 11:32; 25:7–8; 35:28–29), this silence is conspicuous.

“But made like the Son of God.” Here is the decisive phrase. The participle ἀφωμοιωμένος aphōmoiōmenos comes from ἀφομοιόω aphomoioō, “to make like, to produce a likeness or copy.” Three grammatical features deserve attention:

  • It is passive: Melchizedek “has been made like.” Someone else has done the likening. In context that is God, speaking through the Scripture that records him in this way.
  • It is perfect tense, which in Greek describes a past action with a lasting result: he “has been made like” and stands as a likeness.
  • The direction of the comparison is crucial: Melchizedek is made like the Son of God, not the Son made like Melchizedek. The Son is the original; Melchizedek is the copy. The same thought returns in 7:15, where Jesus is a priest “according to the likeness (ὁμοιότητα homoiotēta) of Melchizedek”: the likeness runs both ways, but the Son is prior and Melchizedek is the shadow cast ahead of him.

Note also the title: not “Jesus” or “Christ” but “the Son of God.” In Hebrews the title “Son” highlights Christ’s eternal relation to the Father: the Son “through whom also he made the ages,” the radiance of God’s glory (1:2–3), whose “years will not fail” (1:12). It is the eternal Son, without beginning of days or end of life in reality, whom the Genesis portrait of Melchizedek resembles.

“He remains a priest perpetually.” At last the main verb arrives: μένει menei, “he remains.” The phrase εἰς τὸ διηνεκές eis to diēnekes means “continuously, perpetually, without interruption.” It is a word Hebrews alone uses in the New Testament (also 10:1, 12, 14). Strictly, it is not quite the same as “forever” (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna) in Psalm 110:4; it emphasizes that Melchizedek’s priesthood, as Scripture presents it, is never handed on, never interrupted, never terminated. Genesis gives us no successor to Melchizedek. His priesthood “remains” as far as the record is concerned.

Interpretive Options: Who Was Melchizedek?

  • View 1: A Christophany. Some interpreters, ancient and modern, have argued that Melchizedek was a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son of God himself, like “the angel of the Lord” in some Old Testament passages. The words “without father … without mother … having neither beginning of days nor end of life” are taken literally as true only of God.
  • View 2: A heavenly or angelic being. Some, drawing on 11QMelchizedek, propose that the author regarded Melchizedek as an exalted heavenly figure who appeared to Abraham. (A fringe group in the early church, the “Melchizedekians” described by Epiphanius, went further and regarded Melchizedek as a power greater than Christ.)
  • View 3: A historical king-priest whose literary portrait in Genesis prefigures Christ. Melchizedek was a real human being, the king of Salem in Abraham’s time, a genuine priest of the true God. The things Hebrews says in 7:3 describe the way Scripture presents him: no parents, no genealogy, no birth, no death recorded. God so shaped the scriptural record that Melchizedek became a type—a God-given pattern—of the eternal Son.

Evaluation from the text. The third view best fits what Hebrews actually says, and it is the view of the great majority of careful interpreters, from Calvin and Owen to Westcott, Bruce, Hughes, Lane, and Cockerill.

(1) The direction of the comparison. If Melchizedek were the Son himself, it would make no sense to say that he was “made like the Son of God.” One is not made like oneself. The language of likeness requires two distinct persons.

(2) The argument of 7:11–17. The author argues that Psalm 110:4 foretells “a different priest” (ἕτερον … ἱερέα heteron … hierea, 7:11, 15) who would arise “according to the order of Melchizedek” and “according to the likeness of Melchizedek.” A priest cannot be “according to the order” and “likeness” of himself. The Son and Melchizedek are two figures, one the pattern of the other.

(3) The historical narrative. Genesis presents Melchizedek as a king among kings, ruling a known city, meeting Abraham at a known place (Gen 14:17–18). Hebrews summarizes this with plain historical verbs (7:1, 10). Nothing in either text suggests a heavenly visitor; and Hebrews 1–2 has already insisted at length that the Son is superior to angels, so it would be strange for the author now to found Christ’s priesthood on an angel’s.

(4) The author’s own method. In 7:6 the author explains agenealogētos as “not tracing his descent from them,” that is, from Levi—a statement about genealogical record, not about miraculous origin. And in 7:8 he says that “it is testified” that Melchizedek lives: the testimony is Scripture’s (Psalm 110:4 and the silence of Genesis), not a claim about Melchizedek’s biology.

(5) The incarnation. Hebrews insists that the Son became truly human by sharing “blood and flesh” (2:14) and was “made like his brothers in every respect” (2:17). The Son’s humanity began at the incarnation; a fully human appearance two millennia earlier, as king of a Canaanite city, would sit uneasily with that emphasis.

We conclude, then, that Melchizedek was a real man whom God, in the inspired record of Genesis, presented in such a way that he foreshadows the Son. The silence of Scripture is not accidental; it is part of the portrait God has drawn. Calvin put the point well in substance: the author is not saying that Melchizedek was never born or never died, but that Scripture, by its silence, sets him before us as if he had neither beginning nor end, so that he might be a picture of the eternal Christ. What is true of Melchizedek “literarily” is true of the Son literally. The Son really has no beginning of days (1:2, 10–12) and, having risen, really has no end of life (7:16, 24–25).

The pastoral force of this verse is easy to miss among the debates. The author wants his hearers to see that long before Sinai, long before Aaron or Levi, God had already placed in the Scriptures a priest whose priesthood did not depend on ancestry, who was both king and priest, whose name meant righteousness and whose city meant peace, and who “remains” without successor. The Levitical priesthood they were tempted to return to was, by comparison, a later and temporary arrangement. The Son of God, whom Melchizedek resembled, is the true and permanent Priest.

7:4

Now consider how great this man was, to whom Abraham, the patriarch, gave a tenth from the choicest spoils.

Having laid out the Genesis portrait, the author now begins to argue from it. He calls on his hearers to “consider” or “observe” (θεωρεῖτε theōreite), a verb that means to look at something attentively, to contemplate it. It can be read as a command (“Consider!”) or as a statement (“You see …”); the imperative fits the sermon’s style best (compare 3:1, “consider Jesus,” with a different verb). The preacher invites his hearers to stop and take in the stature of this figure.

“How great” translates πηλίκος pēlikos, “how large, how great,” a word used of physical size (Gal 6:11, “with what large letters”) and then of importance. Melchizedek’s greatness is measured by the greatness of the one who honored him.

The word order in Greek saves the decisive title for the very end of the sentence: “to whom a tenth Abraham gave from the choicest spoils—the patriarch.” The delayed phrase ὁ πατριάρχης ho patriarchēs lands with full weight. Abraham was not just any man; he was the patriarch, the father of the whole nation, the one to whom God gave the promises (6:13–15; 7:6). For a Jewish audience no human being stood higher in the history of Israel. Yet Abraham, the patriarch, gave a tenth to Melchizedek. If the patriarch honored him, how great must Melchizedek be?

Word Study: ἀκροθίνια akrothinia

The word translated “choicest spoils” occurs only here in the New Testament. It is formed from akron, “top,” and this, “heap,” and literally means “the top of the heap.” In Greek usage it referred to the first and best portion of the spoils of war or of a harvest, the part set aside and dedicated to the gods in thanksgiving. Greek cities, for example, dedicated such portions of their war booty at their great sanctuaries. By using this word the author highlights that Abraham’s gift was not a token or leftover. He gave from the best of what he had won, and he gave it as a religious act to a priest of God Most High. The tithe was an act of worship and an acknowledgment of the one who received it.

The author may also intend a contrast with the king of Sodom in Genesis 14:21–24. Abraham refused to keep anything that belonged to Sodom’s king, “not a thread or a sandal strap,” lest anyone say, “I have made Abram rich.” But he willingly gave the best of the spoils to Melchizedek. Abraham would not be indebted to a pagan king, but he gladly acknowledged the priest of God Most High.

7:5

And those of the sons of Levi who receive the priestly office have a commandment according to the law to collect a tenth from the people, that is, from their brothers, even though these have come out of the loins of Abraham.

To show how unusual Abraham’s tithe was, the author compares it with the ordinary tithing system under the law of Moses. Within Israel, the right to receive tithes belonged to the tribe of Levi by divine commandment.

Old Testament Text: Numbers 18:21, 26

Hebrew (18:21): וְלִבְנֵי לֵוִי הִנֵּה נָתַתִּי כָּל־מַעֲשֵׂר בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל לְנַחֲלָה חֵלֶף עֲבֹדָתָם אֲשֶׁר־הֵם עֹבְדִים אֶת־עֲבֹדַת אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד׃ — velivne levi hinneh natatti kol-ma‘aser beyisra’el lenachalah chelef ‘avodatam ’asher-hem ‘ovdim ’et-‘avodat ’ohel mo‘ed, “To the sons of Levi, behold, I have given every tithe in Israel as an inheritance, in return for their service which they serve, the service of the tent of meeting.”

Hebrew (18:26): וְאֶל־הַלְוִיִּם תְּדַבֵּר וְאָמַרְתָּ אֲלֵהֶם כִּי־תִקְחוּ מֵאֵת בְּנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל אֶת־הַמַּעֲשֵׂר אֲשֶׁר נָתַתִּי לָכֶם מֵאִתָּם בְּנַחֲלַתְכֶם וַהֲרֵמֹתֶם מִמֶּנּוּ תְּרוּמַת יְהוָה מַעֲשֵׂר מִן־הַמַּעֲשֵׂר׃ — “And you shall speak to the Levites and say to them, ‘When you take from the sons of Israel the tithe that I have given you from them as your inheritance, then you shall present from it a contribution to the Lord, a tithe from the tithe.’”

The law worked in two stages. The people gave a tenth to the Levites, who had no inheritance of land (Num 18:23–24; Deut 10:9, “The Lord is his inheritance”). The Levites in turn gave “a tithe from the tithe” to the priests, the descendants of Aaron (Num 18:26–28). The tithe was thus the support of those who served at God’s sanctuary on behalf of the people.

The author speaks of “those of the sons of Levi who receive the priestly office” (τὴν ἱερατείαν tēn hierateian, the priestly office or service). Strictly, Numbers gives the people’s tithe to the Levites and a tithe of that tithe to the priests. But the author’s argument concerns the whole Levitical system, which existed to serve the priesthood, and in his own day it was common for priests to be directly involved in collecting tithes (compare Neh 10:38 [Hebrew 10:39], where a priest accompanies the Levites when they receive tithes). Josephus even complains that in the years before the Jewish War the servants of the chief priests went to the threshing floors and took the tithes by force, so that poorer priests starved (Ant. 20.181, 206–207). The hearers would have known that tithes and priests went together.

Two phrases sharpen the point. The Levites have “a commandment according to the law” (ἐντολὴν … κατὰ τὸν νόμον entolēn … kata ton nomon). Their right to tithe was a legal entitlement conferred by the law of Moses. And they collect it “from the people, that is, from their brothers, even though these have come out of the loins of Abraham.” The word “loins” (ὀσφύς osphys) is the Hebrew-style idiom for the seat of procreative power: to come “out of someone’s loins” is to be his descendant (Gen 35:11). The Levites and the people from whom they collect tithes are equals by birth—all are sons of Abraham. Only a specific divine commandment raised the Levites to the position of receiving tithes from their own brothers.

The comparison prepares for the contrast in the next verse. The Levites received tithes (1) because the law commanded it, and (2) from their own kinsmen. Melchizedek received a tithe (1) without any such law, and (2) from Abraham himself, the father of them all.

7:6

But the one who does not trace his descent from them has collected a tenth from Abraham and has blessed the one who had the promises.

“The one who does not trace his descent from them” (ὁ … μὴ γενεαλογούμενος ἐξ αὐτῶν ho … mē genealogoumenos ex autōn) explains the word “without genealogy” of 7:3. Melchizedek had no place in the family tree of Levi; he had no legal claim to tithes under the Mosaic law, which did not yet exist. Nevertheless, he “collected a tenth from Abraham.” His right to receive it rested on something other than ancestry and law: on his own status as priest of God Most High.

Notice the tense of the two verbs: δεδεκάτωκεν dedekatōken, “has collected a tenth,” and εὐλόγηκεν eulogēken, “has blessed.” Both are Greek perfects. A simple past (aorist) would have merely narrated the events, as 7:1 did. The perfect presents them as completed acts whose significance stands permanently recorded. Hebrews often uses the perfect in this way when citing what Scripture records (see 11:17, 28). What happened in Genesis 14 remains on the record, and its meaning still holds.

Then comes the second fact: Melchizedek “has blessed the one who had the promises.” Abraham is described by his supreme privilege: he was the man who “had the promises” (τὸν ἔχοντα τὰς ἐπαγγελίας ton echonta tas epangelias). The hearers have just been reminded of those promises: God swore to Abraham, “Surely I will bless you and multiply you” (6:13–14, quoting Gen 22:17). Abraham was the bearer of God’s great blessing to the world (Gen 12:2–3). Yet this very man, the recipient of God’s promises, received a blessing from Melchizedek. The author lets that fact sink in before stating the principle that explains it.

7:7

Now it is beyond all dispute that the lesser is blessed by the greater.

The author states a principle he expects everyone to grant: “beyond all dispute” (χωρὶς … πάσης ἀντιλογίας chōris … pasēs antilogias, literally “without any contradiction”). The word antilogia means “dispute, contradiction”; the author used it in 6:16, where an oath puts “an end to every dispute.” Here he claims the principle needs no argument at all.

The comparison is expressed in the neuter gender: “the lesser” (τὸ ἔλαττον to elatton) is blessed by “the greater” (τοῦ κρείττονος tou kreittonos). The neuter makes it a general rule: whatever is lower in rank receives blessing from what is higher. The word kreittōn, “better, greater,” is one of the key words of Hebrews (1:4; 6:9; 7:19, 22; 8:6; 9:23; 10:34; 11:16, 35, 40; 12:24). This is its first use in chapter 7, and the chapter will go on to speak of a “better hope” (7:19) and a “better covenant” (7:22).

What kind of blessing is meant? Obviously not every kind of blessing flows from greater to lesser—the Psalms repeatedly call on human beings to “bless the Lord” (Ps 103:1–2), and in that sense the lesser blesses the greater. The author has in mind the authoritative blessing given by one who speaks for God and pronounces God’s favor on another. Such blessing is a priestly act. The Aaronic priests were commanded, “Thus you shall bless the sons of Israel”: “The Lord bless you and keep you; the Lord make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you; the Lord lift up his face toward you and give you peace” (Num 6:23–26). The Hebrew text then adds, “So they shall put my name upon the sons of Israel, and I myself will bless them” (וְשָׂמוּ אֶת־שְׁמִי עַל־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל וַאֲנִי אֲבָרֲכֵם vesamu ’et-shemi ‘al-bene yisra’el va’ani ’avarakem, Num 6:27). The patriarchs, too, blessed their sons with authority (Gen 27; 48–49). In each case the one who blesses stands in a position of authority as God’s representative toward the one blessed.

So when Melchizedek blessed Abraham, he acted as Abraham’s superior in the things of God. Abraham accepted the blessing and confirmed the relationship with his tithe. Neither man disputed it. The two acts—the tithe given upward and the blessing given downward—both point the same way: Melchizedek was greater than Abraham.

7:8

And here, men who die receive tithes; but there, one of whom it is testified that he lives.

The author now adds a further contrast, built on a neat balance in Greek: “here” (ὧδε hōde) … “there” (ἐκεῖ ekei). “Here” refers to the Levitical system, the arrangement of the law, which in the author’s day was still functioning (note the present tense “receive”). “There” refers to the scene in Genesis 14.

“Here, men who die receive tithes.” The participle ἀποθνῄσκοντες apothnēskontes is present tense: “dying men,” men who are subject to death, who one after another die. The generations of priests rose and passed away. Every tithe received in the temple was received by a mortal man who would soon hand his office to another. The theme of priestly mortality will become a central argument in 7:23: “the former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing.”

“But there, one of whom it is testified that he lives.” The verb “it is testified” (μαρτυρούμενος martyroumenos, “being attested”) is a favorite word of Hebrews for the witness of Scripture. In 7:17 the same verb introduces the quotation of Psalm 110:4: “For it is testified, ‘You are a priest forever.’” In chapter 11 the faithful “received testimony” through Scripture’s record of them (11:2, 4, 5, 39). So here, the testimony that Melchizedek “lives” is the testimony of Scripture. Genesis records no death for him, and Psalm 110:4 speaks of a priesthood “forever” after his order. In the scriptural record, Melchizedek is a living priest.

Does the author mean that the historical Melchizedek never died? Those who hold that Melchizedek was a heavenly being (see the box at 7:3) appeal to this verse. But the author has carefully chosen his words. He does not say “Melchizedek never died”; he says “it is testified that he lives.” He is drawing out the significance of Scripture’s portrait, exactly as he did in 7:3. The contrast is between what the law shows us (priests who die) and what the Genesis-and-Psalm portrait shows us (a priest who is presented as living). The full reality to which this portrait points is the Son, who really has become a priest “according to the power of an indestructible life” (7:16) and who “always lives” (7:25).

The rhetorical effect is powerful. Which is greater: a priesthood of men who die, or a priesthood that Scripture presents as living? The hearers, tempted to look back to the temple and its priests, are invited to compare. The best the old system could offer was a succession of mortal men.

7:9

And, so to speak, through Abraham even Levi, the one who receives tithes, has paid a tithe,

The author pushes the argument one step further, and he signals that he knows he is doing so. The phrase ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν hōs epos eipein means “so to speak,” “one might say,” or “to put it a certain way.” It occurs only here in the Bible, but it was a common idiom in polished literary Greek, used (for example, frequently by Philo) to soften a bold statement or to show that an expression is not to be pressed too literally. The author is not claiming that Levi, who was not yet born, literally handed over a tenth. He is speaking in a way that his hearers would recognize as a figure of speech with a real truth behind it.

The truth is this: “through Abraham even Levi, the one who receives tithes, has paid a tithe.” The irony is pointed. Levi—the very tribe whose privilege is to receive tithes (ὁ δεκάτας λαμβάνων ho dekatas lambanōn)—is here found paying one. The verb δεδεκάτωται dedekatōtai, “has been tithed” or “has paid a tithe,” is again perfect: the fact stands on record. The tithe-receiver has become a tithe-payer. If Levi paid tithes to Melchizedek, then Melchizedek is greater than Levi, and the priesthood “according to the order of Melchizedek” is greater than the Levitical priesthood.

7:10

for he was still in the loins of his father when Melchizedek met him.

The author explains how Levi could have paid tithes “through Abraham”: “he was still in the loins of his father.” Abraham was Levi’s great-grandfather (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Levi), and “father” here means “ancestor.” When Abraham met Melchizedek, Levi was, so to speak, present in Abraham, since all of Abraham’s descendants were contained in him as their source.

This way of thinking may seem strange to modern readers, but it is thoroughly biblical. Scripture often treats an ancestor and his descendants as a unity, sharing in one another’s actions and destinies. Scholars call this “corporate solidarity” or “corporate personality.” Jacob’s blessings and curses on his sons in Genesis 49 are really pronouncements about the tribes that would descend from them. The covenant God made with Abraham included his “seed” after him (Gen 17:7). The nation of Israel can be called by the name of its father, “Jacob” or “Israel.” Paul reasons in a similar way when he says that “in Adam all die” (1 Cor 15:22) and that through one man’s trespass many were made sinners (Rom 5:12–19); and in a more positive sense, that believers are “in Christ.” The author of Hebrews has already used this sense of shared identity when he spoke of Jesus and his “brothers” as being “all from one” (2:11).

The phrase “so to speak” in 7:9 shows that the author holds his conclusion with a light touch. It is not the foundation of his argument; the foundation was laid in 7:4–7. It is a vivid way of driving home the point: in Genesis 14, the whole Levitical priesthood, represented in its ancestor, bowed before Melchizedek. Long before the law was given, the priesthood of Levi had already acknowledged a greater priesthood.

The section closes where it opened, with Melchizedek meeting Abraham (compare 7:1). That bookend rounds off the first movement of the chapter. Everything the author needs from Genesis 14 has now been drawn out: Melchizedek’s royal and priestly titles, the significance of Scripture’s silence about his origins and end, his superiority to Abraham shown by blessing and tithe, and his superiority to Levi. In the next section the author will turn back to Psalm 110:4 and ask the decisive question: if a priest “according to the order of Melchizedek” was still to come, what does that say about the priesthood of Aaron?

For Reflection

  1. The author treats the details of Genesis 14—and even its silences—as carefully shaped by God to point to Christ. How does this encourage you to read the Old Testament more attentively, and what guardrails does Hebrews itself provide against reading into Scripture what is not there?
  2. Melchizedek is “first king of righteousness, and then also king of peace.” Why is the order significant? How does it shape your understanding of the peace Christ gives (Rom 5:1)?
  3. Abraham, who refused to be enriched by the king of Sodom, gladly gave the best of his spoils to the priest of God Most High. What does his example teach about honoring God with what is first and best, and about the people and powers to whom we allow ourselves to be indebted?
  4. Abraham, the bearer of God’s promises, received a blessing from someone greater. In what ways do you need to receive, rather than merely possess, God’s blessing through the greater Priest, Jesus?
  5. “Here, men who die receive tithes; but there, one of whom it is testified that he lives.” In what ways are you tempted to rest your spiritual security on institutions or people who will pass away rather than on the living Christ?
