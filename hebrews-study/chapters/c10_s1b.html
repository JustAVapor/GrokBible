10:5

Therefore, when he comes into the world, he says, “Sacrifice and offering you did not desire, but a body you prepared for me;

“Therefore” (διὸ dio) links what follows to the impossibility just stated. Because animal blood cannot take away sins, a different kind of offering is needed, and Scripture had already announced it. The author introduces Psalm 40:6–8 (Hebrew 40:7–9; Greek Psalter 39:7–9) as the words of the Son himself: “when he comes into the world, he says.” The subject is not named, but there is no doubt who is meant. Verse 10 identifies him as “Jesus Christ.”

The phrase “coming into the world” (εἰσερχόμενος εἰς τὸν κόσμον eiserchomenos eis ton kosmon) speaks of the incarnation. The present participle presents the Son’s words as spoken at the moment of his entrance into the world, as the very purpose with which he came. Similar language appears in John’s Gospel (“the true light … was coming into the world,” John 1:9; “I have come into the world,” John 18:37) and in Paul (“Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners,” 1 Tim 1:15). Hebrews has already used a related image at 1:6: “when he brings the firstborn into the world.” Some interpreters think the phrase covers the whole of Christ’s earthly life, which was one continuous act of obedience. That is true to the theology of the passage, but the emphasis falls on the beginning, on the attitude with which the eternal Son took up his human life. From the first, he came to do God’s will and to offer himself.

Hebrews often lets Christ speak through Old Testament texts (2:12–13 quotes Ps 22:22 and Isa 8:17–18 as his words). This is not arbitrary. Psalm 40 is a psalm of David, and the author reads David’s words of obedient self-offering as finding their full truth in David’s greater Son. David sincerely said, “I have come to do your will,” but he did not fully do it, as the rest of the psalm admits: “my iniquities have overtaken me” (Ps 40:12). Only one Son of David has said these words with complete truth. The author’s method here is typological. He traces a pattern in the life of the anointed king that is filled to the full in the Messiah.

The first line of the quotation is “Sacrifice and offering you did not desire” (Θυσίαν καὶ προσφορὰν οὐκ ἠθέλησας thysian kai prosphoran ouk ēthelēsas). The psalm does not mean that God never commanded sacrifices, since he plainly did. It means, in the idiom of the prophets, that God did not want sacrifice as an end in itself or as a substitute for the heart. The same contrast runs through the Old Testament: “Does the Lord delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as much as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Sam 15:22); “I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice” (Hos 6:6); “You do not delight in sacrifice” (Ps 51:16). God’s deepest desire was always a person wholly devoted to his will. The sacrifices were the means he appointed for sinners who could not offer that.

The second line is the famous difference: “but a body you prepared for me” (σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι sōma de katērtisō moi). The Hebrew text of the psalm says something quite different.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 40:6–8 (Hebrew 40:7–9)

Hebrew 40:7: זֶבַח וּמִנְחָה לֹא־חָפַצְתָּ אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי עוֹלָה וַחֲטָאָה לֹא שָׁאָלְתָּ׃ — zevach uminchah lo-chafatsta; oznayim karita li; olah wachata’ah lo sha’alta, “Sacrifice and grain offering you did not delight in; ears you have dug out for me; burnt offering and sin offering you did not ask for.”

Hebrew 40:8: אָז אָמַרְתִּי הִנֵּה־בָאתִי בִּמְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר כָּתוּב עָלָי׃ — az amarti, hinneh-va’ti; bimgillat-sefer katuv alay, “Then I said, ‘Behold, I have come; in the scroll of the book it is written concerning me.’”

Hebrew 40:9: לַעֲשׂוֹת־רְצוֹנְךָ אֱלֹהַי חָפָצְתִּי וְתוֹרָתְךָ בְּתוֹךְ מֵעָי׃ — la‘asot-retsonkha elohay chafatsti, wetoratkha betokh me‘ay, “To do your will, my God, I delight; and your law is within my inmost being.”

“Ears you have dug out for me.” The Hebrew אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי oznayim karita li uses the verb כָּרָה karah, “to dig” (as one digs a well or a pit). The vivid picture is of God hollowing out the psalmist’s ears, opening them so that he can hear and obey. Isaiah’s Servant uses a similar idea: “The Lord God has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious; I did not turn back” (Isa 50:5, פָּתַח־לִי אֹזֶן patach-li ozen). In Hebrew idiom, “to hear” very often means “to obey” (the verb shama‘ carries both senses, as in the Shema, Deut 6:4). The psalmist is saying that God does not want ritual without obedience; instead he has given the psalmist an open, listening ear, a capacity for obedience. Some older interpreters connected the phrase with the law of the slave who chose to serve his master for life, whose ear was pierced at the doorpost (Exod 21:6). That law, however, uses a different verb (וְרָצַע weratsa‘, “and he shall pierce”), and the psalm speaks of both ears, so the connection is at most a suggestive echo, not the meaning of the text.

The Greek Psalter. Hebrews quotes the psalm in Greek, and the Greek tradition is itself divided. The great uncial manuscripts of the Greek Old Testament (Codex Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and Alexandrinus) read “a body you prepared for me,” as Hebrews does. Some other Greek witnesses, along with Latin translations made from the Greek, read “ears” (ὠτία ōtia), and the standard critical edition of the Septuagint prints “ears” as the original Greek reading. A frequently offered explanation is that “body” arose within the Greek tradition through a copying error, since in the capital letters of ancient manuscripts the end of the preceding word and the word “ears” could be misread as “body.” Others think the translator himself paraphrased the Hebrew, rendering the part (ears) by the whole (body). We cannot be certain how the reading arose, and Christian copyists may have reinforced it under the influence of Hebrews. What is certain is that the author of Hebrews quoted the form of the psalm current in the Greek Bible he and his hearers used. Two further small differences may be noted: where the Hebrew and the Greek Psalter say God “did not ask for” burnt offerings, Hebrews reads “you took no pleasure in” (10:6), and where the Psalter continues, “to do your will, my God, I desired,” Hebrews ends the quotation with “to do your will, O God” (10:7), making it the purpose of “I have come.”

How should a reader who defers to the authority of the original languages think about this difference? Several points, taken together, give a clear and honest answer.

First, the Greek reading does not contradict the Hebrew; it states the same truth more fully. The ear is the organ of hearing and therefore of obedience. A body is the whole instrument by which obedience is carried out. To say “you opened my ears” and “you prepared a body for me” is to move from the part to the whole: God has equipped his servant to obey, not merely to offer animals. Calvin made essentially this point, observing that the apostle followed the Greek rendering and that the sense of the passage is not altered, because the psalm speaks of a servant made ready by God for obedience. Many later commentators, Bruce among them, have similarly stressed that the author’s argument does not depend on “body” alone.

Second, the author’s argument depends on the whole sense of the psalm, which is the same in Hebrew and Greek. God does not desire mere sacrifice; he desires a person who says, “I have come to do your will.” This is the point the author himself draws out in 10:8–9. He explains the quotation by citing the line about sacrifices and the line about doing God’s will. He does not mention the body at all until 10:10. The center of gravity is obedience, and that is fully present in the Hebrew.

Third, the word “body” does bring something precious into view, and the author uses it in 10:10: “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ.” In the incarnation the obedience the psalm describes became bodily, embodied, and therefore capable of being offered. The Son did not merely hear God’s will; he did it in a human body, all the way to the cross. The Hebrew psalm’s open ear and the Greek psalm’s prepared body meet in Jesus. He is the Servant whose ear was opened morning by morning (Isa 50:4–5) and who gave his back to the smiters, and he is the Son who had a body prepared for him (cf. 2:14, “he likewise shared in flesh and blood”) so that he could offer it. As many commentators have noted, the obedience of the incarnate Son is not something added to his sacrifice; it is the very substance of it.

The verb “prepared” (κατηρτίσω katērtisō, from καταρτίζω katartizō) means to fit out, equip, or make ready for a purpose. It is used at 11:3 of the ages being “fashioned” by the word of God, and in the benediction at 13:21: “may the God of peace … equip you with every good thing to do his will.” The connection is beautiful. God prepared a body for his Son so that he could do God’s will, and God equips his people so that they too may do his will. The Son’s embodied obedience is the source and pattern of ours.

10:6

in burnt offerings and sin offerings you took no pleasure.

The psalm names four kinds of offering across verses 5 and 6, and together they cover the main categories of the Levitical system. “Sacrifice” (θυσία thysia; Hebrew זֶבַח zevach) refers to animal sacrifices in general, especially those shared in a meal such as the peace offering. “Offering” (προσφορά prosphora; Hebrew מִנְחָה minchah) here stands for the grain offering, the gift of flour and oil. “Burnt offerings” (ὁλοκαυτώματα holokautōmata; Hebrew עוֹלָה olah) were consumed entirely on the altar. The daily morning and evening lambs were burnt offerings. The last term is literally “[offerings] concerning sin” (περὶ ἁμαρτίας peri hamartias). This is the regular Greek Old Testament expression for the sin offering (Hebrew חֲטָאָה chata’ah, a form of the usual chatta’t), the sacrifice that dealt with defilement and guilt (Lev 4–5), and the central offering of the Day of Atonement (Lev 16). The same phrase appears at Isaiah 53:10 in the Greek tradition, and Paul uses it of God sending his Son “as an offering for sin” (Rom 8:3). By naming all four, the psalm sweeps the whole sacrificial system into view. None of it, in itself, was what God most deeply desired.

“You took no pleasure” (οὐκ εὐδόκησας ouk eudokēsas) uses the verb εὐδοκέω eudokeō, “to be well pleased, to delight in.” It is the word of the Father’s voice at Jesus’ baptism: “You are my beloved Son; in you I am well pleased” (Mark 1:11). The contrast is profound. In burnt offerings and sin offerings God took no pleasure; in his Son, who came to do his will, he is well pleased. The author will use the same verb at the end of this chapter, quoting Habakkuk: “if he shrinks back, my soul takes no pleasure in him” (10:38). What pleases God is a life of trusting obedience, perfectly in his Son, and derivatively in those who follow him by faith.

As already noted, the Hebrew says God “did not ask for” (לֹא שָׁאָלְתָּ lo sha’alta) burnt offering and sin offering, and the Greek Psalter agrees. Hebrews’ wording, “took no pleasure,” may come from a Greek text known to the author, or it may be his own adaptation, perhaps influenced by the parallel line of Psalm 51 (“in burnt offering you take no pleasure,” Ps 51:16). Either way, the meaning is the same as in the Hebrew. God “not asking for” sacrifices, in the sense that he did not demand them as his ultimate aim, is the same as his “not delighting” in them. The Hebrew of the psalm’s first line already used “delight” (לֹא־חָפַצְתָּ lo-chafatsta, “you did not delight”).

We should be careful not to over-read the psalm. It does not say God hated the sacrifices he commanded, nor that faithful Israelites who offered them were wrong to do so. The point, both in David’s psalm and in Hebrews, is comparative and ultimate: the sacrifices were never God’s final goal, and they could never please him the way a wholly obedient life pleases him. Hebrews 10 reveals why God set up a system that he did not ultimately “delight in.” It was a shadow that waited for the body.

10:7

Then I said, ‘Behold, I have come—in the scroll of the book it is written concerning me—to do your will, O God.’”

“Then” (τότε tote) marks a turning point. Given that God did not desire the old sacrifices and had prepared a body for his Son, the Son speaks: “Behold, I have come” (Ἰδοὺ ἥκω Idou hēkō). The verb ἥκω hēkō, though present in form, means “I have come, I am here.” It is the word of a servant presenting himself for duty. In the Hebrew, הִנֵּה־בָאתִי hinneh-va’ti, “behold, I have come,” is similar to the response of the prophets and servants of God: “Here I am” (Gen 22:1; 1 Sam 3:4; Isa 6:8). The Son comes as the willing servant of the Father’s purpose.

The middle clause is an aside: “in the scroll of the book it is written concerning me” (ἐν κεφαλίδι βιβλίου γέγραπται περὶ ἐμοῦ en kephalidi bibliou gegraptai peri emou). The Greek word κεφαλίς kephalis, literally “little head,” referred to the knob at the end of the rod around which a scroll was wound, and so by extension to the scroll itself. It translates the Hebrew מְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר megillat-sefer, “a roll of a book.” Ancient books were written on long strips of leather or papyrus and rolled up, and the phrase means simply “the written scroll.”

What scroll did the psalmist mean? In David’s own setting it most likely referred to the book of the law, which set out God’s will for his people and, in particular, for Israel’s king. The king was to write a copy of the law, keep it with him, and read it all his life so that he might keep its commands (Deut 17:18–20). The Hebrew phrase כָּתוּב עָלָי katuv alay can mean “written about me” or “prescribed for me,” that is, written as my duty. Either way, the king understood his life as governed by what God had written. For the author of Hebrews, spoken by Christ, the words take on their fullest sense. The whole Scripture is “written concerning” the Messiah. As Jesus said on the road to Emmaus, “everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled” (Luke 24:44; cf. John 5:39, 46). The verb “it is written” is in the Greek perfect tense (γέγραπται gegraptai): it stands written, and it remains in force. The Son came knowing that his path had already been mapped out in the Scriptures, and he submitted to it.

The purpose of his coming is the climax of the quotation: “to do your will, O God” (τοῦ ποιῆσαι, ὁ θεός, τὸ θέλημά σου tou poiēsai, ho theos, to thelēma sou). The Greek construction (the article with the infinitive) expresses purpose: “I have come in order to do your will.” By ending the quotation here, the author makes these words the point of the whole psalm. The Gospels show this will governing Jesus’ entire life. “My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work” (John 4:34); “I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me” (John 6:38). And in Gethsemane, facing the cup of suffering, he prayed, “Not my will, but yours, be done” (Luke 22:42). Hebrews itself has described that struggle: he offered up prayers and supplications with loud crying and tears, and “although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered” (5:7–8). The will that Christ came to do included his death.

The Hebrew psalm continues with a line the author does not quote but that his readers who knew the psalm would recall: “and your law is within my inmost being” (וְתוֹרָתְךָ בְּתוֹךְ מֵעָי wetoratkha betokh me‘ay, Ps 40:8, Hebrew 40:9). This is remarkably close to the new-covenant promise the author will quote at the end of this section: “I will put my law within them (בְּקִרְבָּם beqirbam), and on their heart I will write it” (Jer 31:33; Heb 10:16). Whether or not the author intended the link, it is a true one. The Son is the one man in whom God’s law was perfectly inward, and through his offering God writes that same law on the hearts of his people. What was true of the Head becomes true, by grace, of the members.

10:8

When he says above, “Sacrifices and offerings and burnt offerings and sin offerings you did not desire, nor did you take pleasure in them” (these are offered according to the law),

Now the author explains the quotation, much as a preacher in the synagogue would expound a text he had just read. He divides it into two parts, the first and the second, and lays them side by side. The word “above” (ἀνώτερον anōteron, “higher up,” here “earlier”) refers to the first part of the quotation, much as we might say “in the lines above.” The present participle “saying” (λέγων legōn) keeps the Son as the speaker.

In repeating the first part, the author makes three small but deliberate changes. He makes all four terms plural (“sacrifices and offerings”), which generalizes them to the whole range of the sacrificial system. He combines the two verbs from the separate lines (“you did not desire, nor did you take pleasure in them”), which presents God’s attitude as one consistent judgment. And he adds a pointed comment of his own: “these are offered according to the law” (αἵτινες κατὰ νόμον προσφέρονται haitines kata nomon prospherontai).

That parenthesis is the heart of the matter. The sacrifices God did not ultimately desire were not pagan sacrifices or the hypocritical offerings of the wicked. They were the very sacrifices commanded by the law of Moses. This is a bold thing to say, and the author says it on the authority of the psalm itself. It is not his own disparagement of Moses. Scripture testifies that the law’s sacrifices were not God’s final purpose. This fits what the author has already argued: “a change of the law” accompanies the change of priesthood (7:12), “the former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness, for the law made nothing perfect” (7:18–19), and the first covenant was “not faultless” (8:7). The pronoun αἵτινες haitines often carries the nuance “which are of such a kind as”: the sacrifices, being by nature legal offerings of animals, belong to the category that God did not ultimately desire.

The present tense “are offered” (προσφέρονται prospherontai) again suggests that these rites were still taking place in Jerusalem when the author wrote. His hearers could see the smoke, or at least hear of it, and some may have felt its pull. He wants them to see that the psalm, spoken by Christ, sets those offerings aside.

10:9

then he has said, “Behold, I have come to do your will.” He takes away the first in order to establish the second.

The second part of the quotation follows: “then he has said, ‘Behold, I have come to do your will.’” The author shortens it, omitting the aside about the scroll and the address “O God,” so that the contrast stands out starkly. On one side: the sacrifices offered according to the law, which God did not desire. On the other: the Son’s coming to do God’s will. The verb “he has said” (εἴρηκεν eirēken) is perfect tense, meaning the word was spoken and stands on record. The word “then” carries a sense of sequence. After the inadequacy of the old sacrifices had been declared, the Son’s offer of himself came next.

Then comes the author’s conclusion, in a crisp sentence: “He takes away the first in order to establish the second” (ἀναιρεῖ τὸ πρῶτον ἵνα τὸ δεύτερον στήσῃ anairei to prōton hina to deuteron stēsē). “The first” and “the second” are neuter in Greek and so refer to things, not persons. The most natural reading is that “the first” is the system of sacrifices according to the law (the first part of the quotation) and “the second” is the doing of God’s will by the Son (the second part). The words also inevitably recall the author’s language of a “first” and “second” covenant (8:7, 13; 9:1, 15, 18). By setting aside the old sacrifices, Christ also sets aside the covenant they belonged to, and establishes the new one in its place.

Word Study: ἀναιρέω anaireō and ἵστημι histēmi

Anaireō means “to take up, take away, do away with, abolish.” In legal settings it can mean to annul, and it is also frequently used in the New Testament for putting someone to death (Acts 2:23; 5:33). It is a strong word: the first is not merely supplemented but removed. Its opposite here is histēmi (“establish, cause to stand, confirm”), which in legal usage means to set up and confirm something as valid. The same verb appears in Paul: “Do we then abolish the law through faith? By no means! On the contrary, we establish the law” (Rom 3:31). Taken together, the two Greek verbs express a decisive change of order. One thing is taken off the stage so that another may take its place permanently.

This sentence has to be read in the light of the whole of Scripture. The author does not mean that God’s moral will revealed in the law is abolished. Jeremiah’s promise, which the author quotes a few verses later, is that God will write his laws on his people’s hearts (10:16). What is taken away is the system of animal sacrifice as the means of dealing with sin, together with the priesthood and covenant arrangements built around it. What is established is the will of God, done perfectly by the Son in his self-offering, and through it the new covenant in which God’s will becomes the inward delight of his people. Jesus said he came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them (Matt 5:17). The sacrificial law is fulfilled by being brought to its goal in him, and once the goal is reached, the shadow has done its work.

For the original hearers, the implication was immediate. To go back to the Levitical sacrifices would be to return to what God himself has taken away, and to reject what God has established. It would be like returning to a shadow after the real body has come into the room.

10:10

By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.

This verse gathers up everything the psalm has said and applies it to believers. “By that will” (ἐν ᾧ θελήματι en hō thelēmati, literally “in which will”) refers to the will of God that the Son came to do. The will is God’s saving purpose, his eternal plan to make a people holy for himself. It was carried out in the Son’s obedience, and so it is “by” or “in” that will that believers are sanctified. Our salvation, in other words, rests ultimately on God’s own will and its perfect fulfillment by Christ, not on our performance of rituals.

“We have been sanctified” (ἡγιασμένοι ἐσμὲν hēgiasmenoi esmen) is a perfect passive participle with the verb “to be.” Greek uses this periphrastic construction to emphasize a resulting state: “we stand sanctified, we are in a state of having been made holy.” The action was done to us (passive), it was completed (perfect), and its result endures. To “sanctify” (ἁγιάζω hagiazō) in Hebrews means above all to consecrate, to set apart for God and make fit to approach him. It belongs to the language of the sanctuary. Under the old covenant, priests, vessels, and people were sanctified by blood and water so they could take part in worship (Exod 29:21; Lev 8:30; cf. Heb 9:13, “sanctifies for the purification of the flesh”). In Christ believers are sanctified in a far deeper way. Their consciences are cleansed, their sins are forgiven, and they are brought into God’s presence as his holy people (2:11; 9:14; 10:22; 13:12). In this verse, sanctification is not primarily the gradual process of growth in holiness (which Hebrews also teaches, 12:10, 14). It is the decisive act of God that makes us his own.

The means is “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ” (διὰ τῆς προσφορᾶς τοῦ σώματος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ dia tēs prosphoras tou sōmatos Iēsou Christou). This is where the psalm’s “body” bears fruit. The body God prepared for the Son was the body the Son offered. His obedience was not just a matter of inward disposition but of a life lived and a death endured in the flesh. The phrase “the offering of the body” appears nowhere else in the New Testament, but it agrees with Peter’s “he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree” (1 Pet 2:24) and Paul’s “you have died to the law through the body of Christ” (Rom 7:4). It also corresponds to the Lord’s own words at the Supper: “This is my body, given for you” (Luke 22:19). Notice too the full name, “Jesus Christ,” used only three times in Hebrews (here; 13:8; 13:21). The author has spent many chapters speaking of the Son and the high priest. Here, at the climax, he names him with the name of his earthly humanity (Jesus) and his messianic office (Christ).

Last, and emphatically placed at the end of the sentence, is “once for all” (ἐφάπαξ ephapax). The word has rung through the central section like a bell: Christ offered himself “once for all” (7:27), entered the sanctuary “once for all” (9:12), and now his body was offered “once for all.” The word means both “once only” and “once with permanent effect.” No repetition is needed or possible. Calvin presses the point against the medieval doctrine of the Mass as a repeated sacrifice: if Christ’s offering was once for all, anyone who claims to offer him again denies its sufficiency. Whatever one concludes about the various Christian traditions of the Lord’s Supper, the author of Hebrews leaves no room for any sacrifice for sin that adds to, repeats, or re-presents Christ’s offering as if it were incomplete. The Supper remembers the sacrifice (1 Cor 11:24–26); it does not repeat it.

The pastoral comfort of this verse is immense. A believer who is struggling with sin, weak in faith, or aware of failure may still say, on the authority of God’s word, “I have been sanctified.” This rests not on how holy I feel, but on what was done by God’s will through the offering of Jesus’ body once for all. The ongoing pursuit of holiness, which Hebrews insists upon (12:14), grows out of this settled consecration. It does not produce it.
