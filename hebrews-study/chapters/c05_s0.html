Hebrews 5

Appointed by God, Perfected through Suffering

At 2:17 the author promised that Jesus would prove to be “a merciful and faithful high priest.” Chapters 3 and 4 dealt with the word “faithful” by setting Jesus beside Moses. At 4:14 the preacher turned to the word “merciful.” “Since, then, we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast the confession.” Chapter 5 continues that paragraph without a break. The chapter division here, like all the chapter divisions in our Bibles, was added in the Middle Ages, long after the sermon was written. It cuts the author’s thought in the middle. The section that begins at 4:14 runs to 5:10. Read 4:14–16 and 5:1–10 together, and you hear one sustained argument. We have a high priest who can sympathize with our weaknesses (4:15). That claim is true, because Jesus meets and surpasses every qualification God laid down for a high priest (5:1–10).

This is the first time Hebrews sets out at length what a high priest is. Everything that follows in chapters 7–10 rests on it: the Melchizedek priesthood, the new covenant, the heavenly sanctuary, the once-for-all sacrifice. The author works like a careful lawyer. First he states the general requirements of the office as the Law of Moses defined them (5:1–4). Then he shows, point by point and in reverse order, how Christ fulfills them (5:5–10). A high priest must be one with the people he represents. He must deal gently with them because he knows weakness himself. Above all, he must be called by God, for no one may take this honor for himself. Christ did not appoint himself. God appointed him, and he did it with two oracles from the Psalms: “You are my Son” (Ps 2:7) and “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Ps 110:4). Christ shared our weakness too, not by sinning, but by praying in the days of his flesh “with a loud cry and tears.” Through suffering he learned obedience and was “made perfect,” fully qualified for his office. He became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him.

With 5:10 the author has reached the brink of his main subject. Melchizedek has now been named twice (5:6, 10). Then, abruptly, he stops. “About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become sluggish in hearing” (5:11). The second part of the chapter, 5:11–14, opens a long pastoral exhortation that runs through 6:20. It is one of the most searching passages in the letter. The listeners have been believers long enough to be teaching others. Instead they need to be taught the ABCs again. They are like infants who still need milk when they ought to be eating solid food. The author will not leave them there. He will press on to the “solid food” of Christ’s Melchizedek priesthood in chapter 7, and he will invite them to come along (6:1).

Structure.

  • 5:1–10: A high priest appointed by God. This concludes the section that began at 4:14. Its two halves mirror each other. The general qualifications of any high priest (5:1–4) are matched by Christ’s fulfillment of them (5:5–10). The section ends with God’s formal designation of Jesus as “high priest according to the order of Melchizedek” (5:10).
  • 5:11–14: From milk to solid food. This is the opening rebuke of a longer exhortation (5:11–6:12). The word “sluggish” (νωθροί nōthroi) frames the whole unit (5:11; 6:12).

Key words to watch.

  • High priest (ἀρχιερεύς archiereus). It appears at the start (5:1), at the turning point (5:5), and at the climax (5:10). Thus Jesus is set within, and then above, the class of “every high priest.”
  • Offer (προσφέρω prospherō). This is the technical verb for bringing a sacrifice to the altar (5:1, 3). Strikingly, the author uses it also of Jesus’ prayers in Gethsemane (5:7).
  • Weakness (ἀσθένεια astheneia). Jesus sympathizes with our “weaknesses” (4:15). The human high priest is “clothed with weakness” (5:2). Christ entered into weakness without sin.
  • Obedience and obey (ὑπακοή hypakoē, ὑπακούω hypakouō). The Son “learned obedience” (5:8). He saves “all who obey him” (5:9). Both words are built on the root “hear,” which ties them to the warning about being “sluggish in hearing” (5:11).
  • Perfect and mature (τελειόω teleioō, τέλειος teleios). Christ was “made perfect” (5:9). Solid food is for the “mature” (5:14). In 6:1 the readers are urged on toward “maturity.” The Son’s completed course is the pattern for the church’s growth.

What to watch for. Hebrews 5 holds together two things we often keep apart. One is the glory of Christ’s appointment by God. The other is the reality of his human suffering. The Son of God wept, cried out, and learned obedience through what he suffered. That is the very thing that makes him able to help us. Then the chapter turns the same searching light on the hearers. The Son was trained through suffering for his office. Believers, too, must be “trained by practice” if they are to grow up into him (5:14). The high priest who was made perfect does not leave his people as infants.
