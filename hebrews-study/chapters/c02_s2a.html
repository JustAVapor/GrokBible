The Pioneer of Salvation, Made Like His Brothers (2:5–18)

Translation

5For it was not to angels that he subjected the world to come, about which we are speaking. 6But someone has solemnly testified somewhere, saying,

“What is man, that you remember him,
or the son of man, that you care for him?
7You made him for a little while lower than the angels;
with glory and honor you crowned him;
8all things you subjected beneath his feet.”

For in subjecting all things to him, he left nothing that is not subject to him. But now we do not yet see all things subjected to him. 9But we do see the one who for a little while was made lower than the angels—Jesus—crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone.

10For it was fitting for him, for whom are all things and through whom are all things, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings. 11For the one who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all from one. That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers, 12saying,

“I will proclaim your name to my brothers;
in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise.”

13And again,

“I myself will put my trust in him.”

And again,

“Here am I, and the children God has given me.”

14Since, then, the children share in blood and flesh, he himself likewise partook of the same things, so that through death he might render powerless the one who has the power of death—that is, the devil— 15and set free all those who through fear of death were held in slavery all their lives. 16For surely it is not angels that he takes hold of; rather, he takes hold of the seed of Abraham. 17Therefore he was obligated to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the things that pertain to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people. 18For because he himself has suffered when he was tested, he is able to help those who are being tested.

Setting and Structure

With the warning of 2:1–4 complete, the author returns to his exposition. The connecting “for” of 2:5 shows that the argument of chapter 1 is resuming: the subject is still the Son and the angels, and still the “salvation” that the angels serve (1:14; 2:3). But the angle now changes completely. Chapter 1 showed the Son exalted above the angels. This section shows him, for a little while, made lower than the angels. Chapter 1 was full of royal and divine titles; this section is full of words of weakness and kinship: flesh and blood, sufferings, death, fear, slavery, brothers, children, testing. The author is not contradicting himself. He is showing the path by which the eternal Son came to sit at God’s right hand as our representative—the path of humiliation that led to exaltation.

The section has three movements:

  • 2:5–9: Psalm 8 and Jesus. The coming world was not subjected to angels but, according to Psalm 8, to “man” and “the son of man.” The author quotes the psalm (2:6–8a) and then comments on it (2:8b–9). God’s purpose was to subject all things to humanity, yet we do not see it fulfilled. What we do see is Jesus, who for a little while was made lower than the angels and is now crowned with glory and honor because he suffered death for everyone.
  • 2:10–13: The pioneer and his brothers. It was fitting for God to perfect the “pioneer” of salvation through sufferings, because he was leading many sons to glory. The Son and those he sanctifies share a common origin, and so he gladly calls them brothers. Three Old Testament quotations, placed on his own lips, prove it (Ps 22:22; Isa 8:17, 18).
  • 2:14–18: Why he shared our nature. Because the children share flesh and blood, he shared it too, in order to destroy the devil’s power over death and set free those enslaved by fear (2:14–15). He takes hold not of angels but of Abraham’s seed (2:16). He had to be like his brothers in every respect, so that he could become a merciful and faithful high priest who makes propitiation for sins (2:17) and helps those who are tested (2:18).

The word “angels” brackets the section: “not to angels” (2:5) and “not angels” (2:16). Within that frame the author answers a question that his exalted portrait of the Son in chapter 1 might raise: if the Son is so great, why did he suffer and die? Wouldn’t the cross have seemed, to many in the ancient world, a disgrace that disproved any claim to divine status? The author answers that the suffering was not an embarrassment to be explained away but the very means by which God accomplished his purpose for humanity. The Son’s humiliation was “fitting” (2:10). It was necessary (2:17). And it is precisely what makes him able to help (2:18).

The section also performs an important transition in the book as a whole. It ends with the first mention of Jesus as “high priest” (2:17), introducing the theme that will dominate chapters 4–10. Almost every key idea of the later priestly argument is introduced here in seed form: Jesus’ solidarity with his people (4:15; 5:1–2), his perfection through suffering (5:8–9; 7:28), his sanctifying of the people (10:10, 14; 13:12), his dealing with sins (1:3; 9:26–28), his help in time of need (4:16), and his superiority to the angelic and Levitical orders. The author works like a composer who sounds every major theme in the overture before developing them one by one.

Two features of the author’s use of the Old Testament deserve notice. First, he reads Psalm 8 in the light of Psalm 110:1, which he quoted at the climax of chapter 1: “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet” (1:13). Both psalms speak of things placed under someone’s feet. Jewish interpreters often linked texts that shared a key word or phrase, and the early church did the same with these two psalms. Paul also reads them together (1 Cor 15:25–27; Eph 1:20–22). The connection allows the author to see in Psalm 8 not only a description of humanity’s original dignity but a prophecy of its fulfillment in the enthroned Messiah.

Second, in 2:12–13 he does something he will do again in 10:5–7: he places the words of Scripture in the mouth of the Son. Psalm 22 and Isaiah 8 are heard as the Son speaking to the Father about his brothers and his children. In chapter 1 the Father spoke to the Son; now the Son speaks of us. The conversation within God is the ground of our salvation.

The cultural setting also matters. In the honor-conscious world of the first century, to be identified with the lowly, the weak, and the condemned was shameful. A crucified man was the lowest of the low (compare 12:2, “despising the shame”). The readers themselves had experienced public shame for their faith (10:33, “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions”). They may well have felt the pull to distance themselves from a crucified Messiah and his despised followers. This section answers that pull with a breathtaking reversal. The exalted Son is “not ashamed” to call them brothers (2:11). If he is not ashamed of them, how can they be ashamed of him (compare 13:13, “let us go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach”)?

Finally, this section introduces the theme of Christ’s full humanity with a clarity unmatched in the New Testament. The Son of 1:3, who upholds all things, truly shared flesh and blood (2:14), was made like his brothers “in every respect” (2:17), suffered, was tested (2:18), and died (2:9). Hebrews holds together the full deity of chapter 1 and the full humanity of chapter 2 without dissolving either. Both are necessary for him to be the mediator and high priest that sinners need.

Verse by Verse

2:5

For it was not to angels that he subjected the world to come, about which we are speaking.

The word “for” (γάρ gar) links this verse to what precedes it. The salvation that must not be neglected (2:3), the salvation that the angels serve (1:14), belongs to “the world to come”—and that world has not been placed under angels. The author is giving one more reason why the Son’s word outweighs the angels’ word: the future belongs not to them but to him, and to the humanity he represents.

The Greek word order is emphatic: “For not to angels did he subject …” The negative and the word “angels” stand first. The author is correcting an assumption, or at least anticipating a question. If the angels were the mediators of the law and the ministers of God’s rule in this age, might they not also rule the age to come? No. God has another purpose.

The verb “subjected” (ὑπέταξεν hypetaxen, from ὑποτάσσω hypotassō, “to place under, to put in subjection”) introduces the key word of the next four verses. It appears four times in 2:5–8, drawn from the last line of the psalm the author is about to quote: “all things you subjected beneath his feet.” The subject of the verb is God, unnamed but obvious. The aorist tense refers to God’s decision, the decree by which he determined who would rule the coming world.

Word Study: τὴν οἰκουμένην τὴν μέλλουσαν tēn oikoumenēn tēn mellousan

οἰκουμένη oikoumenē means “the inhabited world,” the world as the place where people live. In ordinary usage it often meant the Roman Empire (Luke 2:1, “a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the inhabited world should be registered”). The author has already used it in 1:6: “when he again brings the firstborn into the oikoumenē.” There, as here, it refers not to the present world but to the world into which the risen and exalted Son has entered, the world of his enthronement and rule.

The participle μέλλουσαν mellousan, from μέλλω mellō, means “about to be, coming.” Hebrews uses this word repeatedly for the realities that belong to the new age: those “who are about to inherit salvation” (1:14), “the powers of the age to come” (6:5), “the good things that have come” or “are to come” (9:11; 10:1), “the coming judgment” (10:27), and “the city that is to come” (13:14). The “world to come” is therefore not a separate topic from salvation. It is the sphere of salvation, the new creation into which the many sons are being led (2:10) and the rest that remains for the people of God (4:9).

Yet the coming world is not purely future. The readers had already “tasted … the powers of the age to come” (6:5), and they had already “come to Mount Zion … the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22). The world to come has broken into the present through the exaltation of the Son and the gift of the Spirit, though its full manifestation awaits his return (9:28).

Why mention angels at all in relation to the coming world? The author may be reflecting a widespread Jewish understanding that God administered the nations of the present age through angelic powers. The Song of Moses says that when the Most High gave the nations their inheritance, “he fixed the boundaries of the peoples” according to a certain number. The Masoretic Text reads: יַצֵּב גְּבֻלֹת עַמִּים לְמִסְפַּר בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל yatstsev gevulot ‘ammim lemispar benei Yisra’el, “he fixed the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of the sons of Israel” (Deut 32:8). But a Hebrew manuscript of Deuteronomy found at Qumran (4QDeutj) reads “sons of God,” and the Greek translation speaks of “angels of God.” In that reading, each nation was assigned to an angelic guardian, while Israel was the Lord’s own portion (Deut 32:9). Sirach reflects the same idea: God appointed a ruler over every nation, but Israel is the Lord’s portion (Sir 17:17). The book of Daniel speaks of the angelic “prince of Persia” and “prince of Greece” and of Michael, “your prince,” the guardian of Israel (Dan 10:13, 20–21; 12:1).

Whatever role angels have in this age, the author insists that the age to come will be different. It will not be ruled by angels but by humanity—specifically, by the Son who became man and by those he brings to glory. This agrees with Daniel’s vision, where the kingdom is given to “one like a son of man” and to “the holy ones of the Most High” (Dan 7:13–14, 18, 27), and with Paul’s startling statement that the saints will judge angels (1 Cor 6:3). God’s original purpose for humanity, stated at creation, was dominion: “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness, and let them have dominion” (וְיִרְדּוּ veyirdu, “and let them rule,” Gen 1:26). That purpose has not been canceled by sin. It will be fulfilled in the coming world.

The phrase “about which we are speaking” (περὶ ἧς λαλοῦμεν peri hēs laloumen) is a reminder that the coming world has been the author’s subject from the beginning. The enthroned Son, the inheritance, the salvation—all belong to it. The present tense “we are speaking” may also gently remind the readers that the preacher himself is a voice through whom this word now comes to them (compare 13:22).

2:6

But someone has solemnly testified somewhere, saying, “What is man, that you remember him, or the son of man, that you care for him?

The introduction to the quotation sounds strangely vague: “someone … somewhere.” The author certainly knew that he was quoting Psalm 8, a psalm of David. Why, then, does he introduce it so indefinitely? The same kind of formula appears in 4:4 (“he has spoken somewhere about the seventh day”), and Philo of Alexandria sometimes introduces quotations in a similar way. The vagueness is not ignorance but a matter of emphasis. For the author of Hebrews, the decisive speaker in Scripture is God. He introduces the Old Testament again and again with “he says,” “the Holy Spirit says” (3:7), or “God spoke” (1:1). The human writer is of secondary interest. Moreover, the readers knew the psalm well; the author can assume that they will recognize it.

The verb is weighty: διεμαρτύρατο diemartyrato, from διαμαρτύρομαι diamartyromai, “to testify solemnly, to bear earnest witness.” It is the language of formal testimony (Acts 2:40; 20:21, 24; 1 Tim 5:21). It echoes the “witness” of 2:4. The God who bore witness through signs and wonders also bore witness in the psalm concerning humanity’s destiny.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 8:4 (Hebrew 8:5)

Hebrew: מָה־אֱנוֹשׁ כִּי־תִזְכְּרֶנּוּ וּבֶן־אָדָם כִּי תִפְקְדֶנּוּ — mah-’enosh ki-tizkerennu uven-’adam ki tiphqedennu, “What is mortal man, that you remember him, and a son of man, that you attend to him?”

The psalm opens and closes with the same exclamation: יְהוָה אֲדֹנֵינוּ מָה־אַדִּיר שִׁמְךָ בְּכָל־הָאָרֶץ YHWH ’adoneinu mah-’addir shimka bekhol-ha’arets, “O Lord, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth!” (Ps 8:1, 9; Hebrew 8:2, 10). Between these bookends the psalmist looks up at the night sky: “When I see your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars that you have set in place” (Ps 8:3; Hebrew 8:4). Against the vastness of the heavens, humanity seems tiny. Why should the Creator of the stars pay attention to such a creature?

The two Hebrew words for humanity are significant. אֱנוֹשׁ ’enosh often stresses human frailty and mortality: “As for mortal man, his days are like grass” (אֱנוֹשׁ כֶּחָצִיר יָמָיו ’enosh kechatsir yamav, Ps 103:15). בֶן־אָדָם ven-’adam, “son of man” or “son of Adam,” means simply “a human being,” one who belongs to the race of Adam. In Hebrew poetry the second line usually restates the first in other words, so “man” and “son of man” refer to the same subject: humanity.

The two verbs are also rich. זָכַר zakar, “remember,” means more than recall; when God “remembers,” he acts in faithful care (Gen 8:1, “God remembered Noah,” and the waters receded). פָּקַד paqad, “attend to, visit,” is used when God intervenes in someone’s life, whether for blessing or judgment: “the Lord visited Sarah as he had said” (Gen 21:1), and she bore Isaac. The Greek translation, which Hebrews follows exactly here, renders these verbs with μιμνῄσκῃ mimnēskē, “you remember,” and ἐπισκέπτῃ episkeptē, “you look upon, visit, care for.” The latter word is used in the New Testament of God’s saving visitation: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has visited and redeemed his people” (Luke 1:68).

The question “What is man?” is not cynical. Elsewhere a similar question can express humanity’s smallness and transience (Ps 144:3–4; Job 7:17–18). But in Psalm 8 it is an exclamation of wonder. The answer the psalm gives is astonishing: this small creature has been crowned with glory and given dominion over the works of God’s hands. The psalm is a poetic meditation on Genesis 1:26–28, where God made humanity in his image and gave it dominion over the fish, the birds, the livestock, and all the earth.

What about the phrase “son of man”? The reader of the Gospels cannot help noticing that “the Son of Man” was Jesus’ favorite way of referring to himself (for example, Mark 2:10; 8:31; 10:45; 14:62), drawing on the vision of “one like a son of man” who receives an everlasting kingdom (Dan 7:13–14). Did the author of Hebrews intend that echo? He never uses “Son of Man” as a title anywhere in the book, and here the phrase lacks the definite articles that mark the Gospel title. In the psalm it plainly means “a human being.” The author’s point in the next verses is that Psalm 8 describes the destiny of humanity—a destiny that we do not see fulfilled in humanity at large but do see fulfilled in Jesus. It is quite possible that the author and his readers heard an overtone of Jesus’ self-designation; Christians who knew the Gospel tradition could hardly miss it. But the argument does not depend on it. Jesus fulfills Psalm 8 not because the psalm uses a title but because he is the true man who achieves what God intended for humanity.

The question of the psalm, then, becomes the question of the whole section. What is humanity, that God should be mindful of it? Hebrews answers by pointing to the incarnation. God has “remembered” and “visited” humanity in the most astonishing way possible: the Son himself has become a son of man, sharing flesh and blood with the children (2:14), in order to bring them to the glory for which they were made (2:10).

2:7

You made him for a little while lower than the angels; with glory and honor you crowned him;

The psalm now describes humanity’s position in God’s order: below the heavenly beings, yet crowned as king over creation. This line raises two questions that matter greatly for how Hebrews uses the psalm: who are the beings humanity is “lower than,” and what does “a little” mean?

Old Testament Text: Psalm 8:5 (Hebrew 8:6)

Hebrew: וַתְּחַסְּרֵהוּ מְּעַט מֵאֱלֹהִים וְכָבוֹד וְהָדָר תְּעַטְּרֵהוּ — vattechasserehu me‘at me’elohim vekhavod vehadar te‘atterehu, “You made him lack a little from ’elohim, and with glory and majesty you crown him.”

Greek (as quoted in Hebrews, following the Septuagint): ἠλάττωσας αὐτὸν βραχύ τι παρ’ ἀγγέλους, δόξῃ καὶ τιμῇ ἐστεφάνωσας αὐτόν — ēlattōsas auton brachy ti par’ angelous, doxē kai timē estephanōsas auton, “You made him for a little while (or: a little) lower than the angels; with glory and honor you crowned him.”

“Elohim” and “angels.” The Hebrew word אֱלֹהִים ’elohim is grammatically plural. It is the ordinary word for “God” (the one true God, with singular verbs), but it can also mean “gods” or, in some contexts, heavenly beings who belong to God’s court (Ps 82:1, 6; compare Ps 97:7, where “all ’elohim” are called to worship the Lord). The phrase מֵאֱלֹהִים me’elohim can thus be read “than God” or “than the heavenly beings.” Many modern translations take it as “than God,” seeing an allusion to humanity’s creation in God’s image. The Greek translators took it as “than the angels,” and the ancient Aramaic paraphrase (Targum) of the Psalms and later Jewish interpreters understood it similarly. Both readings are linguistically possible, and they do not conflict in substance: humanity ranks just below the heavenly realm, whether that realm is named by its Lord or by its inhabitants. Created in God’s image, humanity was nevertheless a creature of earth, mortal and lower in rank than the spirits who serve in God’s presence.

Hebrews follows the Greek. This was the text the readers knew, and it suited the argument the author had been developing since 1:4: the relation of the Son to the angels. The author does not build anything on a mistranslation. His point—that the Son entered a position lower than that of the heavenly beings—holds whether one reads “God” or “angels.” If anything, the Hebrew “than God” makes the humiliation even more striking: the one who is himself God, the radiance of God’s glory (1:3), entered the condition of the creature who is lower than God.

Word Study: βραχύ τι brachy ti, “a little” or “for a little while”

The Hebrew מְעַט me‘at means “a little, a few.” In Psalm 8 it most naturally refers to degree: humanity is only a little lower than ’elohim—so great is humanity’s dignity. Yet me‘at can also refer to time: וְעוֹד מְעַט וְאֵין רָשָׁע ve‘od me‘at ve’ein rasha‘, “Yet a little while, and the wicked will be no more” (Ps 37:10).

The Greek βραχύ τι brachy ti has the same double possibility. It can mean “a little” in degree, but it can also mean “for a short time.” Luke uses brachy in a temporal sense: Gamaliel ordered the apostles to be put outside “for a little while” (Acts 5:34), and Peter was challenged “after a little while” (Luke 22:58).

In 2:9 the author applies the phrase to Jesus, and there the temporal sense is almost certainly what he intends. Jesus was made lower than the angels for a limited time—the time of his earthly life and death—and is now crowned with glory and honor. The humiliation was real but temporary; the exaltation is permanent. For this reason this translation renders the phrase “for a little while” in both 2:7 and 2:9, so that the reader can see the connection the author draws. The psalmist was speaking of humanity’s high place in creation, only a little below the heavenly beings. The author of Hebrews, reading the psalm in the light of its fulfillment, hears in its words the story of the Son, whose lowering was brief and whose crowning is forever. The two senses are not at war. The very words that marked humanity’s dignity now mark the brevity of the Son’s humiliation on the way to exaltation.

The second half of the verse describes humanity’s royal status: “with glory and honor you crowned him.” The Hebrew pair is כָבוֹד וְהָדָר kavod vehadar, “glory and majesty.” Kavod carries the root sense of “weight,” what gives a person substance and importance; it is the word used for the visible glory of God that filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34). Hadar means “splendor, majesty,” and is often used of royal majesty (Ps 21:5; 45:3–4). The Greek renders these as δόξῃ καὶ τιμῇ doxē kai timē, “glory and honor.” The verb “crowned” (ἐστεφάνωσας estephanōsas, from stephanoō; Hebrew עָטַר ‘atar) evokes a king’s coronation or a victor’s wreath. Humanity was created to reign as God’s vice-regent over creation.

In the ancient world, “glory and honor” were the most prized of goods. People competed for them, and the public estimate of a person’s worth shaped his entire life. The psalm declares that God himself has conferred honor on humanity—not by human competition but by divine gift. The readers of Hebrews, who had lost honor in their society by confessing Christ (10:33), needed to hear that true glory and honor come from God and are given to those who belong to his Son (2:10).

Textual Note

After “you crowned him,” a number of important manuscripts (including Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Alexandrinus, and Codex Ephraemi) add the next line of the psalm: “and you set him over the works of your hands.” Other weighty witnesses, including the early papyrus P46 and Codex Vaticanus, lack it. The SBL Greek New Testament and the Nestle-Aland edition print the shorter text. Metzger explains that the committee, giving weight to the combination of P46 and Vaticanus, regarded the longer reading as a probable scribal expansion, made to complete the quotation from the familiar psalm. The meaning of the passage is unaffected: the author’s argument rests on the final line, “all things you subjected beneath his feet,” which all manuscripts include.

2:8

all things you subjected beneath his feet.” For in subjecting all things to him, he left nothing that is not subject to him. But now we do not yet see all things subjected to him.

The quotation ends with the line on which the author’s whole argument turns: “all things you subjected beneath his feet” (πάντα ὑπέταξας ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν αὐτοῦ panta hypetaxas hypokatō tōn podōn autou). The verb is the same one the author used in 2:5 (“he subjected”), and this is why he quoted the psalm: it tells us to whom God has subjected the coming world.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 8:6 (Hebrew 8:7)

Hebrew: תַּמְשִׁילֵהוּ בְּמַעֲשֵׂי יָדֶיךָ כֹּל שַׁתָּה תַחַת־רַגְלָיו — tamshilehu bema‘asei yadeka, kol shattah tachat-raglav, “You make him rule over the works of your hands; all things you have put under his feet.”

In the psalm, the “all things” are then spelled out: sheep and oxen, the beasts of the field, the birds of the heavens, the fish of the sea (Ps 8:7–8; Hebrew 8:8–9). This is the dominion of Genesis 1:26–28. The Hebrew verb שִׁית shit, “to set, to put,” is rendered in the Greek by ὑπέταξας hypetaxas, “you subjected.” To have something “under one’s feet” was a vivid ancient image of total authority and conquest; conquerors placed their feet on the necks of defeated kings (Josh 10:24), and Psalm 110:1, which Hebrews quoted in 1:13, speaks of God making the king’s enemies “a footstool for your feet.”

The author now comments on the psalm with careful logic. “For in subjecting all things to him, he left nothing that is not subject to him.” The construction ἐν … τῷ ὑποτάξαι en … tō hypotaxai, “in the subjecting,” uses an articular infinitive to mean “when he subjected” or “by subjecting.” The adjective ἀνυπότακτον anypotakton means “not subjected, outside control” (it is used of “unruly” people in Titus 1:6, 10). The author presses the word “all.” In the psalm the immediate examples are animals, but the author reads “all things” as truly all-encompassing. Nothing in creation—including the world to come (2:5)—is excluded from the dominion God intends. Paul reads the same psalm in the same comprehensive way, adding only the obvious exception: “When it says ‘all things are subjected,’ it is plain that he is excepted who subjected all things to him” (1 Cor 15:27). God himself is not subject to the one he has crowned.

Then comes the honest admission: “But now we do not yet see all things subjected to him.” The phrase νῦν δέ nyn de, “but now,” means “as things actually stand.” The word οὔπω oupō, “not yet,” is important. It does not say “never.” It acknowledges a gap between God’s declared purpose and present reality, while implying that the gap will be closed. The perfect participle ὑποτεταγμένα hypotetagmena, “having been subjected,” describes a completed state of subjection that we do not yet observe.

Anyone looking at the world can see the truth of this. Humanity was crowned to rule creation, yet humanity is ruled by death (2:14–15). Creation itself, as Paul says, “was subjected to futility” and groans in bondage to decay (Rom 8:20–22). The readers saw it in their own lives: they were not reigning but suffering—abused, imprisoned, stripped of property (10:32–34). If God promised humanity glory and dominion, where is it?

Interpretive Options: Who Is “Him” in 2:8b?

  • Humanity. The “him” of “we do not yet see all things subjected to him” is humanity, the subject of the psalm. The author observes that the psalm’s promise of universal dominion is not yet fulfilled in the human race. This reading gives full force to the strong contrast that opens verse 9: “But we do see … Jesus.” What we do not see in humanity, we do see in Jesus. Westcott and many later interpreters read the verse in this way.
  • Jesus. The “him” is already Jesus, the one in whom the psalm is fulfilled. Even the exalted Christ does not yet see all things subjected to him, for he is “waiting from that time until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet” (10:13; compare 1 Cor 15:25). The admission would then concern the present stage of Christ’s own reign: he is crowned, but his rule is not yet universally acknowledged.

The first view fits the flow of thought best. Verse 8b and verse 9 are set against each other by “but … but” (δέ … δέ de … de): “but now we do not yet see … but we do see Jesus.” The contrast is sharpest if the first “him” is humanity in general and the second figure is the one man in whom humanity’s destiny is realized. Yet the two views finally converge. Humanity’s dominion will come only in and through Jesus, and Jesus’ dominion will be complete only when he brings the “many sons” to share his glory (2:10). The psalm’s promise to humanity and Christ’s rule are not two different stories but one.

This verse is a model of honest faith. The author does not pretend that the world looks the way God has promised it will. He does not ask his readers to deny their sufferings or to imagine that they already reign. He admits the “not yet.” But he refuses to let what we do not see cancel what we do see. Faith, as he will later say, is “the conviction of things not seen” (11:1). The next verse tells us where faith looks when the evidence of the world seems to contradict the promises of God.

2:9

But we do see the one who for a little while was made lower than the angels—Jesus—crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone.

This is one of the great turning points of Hebrews. “We do not yet see all things subjected to him. But we do see …” What we see is not the fulfillment of Psalm 8 in humanity at large, but its fulfillment in one man. The author has quoted the psalm and admitted the gap between promise and reality. Now he points to the one in whom the gap has already been closed.

The word order in Greek is carefully built to create suspense. Literally: “But the one who for a little while was made lower than the angels we see—Jesus—because of the suffering of death with glory and honor crowned.” The description comes first, drawn word for word from the psalm (“for a little while … lower than the angels”); then the verb “we see”; and only then the name, Ἰησοῦν Iēsoun, “Jesus.” This is the first time the name appears in Hebrews. Up to this point the author has spoken of “the Son” and “the Lord.” Now, just where he speaks of being made lower, of suffering, and of death, he uses the human name given at his birth, the name of the man from Nazareth who was crucified. The author uses the bare name “Jesus” at key moments throughout the book, often at the end of a clause for emphasis (3:1; 4:14; 6:20; 7:22; 10:19; 12:2, 24; 13:12, 20). The one who is the eternal Son is also this particular man, with a human name and a human history.

“We see” (βλέπομεν blepomen) here does not mean physical sight; the readers had never seen Jesus with their eyes (2:3). It is the seeing of faith, which grasps what God has revealed in the gospel. In 2:8 the author used ὁρῶμεν horōmen for “we do not yet see”; here he uses blepomen. The two verbs overlap in meaning, and the change is likely stylistic. The point lies in the contrast of what is seen. We do not see humanity reigning; we do see Jesus crowned. Later the author will urge his readers to “consider Jesus” (3:1) and to run their race “looking to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2). Christian endurance depends on keeping him in view.

Two perfect participles describe Jesus. He is the one “made lower” (ἠλαττωμένον ēlattōmenon) and the one “crowned” (ἐστεφανωμένον estephanōmenon). The Greek perfect tense describes a completed action with continuing results. The incarnation is not a temporary disguise that was later discarded; the Son remains truly human, now exalted. And the crowning is not a hope but an accomplished fact with lasting effect: he has been crowned and he remains crowned. This is what chapter 1 described in the language of Psalm 110: he “sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high” (1:3, 13). The coronation of Psalm 8 and the enthronement of Psalm 110 are the same event. The man Jesus, as humanity’s representative, now occupies the place of glory and honor that God intended for humanity.

“For a little while” is now clearly temporal. Jesus’ humiliation lasted for the span of his earthly life, climaxing in his death. It was not permanent. Westcott and many others have drawn out the point that the psalm’s description of humanity’s lowly position applies to the Son only as a brief and voluntary stage—the one through whom God made the universe (1:2) stooped for a time below the angels whom he had made.

“Because of the suffering of death”

The phrase διὰ τὸ πάθημα τοῦ θανάτου dia to pathēma tou thanatou means “because of the suffering of death.” The preposition διά dia with the accusative case normally gives a reason: “on account of, because of.” In the Greek order the phrase stands between “Jesus” and “crowned,” and it most naturally explains the crowning: Jesus was crowned with glory and honor because he suffered death. This is the same logic Paul uses: “he humbled himself, becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God highly exalted him” (Phil 2:8–9). Hebrews will say it again: “for the joy set before him he endured the cross, despising the shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God” (12:2). The crown was not given in spite of the cross but because of it. His exaltation is the Father’s vindication and reward of his obedient suffering.

Some interpreters have connected the phrase with “made lower” instead: Jesus was made lower than the angels “for the purpose of” suffering death. This is theologically true—the Son became man in order to die (2:14)—but it requires reading dia with the accusative in an unusual sense, and it goes against the natural word order. The phrase “the suffering of death” means “the suffering that consisted in death,” or “the suffering that death involves.” The singular πάθημα pathēma, “suffering,” prepares for the plural παθημάτων pathēmatōn, “sufferings,” in 2:10.

“So that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone”

The final clause, introduced by ὅπως hopōs, “so that, in order that,” states a purpose. Grammatically it is somewhat awkward, since it follows the crowning but speaks of the death. How can the crowning have the purpose of the death? Most interpreters understand that the clause states the purpose of the whole movement just described: Jesus was made lower, suffered, and was crowned, so that his death might be a death “for everyone.” The crowning shows that his death was not a defeat but an effective, accepted sacrifice. A death that God vindicated by exaltation is a death that avails for others. The clause thus looks back to the suffering and forward to its saving result.

“Taste death” (γεύσηται θανάτου geusētai thanatou) is a Semitic idiom meaning “to experience death,” “to die.” Jesus used it: “there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God” (Mark 9:1; compare John 8:52). The phrase does not mean that Jesus only sampled death or took a little sip of it. It means that he experienced death in its full reality, with all its bitterness. The aorist tense views his death as a single, decisive event. Hebrews insists that Jesus really died, and that his death was the experience of the curse of death that lies on humanity because of sin (2:14–15; compare Gen 2:17; Rom 5:12).

Textual Note: “By the Grace of God” or “Apart from God”?

At this point the manuscripts of Hebrews present one of the most famous variants in the New Testament. The great majority of witnesses read χάριτι θεοῦ chariti theou, “by the grace of God.” This reading is found in the earliest papyrus of Hebrews (P46), in the major uncial manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Vaticanus, Ephraemi, Claromontanus, and others), and in nearly all later manuscripts and early versions. It is the reading printed in the Nestle-Aland and SBL editions, and it is followed in this translation.

A small number of witnesses read χωρὶς θεοῦ chōris theou, “apart from God” or “without God.” Its Greek manuscript support is very slight (a few minuscules, notably the original hand of manuscript 1739, a copy that preserves an old form of the text, and 0243), together with some manuscripts of the Latin Vulgate and of the Syriac Peshitta. But it was known to several early church writers. Origen, in the third century, mentions both readings. In the fourth and fifth centuries the reading “apart from God” was favored by theologians of the Antiochene tradition, including Theodore of Mopsuestia, who used it to emphasize that Christ suffered death in his human nature, apart from his divinity. Ambrose and Jerome were also aware of it.

How did the variant arise? Bruce Metzger, explaining the reasoning of the editorial committee of the United Bible Societies’ Greek New Testament, suggests two possibilities. First, a scribe may simply have misread the words: in the capital letters of ancient manuscripts, ΧΑΡΙΤΙ (“by grace”) and ΧΩΡΙΣ (“apart from”) look somewhat alike. Second, and perhaps more likely, a reader may have written “apart from God” in the margin as a comment on 2:8, “he left nothing that is not subject to him,” to note that God himself is the exception (exactly the point Paul makes in 1 Cor 15:27). A later copyist could then have taken the marginal note as a correction belonging in the text and inserted it in the nearest suitable place, 2:9.

A minority of modern scholars have argued that “apart from God” is original, on the principle that the harder reading is more likely to have been changed by scribes than created by them. On this view the phrase might mean that Jesus died abandoned by God (compare Ps 22:1, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”—a psalm quoted in 2:12), or that he died for everyone and everything “except God.” The suggestion is thought-provoking, but it does not persuade. The principle of the harder reading cannot override overwhelmingly early and widespread manuscript evidence, and a reading created by a misreading or a misplaced marginal note would naturally be “harder” than the original. Moreover, “grace” suits Hebrews well. The author speaks of “the throne of grace” (4:16), “the Spirit of grace” (10:29), falling short of “the grace of God” (12:15), hearts strengthened “by grace” (13:9), and he closes with “Grace be with all of you” (13:25). And the very next verse explains Jesus’ death as an expression of God’s own fitting purpose (2:10). The death of Jesus did not happen apart from God’s will; it was the gift of God’s grace.

“By the grace of God” therefore identifies the source of Jesus’ death for others. His death was not an accident of history, nor a tragic failure, nor merely the act of wicked men (though wicked men did put him to death, Acts 2:23). It was God’s gracious provision. The Father did not need to be persuaded to love sinners by the death of his Son; the death of his Son was the expression of his love. “God so loved the world that he gave his only Son” (John 3:16); “God shows his own love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Rom 5:8). When the author later says that Christ makes “propitiation” for sins (2:17), this verse guards against misunderstanding: propitiation in Scripture is not a human gift that changes a reluctant God, but God’s own gracious provision to deal with sin.

Finally, Jesus tasted death “for everyone” (ὑπὲρ παντός hyper pantos). The preposition ὑπέρ hyper, “on behalf of, for the sake of,” is the regular New Testament word for Christ’s death for others (Mark 14:24; Rom 5:6–8; 1 Cor 15:3; Gal 3:13; 1 Pet 3:18). In many of these contexts it carries the sense of substitution: he died in the place of those for whom he died. Hebrews will develop this when it describes Christ as bearing the sins of many (9:28) and offering himself as a sacrifice for sins (10:12).

The word παντός pantos is singular. It could be neuter (“for everything”) or masculine (“for everyone”). A few ancient interpreters, notably Origen, took it as neuter, so that Christ’s death benefits the whole creation. But the context speaks consistently of persons: the “many sons” (2:10), the “brothers” (2:11, 12, 17), the “children” (2:13, 14), “the seed of Abraham” (2:16), “the people” (2:17). Most interpreters therefore take it as masculine. The singular “everyone,” rather than the plural “all,” draws attention to each individual. Jesus did not die for an abstraction called humanity but for persons, each one.

Christians have long debated how the “everyone” of this verse relates to the wider question of the extent of the atonement. Some take it as a statement that Christ died for every human being without exception, comparing texts such as 2 Cor 5:14–15 and 1 Tim 2:6. Others, such as John Owen, note that the immediate context defines those for whom Christ died as the sons he brings to glory, the brothers he sanctifies, and the children God gave him, and so read “everyone” as “every one of them.” Whatever position one takes on that wider question, the text itself makes several things clear. Jesus’ death was for human beings rather than for angels (2:16). It was a death that reaches individuals, not merely a general demonstration. It was rooted in the grace of God. And it is sufficient for everyone who comes to God through him (7:25). The gospel of this death is to be proclaimed to all, and no one who comes will be turned away.

This verse is the center of the chapter. It holds together everything that will follow: the Son’s humiliation and exaltation, his suffering and glory, his solidarity with humanity and his saving death. The readers, struggling under suffering and shame, are invited to look at Jesus. In him they see that suffering is not the end of the story. The path that God’s Son walked—from lowliness through death to glory—is the path on which he now leads his many brothers and sisters.
