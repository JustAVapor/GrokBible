5:5

So also the Christ did not glorify himself to become high priest. It was the one who said to him, “You are my Son; today I have begotten you,”

The word “so” (οὕτως houtōs) marks the turn from the general rule to its application. Everything said of “every high priest” in 5:1–4 will now be tested against Christ, beginning with the last point made, the divine call. The author uses the title “the Christ” (ὁ Χριστός ho Christos), with the article. That is, “the Messiah,” the Anointed One. The title fits, because Israel’s high priest was himself an anointed one (Lev 4:3, “the anointed priest”), and the psalm about to be quoted is addressed to God’s anointed king (Ps 2:2).

He did not glorify himself. The author has just called the priesthood “the honor” (τιμή timē, 5:4). Now he speaks of “glory”: οὐχ ἑαυτὸν ἐδόξασεν ouch heauton edoxasen, “he did not glorify himself.” The pairing of honor and glory recalls 2:7–9, where Jesus is “crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death,” and 3:3, where he is “counted worthy of more glory than Moses.” The verb δοξάζω doxazō here means “to confer honor on, to exalt to an honored position.” The infinitive γενηθῆναι genēthēnai, “to become,” states what the self-glorification would have consisted of: making himself high priest. Christ did not do that. The thought matches Jesus’ own words in the Gospel of John: “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing; it is my Father who glorifies me” (John 8:54). Throughout his ministry Jesus refused to seize honor. He did not grasp at equality with God as a prize to be exploited, but humbled himself (Phil 2:6–8). Therefore God exalted him.

But the one who said to him. The sentence in Greek is compressed. Literally: “but the one who spoke to him, ‘You are my Son …’.” We must supply the verb from the first half: “but the one who said to him … [glorified him].” The author does not name God directly. He identifies God by what God said. That is typical of Hebrews, which from its first line has presented God as the God who speaks (1:1–2). Christ’s appointment rests on a divine word.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 2:7

Hebrew: אֲסַפְּרָה אֶל חֹק יְהוָה אָמַר אֵלַי בְּנִי אַתָּה אֲנִי הַיּוֹם יְלִדְתִּיךָ — ’asapperah el choq YHWH ’amar elay beni attah, ani hayyom yelidtikha, “I will tell of the decree of the Lord: he said to me, ‘You are my son; I today have begotten you.’”

The Greek of Hebrews follows the Septuagint, which renders the Hebrew closely. The author already quoted this verse in 1:5 to show that the Son is superior to the angels. Note the word חֹק choq, “decree, statute.” In the psalm, God’s declaration of sonship is an official decree, a royal installation. The king of Israel speaks of the day the Lord enthroned him on Zion (Ps 2:6) and formally declared him his son, much as God had promised David concerning his offspring: “I will be to him a father, and he will be to me a son” (2 Sam 7:14; quoted in Heb 1:5). In its fullest sense, the psalm speaks of the Messiah. Its promise that the nations will be his inheritance and the ends of the earth his possession (Ps 2:8) exceeded anything any son of David achieved before Christ.

Why quote a sonship text? One might expect the author to prove Christ’s priesthood with a priestly text at once, and he will do so in 5:6. But he begins with Psalm 2:7, which speaks of sonship and kingship rather than priesthood. Why? There are three reasons.

First, the one who appointed Christ high priest is the same God who declared him Son. The author wants us to see that Christ’s priesthood is not a separate honor added to his sonship from outside. It flows from it. The Son who is “the radiance of God’s glory” (1:3) is the one whom God appoints to make purification for sins. In Hebrews, “Son” and “high priest” belong together: “Jesus the Son of God,” our “great high priest” (4:14); “the word of the oath … appoints a Son made perfect forever” (7:28).

Second, Psalm 2 and Psalm 110 belong together. Both are royal psalms addressed to the Lord’s anointed king. Both speak of his enthronement and his victory over the nations. The early church read them side by side as prophecies of Jesus’ exaltation (Acts 2:34–36; 4:25–27; 13:33). Hebrews itself joined them in chapter 1, where Psalm 2:7 opens the chain of quotations (1:5) and Psalm 110:1 closes it (1:13). By quoting Psalm 2:7 and then Psalm 110:4, the author shows that the same royal Son addressed in the one is the priest addressed in the other.

Third, sonship gives Christ’s priesthood its unique quality. Aaron’s priesthood rested on physical descent. Christ’s rests on the decree of God that declares him Son. Because he is the Son, his priesthood is “forever” (5:6; 7:3, 28).

Interpretive Options: When Is the “Today” of Psalm 2:7?

  • Eternity. Some church fathers, including Augustine, read “today” as the eternal present of God, in which the Son is eternally begotten of the Father.
  • The incarnation. Others connect “today” with Christ’s birth, especially since Hebrews 1:6 speaks of God bringing the firstborn into the world.
  • The resurrection and exaltation. The apostolic preaching applies Psalm 2:7 to the resurrection: God has fulfilled his promise “by raising Jesus, as also it is written in the second psalm, ‘You are my Son; today I have begotten you’” (Acts 13:33). Paul says that Jesus was “appointed Son of God in power … by the resurrection from the dead” (Rom 1:4).

In Hebrews the third view fits best, as many modern commentators agree. In 1:3–5 the quotation follows directly on Christ’s taking his seat at God’s right hand. Here, the appointment to priesthood is completed when the Son, “having been made perfect,” is “designated” high priest (5:9–10), which follows his suffering. This does not mean Jesus became the Son at the resurrection. Hebrews 1:2–3 makes clear that the Son was the agent of creation and the radiance of God’s glory before all things. Rather, the resurrection and enthronement are the public installation of the eternal Son in his messianic and priestly office. The Son who always was Son is now enthroned as Son, with all authority, in the body in which he suffered.

5:6

as he also says in another place, “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.”

Now comes the priestly text. “As he also says in another place” (καθὼς καὶ ἐν ἑτέρῳ λέγει kathōs kai en heterō legei) treats the two psalms as two utterances of the same speaker. The present tense λέγει legei, “he says,” is typical of Hebrews. Scripture is not merely what God once said. It is what God says now (compare 3:7, “as the Holy Spirit says”).

This is the first time Hebrews quotes Psalm 110:4. The first verse of the same psalm has already been used twice (1:3, 13), and it lies behind the picture of Christ seated at God’s right hand that runs through the whole sermon (8:1; 10:12; 12:2). But verse 4 is the author’s distinctive contribution. No other New Testament writer quotes it. It will become the controlling text of chapter 7, where the author will explain each phrase in turn: “priest,” “forever,” “according to the order of Melchizedek,” and the oath that introduces the verse (7:11–28).

Old Testament Text: Psalm 110:4

Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק — nishba‘ YHWH velo’ yinnachem, attah-khohen le‘olam ‘al-divrati Malki-tsedeq, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: ‘You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.’”

Hebrews quotes only the second half of the verse here. It saves the oath (“The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind”) for 7:20–22, where it becomes a key argument. The Greek of Hebrews follows the Septuagint closely, which faithfully represents the Hebrew in all essentials.

The phrase עַל־דִּבְרָתִי ‘al-divrati deserves comment. The noun divrah is related to davar, “word, matter,” and in the phrase ‘al-divrat it means “on account of” or “according to the manner of.” The final -i is most likely an old connecting vowel, found in poetic Hebrew, that links the word to the following name. That gives the sense “according to the order (or manner) of Melchizedek,” which is how the Greek translators understood it: κατὰ τὴν τάξιν Μελχισέδεκ kata tēn taxin Melchisedek. The Greek word τάξις taxis means “order, arrangement, rank, kind.” Some interpreters suggest that the -i is the pronoun “my,” so that the line would read “on account of my word, [O] Melchizedek,” or “for my sake, a righteous king.” That reading is grammatically possible. Even so, the main point is the same: the king addressed is installed by God’s sworn word as a priest of a kind that Melchizedek first displayed. The Septuagint rendering, which Hebrews follows, is a sound interpretation of the Hebrew.

The name מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק Malki-tsedeq itself means “my king is righteousness” or “king of righteousness,” as Hebrews will point out (7:2).

Who is Melchizedek? Melchizedek appears only twice in the Old Testament. In Genesis 14:18–20 he meets Abraham after the defeat of the four kings: וּמַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק מֶלֶךְ שָׁלֵם הוֹצִיא לֶחֶם וָיָיִן וְהוּא כֹהֵן לְאֵל עֶלְיוֹן uMalki-tsedeq melekh Shalem hotsi’ lechem vayayin vehu’ khohen le’el ‘elyon, “And Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine; and he was priest of God Most High” (Gen 14:18). He blessed Abraham, and Abraham gave him a tenth of everything. Then, about a thousand years later, he appears again in Psalm 110:4, in an oracle addressed to David’s Lord. That is all. Yet these two brief texts were enough to show that Scripture knows of a priesthood older than Aaron’s, joined with kingship, and not based on descent from Levi. In Melchizedek, king and priest were one person. Israel’s law kept those two offices strictly apart, as Uzziah learned (2 Chr 26:18). Psalm 110 announces that the coming king will hold both offices, as Melchizedek did.

Melchizedek also attracted interest in Jewish writings of the period. A fragmentary scroll from Qumran, known as 11QMelchizedek, presents him as a heavenly figure who brings liberation and judgment at the end of days. Philo treats him allegorically as the divine reason (Allegorical Interpretation 3.79–82). Hebrews, however, does not build on these speculations. As chapter 7 will show, the author argues strictly from what Genesis and the Psalm actually say, and from what they do not say. We will return to these matters there.

A priest forever. For now the author simply sets the quotation in place and lets two words resonate: “priest” and “forever.” The phrase εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna, “forever,” translates the Hebrew לְעוֹלָם le‘olam. Every Aaronic priest served until he died, and then another took his place. The priest of Psalm 110 holds his office permanently. The author will draw out this contrast in 7:23–25: “they were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing; but he, because he remains forever, holds his priesthood permanently. Therefore he is able to save completely those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them.” The psalm, quoted here almost in passing, contains the whole gospel of Hebrews in seed form.

5:7

In the days of his flesh he offered up both prayers and supplications, with a loud cry and tears, to the one who was able to save him out of death, and he was heard because of his reverence.

Having shown that Christ was called by God (5:5–6, matching 5:4), the author now shows that Christ truly shared the weakness of those he represents (matching 5:2–3). Few verses in the New Testament take us so deeply into the human experience of the Son of God.

Grammar and flow. Verses 7–10 form a single long sentence in Greek. It begins with the relative pronoun ὅς hos, “who,” referring back to Christ (5:5). Its main verbs are “he learned” (5:8) and “he became” (5:9). Everything else hangs on these as participles: “having offered” (5:7), “having been heard” (5:7), “being [a Son]” (5:8), “having been made perfect” (5:9), “having been designated” (5:10). In other words, the whole sentence leads from Christ’s prayers and suffering, through his learning obedience, to his becoming the source of salvation and his appointment as high priest. Suffering is not a detour on the way to his priesthood. It is the road.

In the days of his flesh. The phrase ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις τῆς σαρκὸς αὐτοῦ en tais hēmerais tēs sarkos autou refers to Jesus’ earthly life. “Flesh” (σάρξ sarx) here does not carry the sense of sinful human nature that it often has in Paul. It means human existence in its frailty and mortality, as in 2:14, where the children share “blood and flesh,” and Christ “likewise partook of the same things.” The phrase implies that those days are now over. Christ still has a true human nature, but he no longer lives in the condition of weakness and liability to death. He now “lives forever to intercede” (7:25). The author is looking back at a completed period of humiliation.

He offered prayers and supplications. The participle προσενέγκας prosenenkas, “having offered,” comes from προσφέρω prospherō, the very verb used of the high priest’s offering in 5:1 and 5:3. Its use here can hardly be accidental. The author presents Jesus’ prayers as something he brought to God, as a priest brings an offering. Unlike Aaron, Jesus offered nothing for his own sins, for he had none. But he did bring his own need, his anguish, and his submission before the Father. In this way his earthly prayers were part of his priestly self-offering, which reached its climax on the cross.

The two nouns intensify each other. δεήσεις deēseis (from δέησις deēsis) is the common word for “petitions, requests” arising from need. ἱκετηρίας hiketērias (from ἱκετηρία hiketēria) occurs only here in the New Testament. In classical Greek it first meant the olive branch, wrapped with wool, that a suppliant held out when begging for mercy at an altar or before a ruler. In time it came to mean the supplication itself. The word pictures Jesus as a suppliant, one who comes in utter dependence, pleading for help.

With a loud cry and tears. The phrase μετὰ κραυγῆς ἰσχυρᾶς καὶ δακρύων meta kraugēs ischyras kai dakryōn means “with a strong cry and tears.” Most interpreters rightly see a reference to Gethsemane. There Jesus was “greatly distressed and troubled” and said, “My soul is very sorrowful, even to death” (Mark 14:33–34). He fell to the ground and prayed that, if it were possible, the hour might pass from him: “Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will” (Mark 14:35–36). Luke adds that his sweat became like great drops of blood falling to the ground (Luke 22:44, though this verse is missing from some early manuscripts). The Gospels do not mention tears in Gethsemane, but they do record Jesus weeping elsewhere (Luke 19:41; John 11:35). The phrase may also embrace the cross, where Jesus “cried out with a loud voice, ‘My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?’” and finally “uttered a loud cry and breathed his last” (Mark 15:34, 37). Hebrews speaks generally of “the days of his flesh,” so it is wise not to limit the verse to a single scene. It describes the whole pattern of Jesus’ earthly prayer life under the shadow of the cross, which came to its sharpest point in Gethsemane and on Golgotha.

Old Testament Background: The Prayer of the Righteous Sufferer

The language of 5:7 echoes the Psalms, where the righteous sufferer cries to God for deliverance. The most important is Psalm 22, already quoted in Hebrews 2:12. It opens with the cry Jesus uttered on the cross: אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי רָחוֹק מִישׁוּעָתִי דִּבְרֵי שַׁאֲגָתִי Eli Eli lamah ‘azavtani, rachoq mishu‘ati divrey sha’agati, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Far from my salvation are the words of my roaring” (Ps 22:2 in Hebrew; 22:1 in English). Yet the same psalm turns to thanksgiving: וּבְשַׁוְּעוֹ אֵלָיו שָׁמֵעַ uveshav‘o elav shamea‘, “and when he cried to him for help, he heard” (Ps 22:25 in Hebrew; 22:24 in English). The sufferer who cried out is heard, and he goes on to praise God “in the midst of the congregation” (Ps 22:23 in Hebrew), the very words Hebrews puts on Jesus’ lips in 2:12.

Other psalms join tears and prayer. “Hear my prayer, O Lord, and give ear to my cry; do not be silent at my tears” (Ps 39:13 in Hebrew; 39:12 in English). And: כִּי חִלַּצְתָּ נַפְשִׁי מִמָּוֶת אֶת־עֵינִי מִן־דִּמְעָה ki chillatsta nafshi mimmavet, et-‘eyni min-dim‘ah, “for you have rescued my soul from death, my eyes from tears” (Ps 116:8). Jesus prayed as the true righteous sufferer of whom these psalms speak.

To the one able to save him out of death. Jesus addressed his prayers πρὸς τὸν δυνάμενον σῴζειν αὐτὸν ἐκ θανάτου pros ton dynamenon sōzein auton ek thanatou, “to the one able to save him out of death.” This is a description of God, and it is significant. Jesus prayed in faith to the God who has power over death. In Gethsemane he said, “All things are possible for you” (Mark 14:36). The preposition is ἐκ ek, “out of,” not ἀπό apo, “away from.” The difference should not be pressed too hard, since the two prepositions overlap in New Testament Greek. Still, “save out of death” fits naturally with the idea of being brought out from death after entering it, rather than being kept from dying in the first place. The author will later speak of “the God of peace who brought up from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep” (13:20).

Word Study: εὐλάβεια eulabeia

The noun comes from εὖ eu, “well,” and λαμβάνω lambanō, “take hold.” It originally meant caution or circumspection, “taking hold well” of a situation. In biblical and Jewish Greek it came to mean “reverence, godly fear,” the attitude of one who takes God seriously and submits to him. Hebrews uses the noun again in 12:28: “let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe.” It uses the related verb of Noah, who “moved with reverent fear” built the ark (11:7). In each case the word describes a positive religious virtue: humble, obedient reverence before God.

He was heard because of his reverence. This is the most debated phrase in the verse. The Greek is εἰσακουσθεὶς ἀπὸ τῆς εὐλαβείας eisakoustheis apo tēs eulabeias. The participle εἰσακουσθείς eisakoustheis, from εἰσακούω eisakouō, is the regular word in the Greek Bible for God “hearing” prayer in the sense of answering it (as in Luke 1:13: “your prayer has been heard”). Two questions arise. What does apo tēs eulabeias mean? And in what sense was Jesus heard, since he did die?

Interpretive Options: “Heard Because of His Reverence”

  • “Heard because of his reverent submission.” Here apo gives the cause, as it sometimes does in Hellenistic Greek, and eulabeia has its usual biblical sense of godly reverence. This is the view of most modern commentators, including Bruce, Lane and Attridge, and of many older interpreters as well. It matches Jesus’ prayer in Gethsemane, which ended in submission: “not what I will, but what you will.”
  • “Heard and delivered from his fear.” Some interpreters, including several Reformers, took eulabeia to mean “fear” or “dread” and understood the phrase as “he was heard [and set free] from his fear.” This yields the older English rendering in which he was heard “in that he feared.” On this view, God answered by giving Jesus strength to face death without being overwhelmed by terror (compare Luke 22:43, where an angel strengthens him). But this requires an unusual meaning for eulabeia, which Hebrews itself uses positively (12:28), and an awkward compressed construction.
  • “He was not heard.” Because Jesus died, the German scholar Adolf von Harnack proposed that a negative had dropped out of the text: “although he was not heard.” No manuscript supports this. It is a conjecture invented to solve a problem, and it contradicts what the text actually says.

The first view is best. It takes the words in their normal biblical sense and fits the context. Throughout this passage the stress falls on Jesus’ obedience (5:8) and his submission to God’s appointment (5:5). His prayer was heard because it was offered in reverent, trusting submission to the Father’s will.

In what sense was he heard? Jesus asked that the cup might pass from him, and it did not. He drank it to the dregs. Yet the author says he was heard. How can this be?

The answer lies in what Jesus most deeply asked for. His prayer in Gethsemane had two parts: “Remove this cup from me,” and “Yet not what I will, but what you will.” The second part governed the first. Jesus’ supreme desire was that the Father’s will be done, and the Father’s will was done. More than that, the God “able to save him out of death” did exactly that. He did not spare him from dying, but he raised him from the dead and exalted him to his right hand. The cry of Psalm 22 turns to the praise of 22:25: “when he cried to him for help, he heard.” In the same way, the cry of Gethsemane and Golgotha was answered on Easter morning. As Peter preached, “God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it” (Acts 2:24). That, the author implies, is the truest hearing of all. Jesus was not delivered from death by avoiding it. He was delivered out of it by passing through it into resurrection life. By this he also destroyed the one who has the power of death and set free those who lived in lifelong fear of death (2:14–15).

Here is deep pastoral comfort for the readers, and for us. They faced suffering and possible death for their faith (10:32–34; 12:4). They may have prayed for deliverance and seen none. The author shows them their high priest, who also prayed with tears and was not spared the cross, and who yet was heard. God’s “yes” to the reverent prayers of his children does not always mean escape from suffering. Sometimes it means being carried through suffering into a salvation that death cannot touch. And the one who walked that road ahead of us now stands before the Father as our merciful high priest. He knows, from the inside, what it is to pray with a loud cry and tears.
