12:25

See that you do not refuse him who is speaking. For if they did not escape when they refused him who warned them on earth, much less will we escape if we turn away from him who warns from heaven.

The description of Zion ended with blood that “speaks.” Now the preacher turns that thought into a direct command. Βλέπετε Blepete, “See! Watch out!” is the same urgent warning he used in 3:12: “See, brothers, that there not be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart.” The verb “refuse” (παραιτήσησθε paraitēsēsthe) is the same word used in 12:19 of the Israelites who “begged” that no further word be spoken to them. The repetition is deliberate. The Israelites at Sinai asked to hear no more because they could not bear the voice; the danger for the readers is that they will turn a deaf ear to a far more gracious voice.

“Him who is speaking.” The present participle τὸν λαλοῦντα ton lalounta describes one who is speaking now. Who is the speaker? The sermon opened with the declaration that “God, having spoken long ago to the fathers … has spoken to us in his Son” (1:1–2), using the same verb. In 12:26 the speaker is the one whose voice shook the earth at Sinai, that is, God. And in 12:24 it is Jesus’ blood that “speaks.” There is no tension. God speaks his final word in and through his Son, and the blood of the Son is the very content of that word: forgiveness, cleansing, and access. To refuse the voice of the blood is to refuse God himself.

“For if they did not escape when they refused him who warned them on earth.” The preacher uses his familiar argument from the lesser to the greater (2:2–3; 10:28–29). “They” are the Israelites of the wilderness generation, who heard God’s voice at Sinai and yet did not trust and obey him. Their fate has been described in chapters 3–4: their bodies fell in the wilderness and they did not enter God’s rest (3:16–19). The participle “warned” (χρηματίζοντα chrēmatizonta) is from χρηματίζω chrēmatizō, a word used for divine revelation: Moses “was warned by God” about the tabernacle (8:5), and Noah was “warned by God” about the flood (11:7).

Interpretive Options: Who Warned “on Earth”?

  • Moses. Some interpreters, including some early and Reformation commentators, have understood “him who warned them on earth” as Moses, the earthly mediator, in contrast to Christ, who speaks from heaven.
  • God, speaking at Sinai. Most modern commentators understand both speakers to be God. The contrast is not between two persons but between two places and modes of revelation: God spoke “on earth” at Sinai, and he now speaks “from heaven” through his exalted Son.

The next verse settles the question. The one “whose voice shook the earth then” (12:26) is clearly the God who spoke at Sinai. The voice that shook the earth was not Moses’ voice. And the word chrēmatizō is used in Hebrews for God’s own revelation. The contrast, then, is between God speaking on earth, from a physical mountain, through the old covenant, and God speaking from heaven, through his Son, who has passed through the heavens (4:14) and sits at the right hand of the throne (12:2). The same God speaks; the revelation is fuller, and the responsibility greater.

“Much less will we escape if we turn away from him who warns from heaven.” The Greek is condensed: “much more we, who turn away from the one from heaven.” The words “will not escape” are carried over from the first half. The verb ἀποστρεφόμενοι apostrephomenoi, “turning away from,” is stronger than “refuse.” It describes a deliberate turning of one’s back, the movement of apostasy that Hebrews has warned against throughout (3:12; 6:6; 10:29, 39). The phrase “from heaven” (ἀπ’ οὐρανῶν ap’ ouranōn) recalls the many “heavenly” realities of the sermon and especially the exalted Christ. Revelation from heaven is the final and complete word of God (1:2). If rejecting the earthly revelation at Sinai brought judgment, rejecting the heavenly revelation in the Son leaves no escape at all (2:3, “how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?”; 10:26–27).

Notice the preacher’s “we.” He does not speak down to his readers as though he were immune to the danger. The warning applies to everyone who hears the gospel, the preacher included.

12:26

His voice shook the earth then, but now he has promised, saying, “Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also heaven.”

“His voice shook the earth then.” The relative pronoun “whose” (οὗ hou) links this verse to “him who warns from heaven”: the same God whose voice shook the earth at Sinai now speaks from heaven. The verb ἐσάλευσεν esaleusen, “shook,” is from σαλεύω saleuō, “to shake, cause to totter.” In the Hebrew of Exodus, “the whole mountain trembled greatly” (Exod 19:18; וַיֶּחֱרַד כָּל־הָהָר מְאֹד vayyecherad kol-hahar me’od). The psalmists remembered that day as a shaking of the earth: “The earth quaked, the heavens poured down rain, before God, the One of Sinai” (Ps 68:8 [Hebrew 68:9]; אֶרֶץ רָעָשָׁה … מִפְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים זֶה סִינַי erets ra‘ashah … mippene Elohim zeh Sinai; compare Judg 5:4–5; Ps 114:4–7).

“But now he has promised.” The contrast between “then” (τότε tote) and “now” (νῦν nyn) is the contrast between the two covenants. The verb ἐπήγγελται epēngeltai, “he has promised,” is in the perfect tense: the promise stands on record and remains in force. It is striking that the preacher calls a prophecy of cosmic shaking a “promise.” For the unbelieving it is a threat; for those receiving the unshakable kingdom, it is a promise, because the shaking will clear away everything that stands between them and their inheritance.

Old Testament Text: Haggai 2:6

Hebrew: כִּי כֹה אָמַר יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת עוֹד אַחַת מְעַט הִיא וַאֲנִי מַרְעִישׁ אֶת־הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֶת־הָאָרֶץ וְאֶת־הַיָּם וְאֶת־הֶחָרָבָה׃ — ki kho amar YHWH tseva’ot, ‘od achat me‘at hi, va’ani mar‘ish et-hashamayim ve’et-ha’arets ve’et-hayyam ve’et-hecharavah.

Translation of the Hebrew: “For thus says the Lord of hosts: Yet once—it is a little while—and I will shake the heavens and the earth and the sea and the dry land.”

The Greek translation renders the opening words simply Ἔτι ἅπαξ Eti hapax, “Yet once (more),” without the Hebrew phrase “it is a little while,” and then reads, “I will shake the heaven and the earth and the sea and the dry land.” Hebrews follows the Greek wording “Yet once more I will shake,” but makes two changes. It omits “the sea and the dry land,” focusing on the two great realms of heaven and earth. And it reshapes the sentence into a contrast, “not only the earth but also heaven,” in order to bring out the difference between Sinai, when God shook only the earth, and the final shaking, which will include the heavens.

The Hebrew original supports the preacher’s use of the text. The word achat, “one, once,” already implies a single, decisive act of God, and the phrase “it is a little while” stresses its nearness. The verb mar‘ish, from רָעַשׁ ra‘ash, is the verb used for the earthquake of God’s appearing (Judg 5:4; Ps 68:9). The context in Haggai is also important. The prophet was encouraging the discouraged people who had returned from exile and were rebuilding the temple, which looked like nothing compared with Solomon’s (Hag 2:3). God promised that he would shake all the nations, that “the treasures of all nations” would come in, and that “the latter glory of this house will be greater than the former” (Hag 2:7–9). Later in the same chapter God repeats, “I am about to shake the heavens and the earth, and I will overthrow the throne of kingdoms; I will destroy the strength of the kingdoms of the nations” (Hag 2:21–22; Hebrew וְהָפַכְתִּי כִּסֵּא מַמְלָכוֹת vehafakhti kisse mamlakhot, “and I will overturn the throne of kingdoms”). The shaking of heaven and earth is linked with the overthrow of earthly kingdoms and the establishment of God’s own rule. Hebrews draws exactly this conclusion: the kingdoms that can be shaken will fall, and believers are receiving “a kingdom that cannot be shaken” (12:28).

The preacher’s use of Haggai, then, is not arbitrary. Haggai addressed discouraged people who were tempted to think that God’s work among them was small and insignificant. God assured them that a greater glory was coming, and that he himself would shake the entire created order to bring it about. The readers of Hebrews were in a similar position: tempted to think that the gospel was less glorious and less solid than the visible institutions of the old covenant. The preacher assures them that the greater glory has come in Christ, and that the final shaking will reveal which things are lasting.

12:27

And this phrase, “Yet once more,” indicates the removal of the things that are shaken, as of things that have been made, so that the things that are not shaken may remain.

The preacher now comments on the quotation, focusing on two words: “Yet once” (Ἔτι ἅπαξ Eti hapax). The verb δηλοῖ dēloi, “indicates, makes clear,” is the same verb used in 9:8, where the Holy Spirit “indicates” something by the arrangement of the tabernacle. The preacher pays close attention to the exact words of Scripture and finds meaning in them, as he did with “today” (3:7–4:11), “another” priest (7:11–15), and “new” covenant (8:13).

The key word is ἅπαξ hapax, “once.” In Hebrews this word and its strengthened form ἐφάπαξ ephapax have a special weight. Christ “has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself” (9:26); “it is appointed for people to die once, and after that comes judgment, so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time” (9:27–28). “Once” in Hebrews signals finality, something that will never be repeated. “Yet once more,” then, means that the coming shaking will be the final one. There will not be another.

“The removal of the things that are shaken.” The noun μετάθεσις metathesis can mean change, transfer, or removal. Hebrews uses it in 7:12 for a “change” of law and in 11:5 for the “taking up” of Enoch. The “things that are shaken” (τῶν σαλευομένων tōn saleuomenōn) are the heaven and the earth of the quotation, the present created order. And they are described as “things that have been made” (ὡς πεποιημένων hōs pepoiēmenōn), that is, created things, which by their very nature are dependent and transient. Hebrews has already applied Psalm 102 to the Son: “they will perish, but you remain; they will all wear out like a garment … like a robe you will roll them up, and like a garment they will be changed; but you are the same, and your years will have no end” (1:11–12; Ps 102:26–27 [Hebrew 102:27–28]).

“So that the things that are not shaken may remain.” The purpose of the shaking is not destruction for its own sake but the revelation and establishment of what is permanent. The verb μείνῃ meinē, “may remain,” is a key word in Hebrews: the Son “remains” (1:11), Melchizedek “remains a priest forever” (7:3), Christ “remains forever” (7:24), believers have a “better and remaining possession” (10:34), and “here we do not have a remaining city” (13:14). What is unshaken is the eternal kingdom of God, the heavenly city, and all who belong to it.

Interpretive Options: Removal or Transformation of Creation?

  • Annihilation. Some interpreters understand metathesis as the complete removal of the present creation, which will be destroyed and replaced by an entirely different, purely spiritual reality.
  • Transformation. Others understand the shaking as a purifying judgment in which everything corrupted and transient is removed, while creation itself is renewed and transformed.

Hebrews by itself emphasizes the contrast between what is transient and what remains, without describing in detail what will happen to the material world. The wider witness of Scripture fills in the picture. Paul says that creation itself “will be set free from its bondage to corruption” (Rom 8:21). Peter speaks of the heavens and earth being dissolved by fire, yet looks for “new heavens and a new earth, in which righteousness dwells” (2 Pet 3:10–13; compare Isa 65:17; 66:22). John sees “a new heaven and a new earth,” and hears God say, “Behold, I am making all things new” (Rev 21:1, 5). Hebrews itself speaks of “the world to come” (2:5), which will be subjected to the Son and his brothers. Psalm 102, quoted in 1:10–12, speaks of creation being “changed” like a garment. The best conclusion is that the final shaking will remove everything in the present order that is subject to sin, decay, and death—everything that belongs merely to “the things that have been made” in their fallen and temporary condition—so that the eternal kingdom of God may stand unobscured. Whether the new creation is best described as a renewal of this one or a radical replacement, the text does not say precisely; what it does say clearly is that nothing shakable will survive, and that God’s kingdom will.

12:28

Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be grateful, and through this let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe;

The conjunction διό dio, “therefore,” introduces the response called for by everything in 12:18–27. Having been brought to Zion and assured that only the unshakable will remain, believers must respond with gratitude and worship.

“Since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken.” The participle παραλαμβάνοντες paralambanontes, “receiving,” is in the present tense. The kingdom is not something believers earn or build; it is a gift they receive. And the process of receiving is underway now. Believers already belong to this kingdom, yet they have not yet entered into its full possession. The verb recalls Daniel’s vision, where, after the beasts representing earthly empires are judged, “the holy ones of the Most High will receive the kingdom and possess the kingdom forever, forever and ever” (Dan 7:18; compare 7:14, 27); the Greek translations of Daniel use this same verb, “receive.” Daniel had also foreseen a kingdom that the God of heaven would set up, “which will never be destroyed” and which “will stand forever” (Dan 2:44). The kingdom of God has been a theme of Hebrews from the beginning: the Son’s “throne is forever and ever, and the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom” (1:8, quoting Ps 45:6). Believers receive a share in that kingdom through their union with the Son.

The adjective ἀσάλευτος asaleutos, “unshakable, immovable,” is formed from saleuō, “shake,” with the negative prefix a-. It picks up the language of 12:26–27 directly: every created thing will be shaken, but this kingdom cannot be. For readers who had lost their property and status, and who were tempted to seek security in visible things, this was a profound comfort. Everything they had lost belonged to the shakable order. What they were receiving could never be taken from them.

Interpretive Options: ἔχωμεν χάριν echōmen charin—“Let us be grateful” or “Let us have grace”?

The phrase ἔχωμεν χάριν echōmen charin literally means “let us have charis.” The noun χάρις charis can mean grace (favor freely given), or gratitude (the response to such favor).

  • “Let us be grateful.” The idiom charin echō, “to have gratitude,” was a common Greek expression for giving thanks, used elsewhere in the New Testament (Luke 17:9; 1 Tim 1:12; 2 Tim 1:3). On this reading the preacher calls for thankfulness in response to the gift of the kingdom, and this thankfulness becomes the motive and means of acceptable worship.
  • “Let us have (or hold on to) grace.” Others take charis in its usual sense in Hebrews, God’s grace (2:9; 4:16; 10:29; 12:15; 13:9, 25), so that the readers are urged to hold on to the grace by which alone they can worship acceptably.

The first reading fits the idiom and the context best. The verb “have” with charin is the standard Greek way of saying “be thankful,” and the response to receiving a kingdom is naturally gratitude. David deSilva has shown how deeply Hebrews is shaped by the ancient understanding of patronage, in which a benefactor’s favor (charis) called for a grateful response (also charis) of loyalty, honor, and service. To receive a gift and then insult the giver was considered among the worst of offenses. That is precisely the danger the preacher has been warning against: the readers have received the greatest of gifts, and to turn away from the Giver would be the ultimate ingratitude (6:4–6; 10:29). The two senses of charis are not far apart. God’s grace is the source; the believer’s gratitude is the response; and the response is itself sustained by grace. But the call here is to thankfulness.

Textual Note

Some manuscripts read the verbs as statements rather than exhortations: ἔχομεν echomen, “we have,” instead of ἔχωμεν echōmen, “let us have,” and λατρεύομεν latreuomen, “we worship,” instead of λατρεύωμεν latreuōmen, “let us worship.” In Greek these forms differ only by one vowel (o or ō) and were pronounced alike in later Greek, so confusion was easy. The exhortation (“let us”) has strong support and fits the hortatory context and the sermon’s pattern of “let us” exhortations. Later manuscripts also read “with reverence and godly fear” (μετὰ αἰδοῦς καὶ εὐλαβείας meta aidous kai eulabeias), the reading behind the King James Version, instead of “with reverence and awe” (μετὰ εὐλαβείας καὶ δέους meta eulabeias kai deous). The latter is supported by the earliest witnesses; the substitution probably arose because the word δέος deos, “awe, fear,” is rare.

“And through this let us offer to God acceptable worship.” “Through this” (δι’ ἧς di’ hēs, “through which”) refers back to the gratitude just mentioned. Gratitude is the channel through which acceptable worship flows. The verb λατρεύωμεν latreuōmen, from λατρεύω latreuō, “to serve, worship,” was used throughout Hebrews for priestly service in the sanctuary (8:5; 9:9; 10:2; 13:10). Now all believers are called to offer such service, for Christ’s blood has cleansed their consciences “to serve the living God” (9:14). The worship of the new covenant is not limited to a sanctuary or a priestly class. The next chapter will spell out what it involves: “a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name,” together with doing good and sharing, “for with such sacrifices God is pleased” (13:15–16).

The adverb εὐαρέστως euarestōs, “acceptably, in a way that pleases,” belongs to the word family Hebrews used of Enoch, who “pleased God” (11:5), and in the statement that “without faith it is impossible to please him” (11:6). The same family appears in 13:16, 21. Acceptable worship is worship offered in faith, through Christ, with a grateful heart.

“With reverence and awe.” The noun εὐλάβεια eulabeia, “reverence, godly fear,” was used in 5:7 of Jesus himself, who “was heard because of his reverence.” The noun δέος deos, “awe, fear,” occurs only here in the New Testament. The two words together describe the right attitude of worshipers before a holy God. The gospel does not abolish reverence; it transforms it. Under the old covenant, fear kept the people at a distance (12:18–21). Under the new covenant, believers draw near—but they draw near to the same holy God. Their boldness (4:16; 10:19) is not casualness; it is confidence grounded in the blood of Jesus, and it is accompanied by awe.

12:29

for indeed our God is a consuming fire.

The chapter ends with a brief, solemn line that gives the reason for reverence and awe. The combination καὶ γάρ kai gar, “for indeed, for also,” adds weight: this is not a new thought but a reminder of something that must never be forgotten. The words are taken from Deuteronomy.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 4:24

Hebrew: כִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה הוּא אֵל קַנָּא׃ — ki YHWH Eloheka esh okhlah hu, El qanna’, “For the Lord your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God.”

The Hebrew אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה esh okhlah is literally “an eating fire,” a fire that devours. The Greek translation and Hebrews render it πῦρ καταναλίσκον pyr katanaliskon, “a fire that consumes completely.” Hebrews changes “your God” to “our God,” applying the words to the whole people of God, preacher and hearers alike.

The context in Deuteronomy is important. Moses has just reminded Israel of the day at Horeb, when God spoke to them out of the fire (Deut 4:10–12), the same scene Hebrews described in 12:18–21. He then warns them: “Take care, lest you forget the covenant of the Lord your God, which he made with you, and make for yourselves a carved image” (Deut 4:23). The reason follows: “For the Lord your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God.” God’s “jealousy” is not petty envy but his exclusive claim on the love and loyalty of his covenant people, as a faithful husband rightly claims his wife’s faithfulness. Turning from him to other gods would provoke his burning holiness. The same image appears in Deuteronomy 9:3, where God goes before Israel “as a consuming fire” to destroy their enemies. The fire that threatens the unfaithful is the same fire that protects and fights for his people.

By ending with this verse, Hebrews brings the Sinai–Zion contrast to a profound conclusion. The God of Zion is the God of Sinai. Grace has not made him less holy. Under the new covenant, believers do not stand at a distance from the fire; they have been brought near through the blood of Jesus. But the fire is still fire. It is a fire that purifies those who come through Christ, and a fire that consumes those who refuse him (10:27, “a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries”). Isaiah asked, “Who among us can dwell with the consuming fire? Who among us can dwell with everlasting burnings?” (Isa 33:14; Hebrew מִי יָגוּר לָנוּ אֵשׁ אוֹכֵלָה mi yagur lanu esh okhelah). His answer was, “the one who walks righteously” (Isa 33:15). Hebrews gives the fuller answer: those who have been made righteous and perfected through Christ, the mediator of the new covenant, whose sprinkled blood speaks a better word.

This final verse is not meant to drive believers away in terror, but to keep them from treating grace lightly. It explains why the warnings of Hebrews are so serious and why the call to persevere is so urgent. It also explains why the gospel is such good news. The God who is a consuming fire has himself provided the way into his presence. The privilege of drawing near to him is so great precisely because he is so holy. Those who know this will worship him “with reverence and awe”—and will gladly go on to the practical worship of love, hospitality, faithfulness, and praise that chapter 13 describes.

For Reflection

  1. Hebrews says believers “have come” to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem. How might it change your experience of gathered worship to realize that you are joining angels in festal gathering and the spirits of the righteous made perfect?
  2. The Israelites begged not to hear God’s voice; the readers are warned not to refuse him who speaks from heaven. In what ways are we tempted to turn a deaf ear to God’s word today?
  3. Jesus’ blood “speaks better than Abel.” What does his blood say to you about your guilt, your access to God, and your standing in his family?
  4. God will shake everything that can be shaken. Which of the things you rely on for security belong to the “shakable” order, and how does the promise of an unshakable kingdom free you from clinging to them?
  5. Gratitude is the channel of acceptable worship. How can thankfulness for the kingdom you are receiving shape your daily obedience and service?
  6. How do the truths that “our God is a consuming fire” and that we have been brought near through Jesus’ blood work together to produce worship that is both bold and reverent?
