A Kingdom That Cannot Be Shaken (12:18–29)

Translation

18For you have not come to what can be touched, to a blazing fire, and to darkness and gloom and whirlwind, 19and to the blast of a trumpet and a voice of words, which those who heard begged that no further word be spoken to them. 20For they could not bear what was commanded:

“If even a beast touches the mountain, it shall be stoned.”

21Indeed, so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, “I am terrified and trembling.”

22But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to myriads of angels in festal gathering, 23and to the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven, and to God, the judge of all, and to the spirits of the righteous made perfect, 24and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks better than Abel.

25See that you do not refuse him who is speaking. For if they did not escape when they refused him who warned them on earth, much less will we escape if we turn away from him who warns from heaven. 26His voice shook the earth then, but now he has promised, saying,

“Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also heaven.”

27And this phrase, “Yet once more,” indicates the removal of the things that are shaken, as of things that have been made, so that the things that are not shaken may remain. 28Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be grateful, and through this let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe; 29for indeed our God is a consuming fire.

Setting and Structure

The final section of Hebrews 12 is the climax of the sermon’s long argument that the new covenant is better than the old. It gathers up the themes of the whole book—God speaking, the mediator, the covenant, the blood, the heavenly city, the danger of refusing God’s word—and sets them in a single, unforgettable contrast between two mountains. It then turns that contrast into a final warning and a call to worship.

The section has two parts. The first (12:18–24) is built on a sharp antithesis: “you have not come to …” (12:18) and “but you have come to …” (12:22). The first half describes Sinai, though without naming it, in terms drawn from Exodus 19–20 and Deuteronomy 4–5 and 9: something tangible, fire, darkness, gloom, storm, trumpet, a voice so terrible that the people begged not to hear it, a command that even a beast touching the mountain must die, and Moses himself trembling. The second half describes Zion: the city of the living God, festal angels, the assembly of the firstborn, God the judge, the perfected righteous, Jesus the mediator, and his sprinkled blood. Each half can be counted as a list of seven elements, and the parallel structure invites the reader to compare them item by item. Sinai is earthly, physical, dark, and terrifying; it keeps people at a distance. Zion is heavenly, joyful, populated, and welcoming; it brings people near.

The second part (12:25–29) turns from description to exhortation. Because the same God who spoke at Sinai now speaks from heaven, the danger of refusing him is greater, not less. The voice that once shook the earth has promised, through the prophet Haggai, to shake earth and heaven “once more,” removing everything created and transient so that only what cannot be shaken will remain. Those who are receiving the unshakable kingdom must respond with gratitude and reverent worship, because God remains “a consuming fire.”

The key verb of the first part is προσεληλύθατε proselēlythate, “you have come,” in the perfect tense (12:18, 22). This is the same verb the sermon has used for drawing near to God in worship: “let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16); he “is able to save completely those who draw near to God through him” (7:25); “let us draw near with a true heart” (10:22); “whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists” (11:6). The perfect tense means that the readers have already arrived and now stand in that position. They are not simply on their way to Zion; in Christ, they have come to it. This is one of the clearest expressions in the New Testament of what is sometimes called “realized” or “inaugurated” eschatology: the blessings of the age to come are already present to faith, though their full manifestation is still future. The same sermon says that “here we do not have a lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come” (13:14). Believers are both already at Zion, in worship, and still on pilgrimage toward it.

The contrast of the two mountains is not a contrast between a bad God and a good God, nor between a God of law and a God of love. The God of Sinai and the God of Zion are one and the same; the section ends by reminding the readers that “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29), quoting the very book that describes Sinai. The contrast is between two covenants and two modes of access. Under the old covenant, the people stood at a distance, could not touch the mountain, and begged to hear no more. Under the new covenant, the way into God’s presence has been opened through the blood of Jesus (10:19–22), and believers are welcomed into the festal assembly of heaven. Paul makes a similar contrast in Galatians 4:24–26, where Mount Sinai corresponds to the present Jerusalem in slavery, but “the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother.”

The section also gathers up earlier warnings. The argument of 12:25 (“if they did not escape … much less will we”) repeats the logic of 2:2–3 and 10:28–29, completing a pattern of warnings that runs through the whole sermon. The God who “spoke” at many times and in many ways to the fathers, and has now “spoken” in his Son (1:1–2), is the God “who is speaking” now (12:25). The sermon that began with God speaking ends its main argument with a warning not to refuse the one who speaks.

Verse by Verse

12:18

For you have not come to what can be touched, to a blazing fire, and to darkness and gloom and whirlwind,

The “for” (γάρ gar) connects this paragraph to the warnings of 12:14–17. The reason the readers must pursue holiness and not despise their birthright as Esau did is that they have come to a far greater place than Israel ever stood: not Sinai, but Zion.

“You have not come.” The perfect tense προσεληλύθατε proselēlythate describes their present standing. The negative is placed first in the Greek for emphasis: “Not to … have you come.” Some of the readers were tempted to look back to the old covenant and its institutions, perhaps thinking them more solid and tangible than the invisible realities of the gospel. The preacher reminds them where they actually stand.

“To what can be touched.” The first item is a present passive participle, ψηλαφωμένῳ psēlaphōmenō, from ψηλαφάω psēlaphaō, “to touch, feel, handle.” Sinai was a physical mountain, a place of earth and rock that could be touched—though touching it was forbidden on pain of death (Exod 19:12–13). The point is not that physical things are evil; Hebrews affirms the goodness of creation (11:3) and the true humanity of Jesus (2:14). The point is that Sinai belonged to the earthly, material order, “this creation” (9:11), the realm of copies and shadows (8:5; 9:23–24), which is destined to be shaken (12:27). The new covenant’s realities are heavenly.

Textual Note: “A mountain that can be touched”?

Many later manuscripts add the word ὄρει orei, “mountain,” after “what can be touched,” giving “a mountain that can be touched” (as in the King James Version). The earliest and best witnesses, including P46, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Alexandrinus, lack the word. Metzger explains the addition as a natural scribal attempt to supply the obvious noun, perhaps influenced by “Mount Zion” in 12:22. The shorter text is more striking. By not naming Sinai, and not even saying “mountain,” the preacher keeps the focus on its tangible, earthly character.

“To a blazing fire.” The phrase κεκαυμένῳ πυρί kekaumenō pyri can be read as “to a blazing fire” or, taking both participles together with an implied noun, “to what can be touched and has been set ablaze with fire.” Either way, the reference is to the fire that enveloped the mountain: “Mount Sinai was wrapped in smoke because the Lord had descended upon it in fire” (Exod 19:18); “the mountain was burning with fire to the heart of heaven” (Deut 4:11; Hebrew וְהָהָר בֹּעֵר בָּאֵשׁ עַד־לֵב הַשָּׁמַיִם vehahar bo‘er ba’esh ‘ad-lev hashamayim).

“And to darkness and gloom and whirlwind.” Three nouns follow, each joined by “and,” a style that piles up the terrors one after another. γνόφος gnophos is thick darkness or a dark cloud; ζόφος zophos is deep gloom, the darkness of the underworld in Greek poetry (compare 2 Pet 2:4, 17; Jude 6, 13); θύελλα thyella is a storm or whirlwind. The description follows Deuteronomy, where Moses recalls the day at Horeb: the mountain burned with fire, with “darkness, cloud, and thick darkness” (Deut 4:11; Hebrew חֹשֶׁךְ עָנָן וַעֲרָפֶל choshekh ‘anan va‘arafel), and the Lord spoke “out of the midst of the fire, the cloud, and the thick darkness” (Deut 5:22). The Greek translation of these verses uses gnophos and thyella, the very words Hebrews uses here. In Exodus, Moses “drew near to the thick darkness where God was” (Exod 20:21), while the people stood far off. Sinai was a revelation of God that also concealed him: fire that could not be approached, darkness that could not be penetrated.

12:19

and to the blast of a trumpet and a voice of words, which those who heard begged that no further word be spoken to them.

Two sounds complete the description of Sinai. The first is “the blast of a trumpet” (σάλπιγγος ἤχῳ salpingos ēchō; ἦχος ēchos, “sound, roar,” gives us “echo”). In Exodus, “the sound of the shofar was very strong, and all the people in the camp trembled” (Exod 19:16; Hebrew וְקֹל שֹׁפָר חָזָק מְאֹד וַיֶּחֱרַד כָּל־הָעָם אֲשֶׁר בַּמַּחֲנֶה veqol shofar chazaq me’od vayyecherad kol-ha‘am asher bammachaneh), and the sound “grew louder and louder” (Exod 19:19). The trumpet announced the presence of the divine King. No human hand blew it.

The second is “a voice of words” (φωνῇ ῥημάτων phōnē rhēmatōn), echoing Deuteronomy 4:12: “The Lord spoke to you out of the midst of the fire; you heard the sound of words, but saw no form; there was only a voice” (Hebrew קוֹל דְּבָרִים אַתֶּם שֹׁמְעִים וּתְמוּנָה אֵינְכֶם רֹאִים זוּלָתִי קוֹל qol devarim attem shom‘im utemunah enkhem ro’im zulati qol). The climax of Sinai was not the fire or the storm but the voice of God speaking the Ten Words. Here lies the heart of the comparison that will follow in 12:25: the God who spoke at Sinai is the God who speaks now.

“Which those who heard begged that no further word be spoken to them.” The verb παρῃτήσαντο parētēsanto, from παραιτέομαι paraiteomai, means “to beg off, ask to be excused, refuse.” This same verb will be used twice in 12:25, where the readers are warned not to “refuse” him who is speaking. The repetition is pointed. At Sinai the people asked not to hear any more: “You speak with us, and we will listen; but do not let God speak with us, lest we die” (Exod 20:19; Hebrew וְאַל־יְדַבֵּר עִמָּנוּ אֱלֹהִים פֶּן־נָמוּת ve’al-yedabber ‘immanu Elohim pen-namut). Deuteronomy records the same request: “Why should we die? For this great fire will consume us; if we hear the voice of the Lord our God any longer, we shall die” (Deut 5:25; see 5:23–27; 18:16).

It is important to note that the Israelites’ request was not in itself sinful. God approved it: “They have spoken well in all that they have spoken” (Deut 5:28; 18:17). Their request recognized that sinful people cannot bear the unmediated presence of a holy God, and God answered it by appointing Moses as mediator and promising a greater prophet to come (Deut 18:15–19). Hebrews is not condemning the Israelites for this request; it is describing the effect of Sinai. The old covenant revelation, glorious as it was, drove people back in fear and required them to stand at a distance. That is precisely the contrast with Zion, where believers are brought near through a better mediator (12:24).

12:20

For they could not bear what was commanded: “If even a beast touches the mountain, it shall be stoned.”

The “for” explains why the people begged to hear no more. The imperfect ἔφερον epheron, “they could not bear, endure,” describes their continuing inability. “What was commanded” (τὸ διαστελλόμενον to diastellomenon) refers to the strict prohibition God gave before the theophany.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 19:12–13

Hebrew: וְהִגְבַּלְתָּ אֶת־הָעָם סָבִיב לֵאמֹר הִשָּׁמְרוּ לָכֶם עֲלוֹת בָּהָר וּנְגֹעַ בְּקָצֵהוּ כָּל־הַנֹּגֵעַ בָּהָר מוֹת יוּמָת׃ לֹא־תִגַּע בּוֹ יָד כִּי־סָקוֹל יִסָּקֵל אוֹ־יָרֹה יִיָּרֶה אִם־בְּהֵמָה אִם־אִישׁ לֹא יִחְיֶה

Translation of the Hebrew: “You shall set limits for the people all around, saying, ‘Take care not to go up on the mountain or touch its edge. Whoever touches the mountain shall surely be put to death. No hand shall touch him, but he shall surely be stoned or shot through; whether beast or man, he shall not live.’”

Hebrews condenses the command into a single line and focuses on its most startling element. The Hebrew says, “whether beast or man” (אִם־בְּהֵמָה אִם־אִישׁ im-behemah im-ish). Hebrews says, “If even a beast touches the mountain” (Κἂν θηρίον θίγῃ τοῦ ὄρους Kan thērion thigē tou orous). The word κἄν kan, “even if,” draws out the force of the original. If an animal, which cannot know God’s law, had to die for touching the holy mountain, how much more a human being! The detail that the offender was to be stoned or shot, so that “no hand shall touch him,” shows that the holiness of the mountain was so intense that even the one who had touched it became untouchable.

The point is the unapproachable holiness of God under the old covenant. The mountain was fenced off; only Moses (and, at one stage, Aaron) could go up (Exod 19:20–24). This physical barrier corresponds to the barriers of the tabernacle, where the curtain kept everyone but the high priest out of the Most Holy Place, and he could enter only once a year with blood (9:7–8). The Holy Spirit, says Hebrews, was showing by this “that the way into the sanctuary had not yet been disclosed” (9:8). Sinai said, “Keep away.” The gospel says, “Draw near” (10:22).

12:21

Indeed, so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, “I am terrified and trembling.”

The final item in the description of Sinai is the most striking of all. Not only the people, but Moses himself—the mediator of the old covenant, the man who spoke with God face to face (Exod 33:11; Num 12:8), whom Hebrews has honored as faithful in all God’s house (3:2–5)—was overwhelmed with fear. The sentence is introduced by “indeed” (καί kai, “and, even”) and the adverb “so” (οὕτω houtō), stressing the degree of the terror. “The sight” (τὸ φανταζόμενον to phantazomenon, “what was made to appear, the spectacle”) refers to the whole visible manifestation of God on the mountain. The adjective φοβερόν phoberon, “terrifying,” was used earlier in the sermon: “a terrifying expectation of judgment” (10:27) and “it is a terrifying thing to fall into the hands of the living God” (10:31).

Old Testament Text: Moses’ Words and Deuteronomy 9:19

The exact words “I am terrified and trembling” (Ἔκφοβός εἰμι καὶ ἔντρομος Ekphobos eimi kai entromos) are not found in the Exodus account of the giving of the law. The closest Old Testament text is Deuteronomy 9:19, where Moses recalls his fear after Israel made the golden calf at the foot of Sinai: “For I was in dread of the anger and the wrath with which the Lord was angry with you, to destroy you” (Hebrew כִּי יָגֹרְתִּי מִפְּנֵי הָאַף וְהַחֵמָה ki yagorti mippene ha’af vehachemah, “for I was in dread before the anger and the wrath”). The Greek translation of that verse renders the first words “I am terrified” with the same word Hebrews uses, ἔκφοβος ekphobos.

The second word, “trembling” (ἔντρομος entromos), is not in Deuteronomy 9:19. It may reflect a Jewish tradition about Moses’ fear at Sinai; Stephen, recounting Moses’ encounter with God at the burning bush, also says that Moses “became trembling” (ἔντρομος) and did not dare to look (Acts 7:32). The Hebrew text of Exodus says that “the whole mountain trembled greatly” and “all the people trembled” (Exod 19:16, 18), using the verb חָרַד charad, “to tremble.”

How should we understand the preacher’s use of these words? He applies to the Sinai experience as a whole a statement that Deuteronomy records in the context of the covenant’s near-breaking at Sinai. This is not careless; the golden calf episode took place at the same mountain during the same forty days, and it exposed with terrible clarity what the Sinai revelation meant for sinful people: the holy God whose voice spoke from the fire was also the God whose wrath burned against covenant-breakers. Even Moses, the great mediator, trembled before that holiness. The preacher’s point is that the old covenant, at its very source, was marked by fear. If Moses trembled, who could stand?

This verse brings the description of Sinai to a fitting close. The old covenant’s mediator, for all his greatness, could not remove the terror of God’s holiness. That would require a better mediator and better blood (12:24).
