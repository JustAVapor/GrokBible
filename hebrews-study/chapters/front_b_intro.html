Introduction to Hebrews

“Consider Jesus”

Hebrews is one of the most theologically rich, carefully argued, and pastorally urgent books of the New Testament. It opens without a greeting, with a single majestic sentence announcing that the God who spoke in the past through the prophets “has spoken to us in a Son” (1:1–2). It closes with a benediction and a few personal lines, asking its hearers to “bear with the word of exhortation” (13:22). Between those two points the author builds an argument from the Old Testament Scriptures that Jesus is the Son of God, greater than the angels, greater than Moses, greater than Joshua, greater than Aaron and every Levitical priest; that his priesthood, covenant, sanctuary, and sacrifice are “better” than all that went before; and that, therefore, the people of God must not drift, harden their hearts, grow sluggish, shrink back, or turn away, but must hold fast, draw near, run with endurance, and go out to Jesus “outside the camp,” bearing his reproach.

This introduction sets out what can be known about the book’s origin and setting, how it is built, and what its major themes are. Questions that cannot be settled with certainty are noted as such; the study does not depend on any answer the text itself does not give.

The Title

The title “To the Hebrews” (Πρὸς Ἑβραίους Pros Hebraious) was not written by the author. It does not appear in the text itself. But it is very old: it stands at the head of the book in the earliest surviving manuscript of Hebrews, the Chester Beatty papyrus known as 𝔓46 (copied around AD 200), where Hebrews is placed among the letters of Paul, immediately after Romans. The title reflects an early and sound judgment about the readers: the whole argument assumes an audience steeped in the Scriptures of Israel, the priesthood, the tabernacle, and the sacrificial system, and tempted to find in those things a security that, the author insists, is now found only in Christ.

The Author

Hebrews is anonymous. The author never names himself, and the book contains no opening greeting in which a name would normally appear. Yet the text tells us a good deal about him:

  • He belonged to the second generation of believers. The salvation “was first spoken through the Lord, and was confirmed to us by those who heard” (2:3). The author received the gospel from eyewitnesses; he does not claim to be one.
  • He was a man. In 11:32 he writes, “time will fail me if I tell [διηγούμενον diēgoumenon],” and the participle is masculine in form.
  • He knew the Greek Scriptures intimately and wrote the most polished, rhetorically sophisticated Greek in the New Testament—rich in vocabulary (it contains many words found nowhere else in the New Testament), carefully balanced in its periods, skilled in the techniques of ancient oratory such as alliteration, wordplay, and the argument “from the lesser to the greater.”
  • He was connected with the Pauline circle: he knows Timothy, calls him “our brother,” and hopes to travel with him (13:23).
  • He knew his hearers personally, had been with them before, and hoped to be “restored” to them soon (13:19).

The early church was divided about his identity. In the East, especially in Alexandria, Hebrews was received among the letters of Paul, though thoughtful scholars noticed the difference in style. Eusebius reports that Clement of Alexandria held that Paul wrote it in Hebrew for Hebrews and that Luke translated it into Greek; and that Origen judged the thoughts to be the apostle’s while the style and composition belonged to someone else who recalled the apostle’s teaching—concluding that who actually wrote the letter, God knows (Ecclesiastical History 6.14; 6.25). In the West, Hebrews was for a long time not attributed to Paul at all. Tertullian named Barnabas, the Levite from Cyprus (On Modesty 20; cf. Acts 4:36). Later suggestions include Luke, Clement of Rome, Silas, and—most memorably—Apollos, proposed by Martin Luther, an “eloquent man, mighty in the Scriptures,” from Alexandria (Acts 18:24). John Calvin judged that Paul was not the author, pointing to the style and especially to 2:3, since Paul insisted that he received his gospel not from men but by revelation of Jesus Christ (Gal 1:11–12).

Calvin’s observation remains weighty, and the present study follows the view that has become common among commentators of many traditions: the author is unknown to us, though not to his first readers or to God. Nothing in the book’s authority or meaning depends on a name. The church received Hebrews because it recognized in it the voice of the apostolic gospel—God speaking in his Son. In this study he is called simply “the author,” “the writer,” or “the preacher.”

What Kind of Writing Is Hebrews?

Hebrews ends like a letter (13:18–25), but it does not begin like one. Its author calls it a “word of exhortation” (τοῦ λόγου τῆς παρακλήσεως tou logou tēs paraklēseōs, 13:22). The same phrase is used in Acts 13:15, where the synagogue rulers at Pisidian Antioch invite Paul and Barnabas, after the reading of the Law and the Prophets, to give “a word of exhortation” to the people—that is, a sermon. Hebrews is a written sermon, meant to be read aloud to a gathered congregation, with a brief letter-ending attached so that it could be sent.

Its sermonic character shows everywhere. The author speaks of “speaking” rather than “writing” (2:5; 5:11; 6:9; 8:1; 9:5; 11:32). He constantly includes himself with his hearers: “let us hold fast,” “let us draw near,” “let us run.” And the book moves back and forth between two kinds of discourse:

  • Exposition: careful explanation of Old Testament texts—especially Psalm 2, Psalm 8, Psalm 95, Psalm 110, Jeremiah 31, and Psalm 40—showing how they find their meaning in Jesus.
  • Exhortation: urgent application of that teaching to the hearers, including some of the most solemn warnings in the Bible (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:19–39; 12:14–29) and some of its most tender encouragements (4:14–16; 6:9–20; 10:19–25; 12:1–13; 13:5–6, 20–21).

The exposition always serves the exhortation. The author does not teach about the priesthood of Christ to satisfy curiosity; he teaches it so that weary believers will “draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16) and not “throw away [their] boldness” (10:35).

The First Readers

From the book itself we learn that the hearers:

  • had heard the gospel from eyewitnesses and seen it confirmed by “signs and wonders and various miracles and distributions of the Holy Spirit” (2:3–4);
  • had been believers long enough that they “ought to be teachers” by now (5:12);
  • had shown love to God’s people and were still serving them (6:10);
  • had in “the former days,” after they were “enlightened,” endured “a great contest of sufferings”: public insults and afflictions, partnership with others so treated, compassion on prisoners, and the plundering of their property, which they accepted “with joy” (10:32–34);
  • had not yet “resisted to the point of blood” (12:4)—no one in the congregation had yet been killed for the faith;
  • had leaders who had spoken God’s word to them and had died (13:7), and present leaders to whom they owed obedience (13:17);
  • were in danger of drifting away (2:1), hardening through sin’s deceitfulness (3:13), growing “sluggish” (5:11; 6:12), neglecting to meet together (10:25), and shrinking back (10:38–39);
  • were being drawn toward “various and strange teachings” connected with “foods” (13:9).

The whole argument—built on the priesthood, the tabernacle, the covenant at Sinai, the Day of Atonement, the Levitical sacrifices—assumes readers who knew and revered these institutions. Most interpreters therefore conclude that the congregation was largely Jewish Christian (though Gentile believers who had learned the Scriptures in the synagogue may well have been among them), and that the danger facing them was a retreat from open confession of Jesus back into the relative safety and familiar securities of the synagogue and the old covenant order. Judaism was a recognized and ancient religion in the Roman world; a sect following a crucified Messiah was not. To go back would have meant relief from shame and loss. The author’s answer is that there is nothing to go back to: the old covenant was a “shadow” and a “copy” pointing forward to Christ (8:5; 10:1), and to turn from him is to turn from the only sacrifice for sins that remains (10:26).

Destination and Date

The only explicit geographical clue is 13:24: “Those from Italy greet you.” The phrase could describe people in Italy sending greetings, or Italians away from home sending greetings back to Italy. The second reading fits the expression naturally, and several other lines of evidence point the same way: the first writer known to use Hebrews is Clement of Rome, whose letter to the Corinthians (usually dated about AD 95–96) echoes Hebrews repeatedly (most clearly in 1 Clement 36, which draws on Heb 1:3–13); the word Hebrews uses for the church’s leaders (ἡγούμενοι hēgoumenoi, 13:7, 17, 24) is used of church leaders in early Roman Christian writings; and the persecution described in 10:32–34—insult, imprisonment, loss of property, but no martyrdom—fits what is known of the expulsion of Jews from Rome under the emperor Claudius around AD 49 (Suetonius, Claudius 25.4; cf. Acts 18:2). Many commentators therefore conclude that Hebrews was sent to a congregation (perhaps a house church) in or near Rome. Other proposals—Jerusalem, Alexandria, Antioch, Asia Minor—have been made, and certainty is not possible; but Rome is the most probable destination.

As to date, Hebrews was certainly written before 1 Clement (c. AD 96). Several features point to a date before AD 70, when the Romans destroyed Jerusalem and its temple and the sacrifices ceased:

  • The author repeatedly speaks of the Levitical ministry in the present tense, as still going on (8:4–5, 13; 9:6–9; 10:1–3, 11). In 10:2 he asks whether the sacrifices “would not have ceased to be offered” if they could perfect the worshipers—a question that would have been strange after they had in fact ceased.
  • The destruction of the temple would have been the most powerful possible confirmation of his argument that the old covenant was “becoming obsolete and growing old” and was “near to vanishing” (8:13), yet he never mentions it.
  • Timothy is still alive and active (13:23).
  • The congregation had not yet suffered martyrdom (12:4), which—if the letter went to Rome—suggests a date before Nero’s savage persecution of Christians in Rome after the great fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, Annals 15.44), or at its very threshold.

The present-tense descriptions could in principle be the timeless present of someone describing what Scripture says about the tabernacle, and some scholars date the book later. But taken together, the evidence makes a date in the 60s, before AD 70, the most likely, and this study adopts that view. The hearers stood, then, on the edge of a storm: a fresh wave of pressure was rising, and the question was whether they would hold fast.

Hebrews and the Old Testament

No New Testament book is so saturated with the Old Testament. Hebrews contains some thirty-five direct quotations and scores of allusions (see Appendix A). Three features of the author’s use of Scripture are especially important:

  1. Scripture is God speaking now. The author almost never says “it is written.” Instead he says “God says,” “he says,” “the Holy Spirit says,” or puts the words of a psalm on the lips of Christ (2:12–13; 10:5–7). For him the Scriptures are the living voice of God addressing the congregation “today” (3:7; 4:7, 12).
  2. The Old Testament anticipates its own fulfillment. Much of the argument rests on the observation that the Old Testament itself points beyond the arrangements of Sinai. Psalm 95, written long after Joshua led Israel into Canaan, still offers a “rest”—so Joshua did not give the final rest (4:8). Psalm 110 promises a priest “according to the order of Melchizedek”—so the Levitical priesthood was not final (7:11). Jeremiah 31 promises a “new covenant”—so the first was not faultless (8:7, 13). Psalm 40 sets God’s will above sacrifices—so the sacrifices were not the final way (10:5–10). The author is not imposing Christ on the Old Testament; he is showing that the Old Testament was reaching for him all along.
  3. Typology: shadow and reality. The tabernacle, priesthood, and sacrifices were real institutions given by God, but they were a “copy and shadow” (8:5) and “a parable for the present time” (9:9) of the heavenly sanctuary and the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ. The pattern was shown to Moses on the mountain (8:5; Exod 25:40). Hebrews’ framework is not the philosophy of Plato but the Bible’s own: a heavenly sanctuary where God dwells, and an earthly sanctuary built to its pattern, and a movement through history from promise to fulfillment, from “these last days” (1:2) to the “consummation of the ages” (9:26).

The Structure of the Book

Commentators have proposed many outlines of Hebrews, because the author weaves his themes so tightly that one section often announces the next before the present one is finished (for example, 2:17 introduces Jesus as “merciful and faithful high priest”; 3:1–6 treats his faithfulness; 4:14–5:10 his mercy). The outline used in this study is simple:

  1. God’s Final Word in His Son (1:1–2:18). The Son is superior to the angels (1:1–14); therefore do not drift from so great a salvation (2:1–4); the Son became lower than the angels for a little while to taste death for everyone, destroy the devil, and become a merciful and faithful high priest (2:5–18).
  2. The Faithful Son and the Promised Rest (3:1–4:13). Jesus is worthy of more glory than Moses (3:1–6); do not harden your hearts as the wilderness generation did, but strive to enter God’s rest (3:7–4:13).
  3. The Great High Priest after the Order of Melchizedek (4:14–7:28). Our sympathetic high priest invites us to the throne of grace (4:14–16); he is appointed by God and perfected through suffering (5:1–10); a warning against sluggishness and falling away, with assurance of God’s unchangeable promise (5:11–6:20); Melchizedek and the priest forever (7:1–28).
  4. The Better Covenant, Sanctuary, and Sacrifice (8:1–10:18). The minister of the true tabernacle and mediator of the new covenant (8:1–13); the earthly sanctuary and the heavenly, the blood of animals and the blood of Christ (9:1–28); one sacrifice for sins for all time (10:1–18).
  5. Draw Near, Hold Fast, Run with Endurance (10:19–12:29). The call to draw near and hold fast, with a solemn warning and a reminder of former faithfulness (10:19–39); the faith of the elders who received God’s testimony (11:1–40); looking to Jesus, enduring the Father’s discipline, and receiving an unshakable kingdom (12:1–29).
  6. Life Outside the Camp (13:1–25). Love, hospitality, purity, contentment, faithfulness to leaders, the sacrifice of praise and good works (13:1–19); benediction (13:20–21); final greetings (13:22–25).

Major Themes

1. The Son: God’s Final Word

Hebrews presents one of the highest Christologies in the New Testament. The Son is heir of all things and agent of creation; the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of his being; the one who upholds all things by his word (1:2–3). He is addressed in the Psalms as “God” (1:8) and as the “Lord” who laid the foundations of the earth and remains the same while creation wears out (1:10–12; 13:8). Yet Hebrews also presents, more vividly than any other epistle, the true humanity of Jesus: he shared flesh and blood (2:14), was tested in every respect as we are, yet without sin (4:15), offered up prayers with loud cries and tears (5:7), learned obedience through what he suffered (5:8), and endured the cross, despising the shame (12:2). Only one who is both fully God and truly man can be the final word of God and the perfect high priest of his people.

2. The High Priest after the Order of Melchizedek

Hebrews alone among the New Testament writings calls Jesus “high priest,” and develops the theme at length. Drawing on Psalm 110:4—“You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek”—and on the mysterious king-priest of Genesis 14, the author shows that Jesus’ priesthood rests not on descent from Levi but on God’s oath and on “the power of an indestructible life” (7:16). Because he lives forever, “he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them” (7:25).

3. “Better”: The Superiority of the New Covenant

The Greek word κρείττων kreittōn, “better,” occurs thirteen times in Hebrews—more than in the rest of the New Testament combined. Christ is better than the angels (1:4); he brings a better hope (7:19), is the guarantor of a better covenant (7:22) enacted on better promises (8:6); he offers better sacrifices (9:23); believers have a better possession (10:34), seek a better country (11:16), hope for a better resurrection (11:35), and come to blood that speaks better than Abel’s (12:24). Closely related is the word “covenant” (διαθήκη diathēkē), which occurs seventeen times in Hebrews—about half of all its New Testament uses. The new covenant promised in Jeremiah 31 is the frame of the central section of the book.

4. Once for All

Against the endless repetition of the old sacrifices—daily, yearly, never finished—Hebrews sets the single, sufficient, unrepeatable offering of Christ. The words “once” and “once for all” (ἅπαξ hapax, ἐφάπαξ ephapax) ring through chapters 7–10: he offered himself “once for all” (7:27), entered the holy places “once for all” (9:12), appeared “once” at the consummation of the ages (9:26), was offered “once” to bear the sins of many (9:28). The Levitical priests always stood, because their work was never done; Christ “sat down” (10:11–12).

5. Access: Drawing Near to God

The goal of priesthood and sacrifice is access to God. Under the first covenant, only the high priest entered the Most Holy Place, once a year, not without blood (9:7). Now believers have “boldness for entrance into the holy places by the blood of Jesus” (10:19), and are invited—again and again—to “draw near” (προσέρχομαι proserchomai, 4:16; 7:25; 10:1, 22; 11:6; 12:18, 22).

6. Faith, Endurance, and the Danger of Falling Away

Faith in Hebrews looks forward: it is “the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1). It trusts God’s promise when the fulfillment is not yet visible, and so it endures. The word “faith” (πίστις pistis) appears thirty-two times. Its opposite is unbelief that hardens the heart and turns away from the living God (3:12). Hebrews’ warnings against apostasy are among the most solemn in Scripture, and this study treats them with full seriousness. At the same time, Hebrews grounds the believer’s perseverance not in human strength but in God’s unchangeable oath (6:17–18), in Christ’s perpetual intercession (7:25), in his once-for-all perfecting of the sanctified (10:14), and in the God of peace who works in his people what is pleasing in his sight (13:20–21). The warnings and the assurances belong together: the warnings are among the means by which God keeps his people, and the assurances are given so that they may run with endurance. As the author says of his hearers, “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39).

7. Pilgrimage toward the City of God

The people of God in Hebrews are a people on the move—like Israel in the wilderness, like Abraham living in tents. They have “no lasting city here” but seek “the city that is to come” (13:14), “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (11:10). They have already come, in worship, to “Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22), and they are receiving “a kingdom that cannot be shaken” (12:28). This hope is “an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast,” fastened behind the curtain where Jesus has gone as forerunner (6:19–20).

Hebrews in the Church

Hebrews was known and used by Christians very early—in Rome by the time of Clement (c. AD 96), in Alexandria by the late second century. The Eastern churches generally received it as Pauline and canonical. In the West its acceptance was slower, partly because its authorship was uncertain and partly because some used its warnings (6:4–6; 10:26) to deny restoration to believers who had lapsed under persecution. By the late fourth century, in the time of Athanasius in the East and of Jerome and Augustine in the West, Hebrews was received throughout the church as part of the New Testament, and the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) include it. Its place in the canon was secured not by a name but by its content: the church heard in it the gospel of the apostles, centered on the person and work of Jesus Christ.

The book’s influence on Christian worship and theology is immense. It gave the church its fullest account of Christ’s priestly work, shaped the doctrine of his once-for-all atonement, grounded the believer’s access to God in prayer, and supplied Christian hope with some of its most enduring images: the anchor behind the curtain, the cloud of witnesses, the race, the city with foundations, the unshakable kingdom.

Reading Hebrews Today

The first hearers of Hebrews were tired. They had begun well, and they had suffered for it. Now the cost of confessing Jesus was rising, and the old ways looked safer. Many readers today know that weariness. Hebrews does not answer it with techniques, but with a person: “consider Jesus” (3:1); “we see Jesus” (2:9); “looking to Jesus” (12:2); “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever” (13:8). The author believes that if his hearers truly see who Jesus is and what he has done—and where he is now—they will not want to go anywhere else.

The study that follows tries to help the reader see him, verse by verse, in the words the Holy Spirit gave.
