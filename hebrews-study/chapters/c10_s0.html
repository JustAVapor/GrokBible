Hebrews 10

Once for All — Therefore Draw Near

Hebrews 10 is the hinge of the whole sermon. Its first eighteen verses bring to a close the long central exposition that began when the author announced his “main point” (8:1): Jesus is the high priest of the new covenant, who ministers in the true sanctuary on the basis of a better sacrifice. Its remaining verses (10:19–39) turn that exposition into urgent pastoral appeal. Nowhere else in Hebrews does doctrine pass into exhortation so visibly. The chapter pivots on a single word, “therefore” (οὖν oun, 10:19). Everything before it explains what Christ has done; everything after it tells the congregation what to do in light of it.

10:1–18: one sacrifice for all time. The first half is a summary and a climax. The author gathers the threads of chapters 7–9 and ties them with three Old Testament texts. The first is Psalm 40, heard as the words of the Son “when he comes into the world”: God did not desire sacrifice and offering, “but a body you prepared for me … Behold, I have come to do your will.” The second is Psalm 110:1, which shows the Son seated at God’s right hand, waiting for his enemies to become his footstool. The third is Jeremiah 31:31–34, the new-covenant promise, quoted again (from 8:8–12) as the testimony of the Holy Spirit. The argument moves in four steps. First, the law’s yearly sacrifices were only a “shadow” and could never perfect the worshiper; their very repetition was a yearly “reminder of sins” (10:1–4). Second, Christ came to do the will of God, offering his own body once for all (10:5–10). Third, the Levitical priests always stand, because their work is never finished, while Christ sat down, because his is (10:11–14). Fourth, the Spirit’s word in Jeremiah promises a forgiveness so complete that “there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:15–18).

10:19–39: draw near, hold fast, consider one another. The second half begins with a long sentence (10:19–25) that recalls what believers now “have”: boldness to enter the sanctuary by the blood of Jesus, a new and living way through the curtain, and a great priest over the house of God. Three exhortations follow: “let us draw near” with a true heart, “let us hold fast” the confession of our hope, and “let us consider one another” to stir up love and good works, not abandoning the assembly as some were doing. Then comes the fourth and most severe of the sermon’s warnings (10:26–31). For one who deliberately and persistently sins after receiving the knowledge of the truth, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.” Such a person has trampled the Son of God, treated the covenant blood as common, and insulted the Spirit of grace, and only a fearful expectation of judgment remains. The author at once balances this warning with encouragement (10:32–39). He reminds the readers of “the former days,” when, newly enlightened, they endured public abuse, stood with prisoners, and joyfully accepted the seizure of their property, knowing they had a better and lasting possession. So he urges them not to throw away their boldness. They need endurance. He seals the appeal with Habakkuk 2:3–4 in its Greek form: the coming one will come and will not delay; “my righteous one shall live by faith,” and God takes no pleasure in the one who shrinks back. That text in turn opens the great roll call of faith in chapter 11.

How the two halves belong together. The halves are bound by repeated words, and noticing them is one of the best ways to read the chapter. The Son comes “to do your will” (10:7, 9), and the readers need endurance so that, “having done the will of God,” they may receive the promise (10:36). The “body” prepared for Christ and offered once for all (10:5, 10) answers to the believers’ “body washed with pure water” (10:22). “No longer an offering for sin” (10:18) becomes, in the warning, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins” (10:26). The one in whose sacrifices God “took no pleasure” (10:6, 8) is echoed when God “takes no pleasure” in the one who shrinks back (10:38). Christ “waiting” (ἐκδεχόμενος ekdechomenos, 10:13) for his enemies to be subdued is set against the “fearful expectation” (ἐκδοχὴ ekdochē, 10:27) of judgment for God’s adversaries. And the boldness (παρρησία parrēsia) that opens the exhortation (10:19) is the boldness the readers must not throw away (10:35).

Key terms to watch. The verb “sanctify” (ἁγιάζω hagiazō) appears at 10:10, 10:14 and 10:29 (cf. 2:11; 13:12). In Hebrews it means chiefly to consecrate: to make a people clean and fit to approach a holy God. “Perfect” (τελειόω teleioō, 10:1, 14) means bringing worshipers to the goal of a cleansed conscience and real access to God. “Once for all” (ἐφάπαξ ephapax, 10:10) and “for all time” (εἰς τὸ διηνεκές eis to diēnekes, 10:1, 12, 14) stand against the endless “year by year” and “day by day” of the old order. “Draw near” (προσέρχομαι proserchomai, 10:1, 22) names what the law could not achieve and what the gospel now invites. The chapter’s movement is the gospel’s own: because Christ has finished his offering and sat down, his people may come in, stand firm, and press on.
