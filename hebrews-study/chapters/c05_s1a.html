A High Priest Appointed by God (5:1–10)

This section began at 4:14.

Translation

1For every high priest, taken from among men, is appointed on behalf of men in the things relating to God, to offer both gifts and sacrifices for sins. 2He is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is also clothed with weakness; 3and because of this he is obligated to make offering for sins, for himself just as for the people. 4And no one takes the honor for himself; he takes it only when called by God, just as Aaron was.

5So also the Christ did not glorify himself to become high priest, but he was glorified by the one who said to him,

“You are my Son; today I have begotten you,”

6as he also says in another place,

“You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.”

7In the days of his flesh he offered up both prayers and supplications, with a loud cry and tears, to the one who was able to save him out of death, and he was heard because of his reverence. 8Although he was a Son, he learned obedience from the things he suffered; 9and having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him, 10having been designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek.

Setting and Structure

These ten verses complete the paragraph the author opened at 4:14. There he called Jesus “a great high priest who has passed through the heavens” and assured his hearers that this high priest is able “to sympathize with our weaknesses” (4:15). Now he grounds that assurance. The little word “for” (γάρ gar) at the start of 5:1 shows that what follows explains what has just been said. Why may we draw near to the throne of grace with boldness (4:16)? Because Jesus truly is a high priest. He meets every requirement God set for that office, and in each case he exceeds it.

The argument moves in two balanced halves. Verses 1–4 describe “every high priest” in general terms, drawn from the Law of Moses. The present tenses (“is appointed,” “offers,” “is obligated”) describe the Levitical system as a living institution. This fits the view that Hebrews was written while the temple still stood. Verses 5–10 then apply these requirements to Christ, and they do so in reverse order. Many commentators, including Lane and Attridge, have noted the resulting mirror-image (chiastic) pattern:

  • A (5:1) The high priest’s office: appointed on behalf of men to offer sacrifices.
    • B (5:2–3) His solidarity with the people: he shares their weakness and so deals gently with them.
      • C (5:4) His call: no one takes the honor for himself, but is called by God, as Aaron was.
      • C′ (5:5–6) Christ’s call: God, who said “You are my Son,” appointed him “a priest forever.”
    • B′ (5:7–8) Christ’s solidarity: in the days of his flesh he prayed with tears and learned obedience through suffering.
  • A′ (5:9–10) Christ’s office: made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation, designated high priest after the order of Melchizedek.

The pattern brings out both likeness and difference. Christ is like Aaron in that he was called by God and genuinely shared human weakness. He is unlike Aaron at every decisive point. Aaron was called to a priesthood that passed from father to son, ended at death, and needed constant renewal. Christ was called to be “a priest forever” (5:6). Aaron’s weakness included sin, so he had to sacrifice for himself (5:3). Christ’s weakness was the weakness of suffering and mortality, and it was “without sin” (4:15). Aaron offered animals. Christ offered “prayers and supplications” (5:7) and, as the author will show later, himself (7:27; 9:14). Aaron’s sacrifices brought a provisional cleansing that had to be repeated. Christ became “the source of eternal salvation” (5:9).

The historical background is the high priesthood as Israel knew it from the Torah: the call of Aaron (Exod 28–29; Lev 8–9), the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), and the narratives that defended Aaron’s unique call against challengers (Num 16–17). The author’s first readers also knew how far the institution in their own day had fallen from that ideal. In the second century BC, the Hasmonean rulers took the high priesthood for themselves by political arrangement (1 Macc 10:18–21; 14:41). Under Herod and the Roman governors, high priests were appointed and removed at will. Josephus counts twenty-eight high priests between Herod’s reign and the fall of the temple (Ant. 20.250). Against that background, the author’s insistence that “no one takes the honor for himself” (5:4) would have rung with special force. Yet he does not attack the high priests of his day. He describes the office as God designed it, and then he shows that God has now done something greater.

The section also does important work in the larger structure of the sermon. Here Melchizedek is named for the first time (5:6, 10). Psalm 110:4 is introduced, and it will become the backbone of chapter 7. The words “made perfect” (5:9), “offered” (5:7), and “eternal salvation” (5:9) look ahead to the great central exposition of chapters 7–10. Then, just as the author reaches the threshold of that exposition, he pauses for a long exhortation (5:11–6:20). When he resumes at 6:20, he repeats almost word for word the phrase that ends our section: “a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” Hebrews 5:1–10 is therefore both a conclusion and an overture. It concludes the argument for Jesus’ compassion begun in 2:17 and 4:14. It also announces the theme of the main body of the sermon.

Verse by Verse

5:1

For every high priest, taken from among men, is appointed on behalf of men in the things relating to God, to offer both gifts and sacrifices for sins.

The author begins with a definition. “Every high priest” (πᾶς … ἀρχιερεύς pas … archiereus) means every high priest of the kind the Law of Moses established. He is not describing pagan priests. He is reading the Torah’s portrait of Aaron and his successors. Four features stand out in this one verse.

He is taken from among men. The present passive participle λαμβανόμενος lambanomenos, “being taken,” describes something done to him. God chose Aaron “from among the sons of Israel” (Exod 28:1). A high priest is not an angel or a heavenly being sent down to mediate. He is a fellow member of the people. This is exactly why chapter 2 insisted that the Son “had to be made like his brothers in every respect” (2:17). He shared “blood and flesh” (2:14) so that he might be a true representative. The angels of chapters 1–2 could not have been high priests for human beings. Only one taken from among men could be.

He is appointed on behalf of men. The verb καθίσταται kathistatai (from καθίστημι kathistēmi) means “is appointed” or “is put in charge.” It is the language of official installation, and the passive voice anticipates 5:4. Someone else does the appointing. The preposition ὑπέρ hyper, “on behalf of,” appears twice in the verse, once with “men” and once with “sins.” A high priest exists for others. His whole office is representative. When Aaron entered the holy place, he bore the names of the twelve tribes on his shoulders and over his heart (Exod 28:12, 29). He carried the people with him into God’s presence.

He acts in the things relating to God. The phrase τὰ πρὸς τὸν θεόν ta pros ton theon literally means “the things toward God.” The author used exactly the same phrase in 2:17, where Jesus became a merciful and faithful high priest “in the things relating to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people.” A prophet speaks from God to the people. A priest moves in the other direction. He represents the people before God and deals with what stands between them and God, which is their sin.

He offers gifts and sacrifices for sins. The verb προσφέρῃ prospherē (from προσφέρω prospherō, “bring to, offer”) is the regular word in the Greek Old Testament for bringing an offering to the altar. Hebrews uses it some twenty times, more than any other New Testament book. The pair “gifts and sacrifices” (δῶρά τε καὶ θυσίας dōra te kai thysias) returns in 8:3 and 9:9. Some interpreters try to divide the two, taking “gifts” as grain offerings and “sacrifices” as animal offerings. But the pair probably works as a single comprehensive expression, “offerings of every kind,” just as we might say “goods and services.” The phrase “for sins” (ὑπὲρ ἁμαρτιῶν hyper hamartiōn) shows what lies at the center of the priestly task. The high priest had other duties, such as burning incense, tending the lamps, and blessing the people. But his supreme work was the annual Day of Atonement, when he entered the Most Holy Place with blood “for himself and for his house and for all the assembly of Israel” (Lev 16:17). Hebrews will expound that day at length in chapter 9.

This verse also sets the terms for everything that follows. If Jesus is a high priest, he too must be taken from among men, appointed by God, and have “something to offer” (8:3). The author will eventually show what he offered: not the blood of goats and calves, but “his own blood” (9:12), “himself without blemish to God” (9:14). For now the reader is simply asked to hold in mind the job description. A high priest is a man appointed by God to stand for sinners before God and to deal with their sins by sacrifice. That is precisely what the human heart needs and cannot provide for itself.

5:2

He is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is also clothed with weakness;

The first verse described the high priest’s office. This verse describes his disposition. Because he is “taken from among men,” he can relate to the people he represents with a particular kind of compassion. The Greek word for this is unusual and carefully chosen.

Word Study: μετριοπαθέω metriopatheō

The verb appears only here in the Bible. It joins μέτριος metrios, “moderate, measured,” with πάθος pathos, “feeling, passion.” Literally it means “to feel in moderation.” The word belongs to the vocabulary of Greek moral philosophy. The Stoics taught that the wise man should be free of the passions altogether (apatheia). Aristotle and his followers taught instead that the passions should be governed and kept within proper bounds (metriopatheia). Philo, the Jewish philosopher of Alexandria, used the word for Abraham’s grief at Sarah’s death: he mourned, but with restraint (On Abraham 257). In one passage Philo even contrasts Moses, who cuts the passions out entirely, with Aaron, the high priest, who moderates them (Allegorical Interpretation 3.129–132).

Applied to a priest’s dealings with sinners, the word describes a middle course between two failures. One failure is harsh severity, which flares up in anger at every fault and drives the sinner away. The other is indulgent softness, which shrugs at sin and so fails to take it seriously. The high priest who metriopathei neither explodes nor excuses. He bears patiently with the weak while still bringing their sin to the altar. “Deal gently” is a fair English rendering, as long as it includes the idea of restraint as well as kindness.

It is worth noticing that the author did not use this word of Jesus. In 4:15 he said Jesus is able to “sympathize” (συμπαθῆσαι sympathēsai, literally “to suffer with”) with our weaknesses. Some commentators see a deliberate contrast here. The best a human priest can manage is a measured restraint toward sinners. Christ goes further. He enters into our condition and feels it with us. Whether or not the contrast is intended, the movement from 4:15 to 5:2 sets the human high priest’s compassion beside Christ’s, and Christ’s is the fuller of the two.

The ignorant and wayward. The objects of the priest’s gentleness are τοῖς ἀγνοοῦσι καὶ πλανωμένοις tois agnoousi kai planōmenois. The single article “the” governs both participles. That suggests one group described in two ways, not two separate groups. ἀγνοέω agnoeō means “to not know, to act in ignorance.” πλανάω planaō in the passive means “to go astray, wander.” It is the word Hebrews used for the wilderness generation who “always go astray in their heart” (3:10). The two words together describe people who sin through weakness, error, and wandering, rather than through deliberate, defiant rebellion.

Old Testament Text: Numbers 15:27–31

The Law of Moses distinguished sharply between two kinds of sin. On one side were sins committed בִשְׁגָגָה bishgagah, “in error” or “inadvertently” (Lev 4:2; 5:18; Num 15:22–29). For these, sacrifice was provided: “the priest shall make atonement for the person who errs, when he sins in error before the Lord, to make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven” (Num 15:28).

On the other side was the sin committed with a high hand. Hebrew: וְהַנֶּפֶשׁ אֲשֶׁר־תַּעֲשֶׂה בְּיָד רָמָה … אֶת־יְהוָה הוּא מְגַדֵּף — vehannefesh asher-ta‘aseh beyad ramah … et-YHWH hu megaddef, “But the person who acts with a high hand … that one blasphemes the Lord” (Num 15:30). The next verse explains: כִּי דְבַר־יְהוָה בָּזָה ki devar-YHWH bazah, “for he has despised the word of the Lord” (Num 15:31). For such a person, the law named no sacrifice. He was to be “cut off” from his people.

Hebrews 5:2 is speaking about the first category. The priest deals gently with those who stray through weakness and ignorance. Hebrews 9:7 uses the related noun, ἀγνοημάτων agnoēmatōn, “sins of ignorance,” to describe what the Day of Atonement covered. The other category is not forgotten. In 6:4–6 and 10:26–29 the author warns of those who “sin willfully” after receiving the knowledge of the truth and so trample underfoot the Son of God. That is the high-handed sin of Numbers 15:30–31, the sin that despises the word of the Lord.

We should not draw this line too mechanically, however. The Day of Atonement cleansed the sanctuary and the people from “all their transgressions” (פִּשְׁעֵיהֶם pish‘eyhem, a strong word for rebellion) as well as their impurities (Lev 16:16, 21). And Scripture tells of grievous, deliberate sinners, David above all, who were forgiven when they repented (2 Sam 12:13; Ps 51). The “high hand” of Numbers 15 is not every sin committed knowingly. It is the settled, defiant rejection of God’s word that refuses the remedy God provides. That is the danger Hebrews will warn about. Here, though, the emphasis is pastoral. The ones who need the priest are the weak, the confused, and the wandering. They are exactly the kind of people the readers were in danger of becoming.

Clothed with weakness. The reason the high priest can deal gently is that “he himself is also clothed with weakness.” The verb περίκειται perikeitai (from περίκειμαι perikeimai) means “to lie around, be wrapped around.” In 12:1 the same verb describes the “cloud of witnesses” that surrounds us. Here it pictures weakness as something the priest wears. There may be a quiet irony in this. The high priest of Israel was dressed in garments “for glory and for beauty” (Exod 28:2): the ephod, the jeweled breastpiece, and the golden plate engraved “Holy to the Lord.” Yet beneath that splendor he was wrapped in the same frailty as everyone else. The word ἀσθένεια astheneia, “weakness,” links back to 4:15 (“our weaknesses”) and forward to 7:28 (“the law appoints men in their weakness as high priests”). In the case of Aaron and his sons, this weakness included moral weakness and sin, as the next verse makes plain. In Christ’s case it did not, but he truly took on the weakness of human flesh, its fatigue, sorrow, temptation, and mortality (2:14–18; 5:7).

5:3

and because of this he is obligated to make offering for sins, for himself just as for the people.

“Because of this” (δι’ αὐτήν di’ autēn, literally “because of it”) refers to the weakness just mentioned. The feminine pronoun matches the feminine noun astheneia. The weakness that makes the high priest gentle also makes him guilty. He is not only a mediator for sinners. He is himself a sinner who needs a mediator.

The verb ὀφείλει opheilei, “he is obligated, he must,” expresses a duty that the Law imposed. On the day Aaron began his ministry, Moses commanded him: קְרַב אֶל־הַמִּזְבֵּחַ וַעֲשֵׂה אֶת־חַטָּאתְךָ וְאֶת־עֹלָתֶךָ וְכַפֵּר בַּעַדְךָ וּבְעַד הָעָם qerav el-hammizbeach va‘aseh et-chatta’tekha ve’et-‘olatekha vekhapper ba‘adkha uv‘ad ha‘am, “Draw near to the altar and offer your sin offering and your burnt offering, and make atonement for yourself and for the people” (Lev 9:7). The same order governed the Day of Atonement. Before he could do anything for Israel, Aaron had to offer “the bull of the sin offering which is for himself, and make atonement for himself and for his house” (Lev 16:6, 11). Only after that could he slaughter the goat for the people (Lev 16:15). The Law even provided a special sin offering for “the anointed priest” who sinned and so brought guilt on the people (Lev 4:3).

Historical Background: The High Priest’s Confession

The Mishnah, which records Jewish tradition about temple practice compiled around AD 200, describes how the high priest on the Day of Atonement laid his hands on his own bull and confessed his sins and those of his household before God (m. Yoma 3:8; 4:2). Only afterward did he confess on behalf of the priests and then of all Israel. Whatever the exact age of these details, they reflect what Leviticus requires. The man who would plead for the nation began by pleading for himself. Every year, the most sacred act of Israel’s worship opened with a confession that the high priest was himself a sinner.

The author mentions this requirement here without comment, but he is laying the groundwork for one of his main arguments. In 7:27 he will return to it: Christ “has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins and then for those of the people.” In 9:7 he will note that the high priest entered the Most Holy Place “not without blood, which he offers for himself and for the sins of the people committed in ignorance.” Christ is the high priest who is “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26). He was tempted in every respect as we are, “yet without sin” (4:15).

So at this point the likeness between Aaron and Christ becomes a contrast. Aaron could deal gently with sinners partly because he was one of them. Christ deals with sinners out of a deeper compassion, not that of a fellow offender but that of one who has felt the full weight of temptation without yielding (2:18). Calvin observes that the weakness of the Levitical priests served to make them humane toward others. Christ, he notes, shares our infirmities but not our sins, and so his compassion is not compromised by any need of his own. There is great comfort in this for the believer. The priest who receives us is not distracted by his own guilt. He is free to give himself entirely to our need.

5:4

And no one takes the honor for himself; he takes it only when called by God, just as Aaron was.

The third qualification is the most fundamental. A high priest must be called by God. The word order in Greek puts the emphasis on “for himself”: οὐχ ἑαυτῷ τις λαμβάνει τὴν τιμήν ouch heautō tis lambanei tēn timēn, “not for himself does anyone take the honor.” The verb is the same one used in 5:1 (λαμβάνω lambanō, “take”). There the high priest “is taken” by God from among men. Here no one may “take” the office for himself. The priesthood is something one receives, not something one seizes.

The office is called “the honor” (τὴν τιμήν tēn timēn). In the ancient Mediterranean world, honor was the most prized social good. Public offices, especially priesthoods, were sought after as marks of distinction. In many Greek cities priesthoods could even be purchased. Israel’s high priesthood, too, was the highest honor in the nation. Precisely for that reason the author insists that it lay entirely in God’s gift. The participle καλούμενος kaloumenos, “being called,” is passive, and the agent is named: ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ hypo tou theou, “by God.”

Just as Aaron. Aaron did not volunteer. God said to Moses: וְאַתָּה הַקְרֵב אֵלֶיךָ אֶת־אַהֲרֹן אָחִיךָ וְאֶת־בָּנָיו אִתּוֹ מִתּוֹךְ בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל לְכַהֲנוֹ־לִי ve’attah haqrev eleykha et-Aharon achikha ve’et-banav itto mittokh beney Yisra’el lekhahano-li, “And you, bring near to yourself Aaron your brother, and his sons with him, from among the sons of Israel, to serve me as priest” (Exod 28:1). Notice “from among the sons of Israel.” This is the “taken from among men” of Hebrews 5:1. The priesthood was given to Aaron’s line “by a perpetual statute” (Exod 29:9).

Historical Background: Those Who Seized the Priesthood

Israel’s Scriptures record several attempts to take the priestly honor without God’s call, and each ended in judgment.

  • Korah and his company (Num 16–17). Korah was a Levite who already served at the tabernacle, but he wanted more. Moses rebuked him: וּבִקַּשְׁתֶּם גַּם־כְּהֻנָּה uviqqashtem gam-kehunnah, “and you seek the priesthood also?” (Num 16:10). The test of the censers would show הָאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר־יִבְחַר יְהוָה הוּא הַקָּדוֹשׁ ha’ish asher-yivchar YHWH hu haqqadosh, “the man whom the Lord chooses, he is the holy one” (Num 16:7). The earth swallowed the rebels and fire consumed the 250 who offered incense. Their bronze censers were hammered into a covering for the altar as a memorial “so that no outsider, who is not of the offspring of Aaron, should draw near to burn incense before the Lord, and become like Korah and his company” (Num 17:5 in Hebrew; 16:40 in English Bibles). Then God confirmed Aaron’s call by making his staff sprout, blossom, and bear almonds overnight (Num 17:16–23 in Hebrew; 17:1–8 in English). Hebrews remembers that staff as one of the treasures kept in the ark (9:4).
  • King Saul grew impatient waiting for Samuel and offered the burnt offering himself. For this he was told that his kingdom would not continue (1 Sam 13:8–14).
  • King Uzziah, at the height of his power, “became proud, to his destruction,” and entered the temple to burn incense. Azariah the priest and eighty others withstood him: “It is not for you, Uzziah, to burn incense to the Lord, but for the priests, the sons of Aaron, who are consecrated to burn incense” (2 Chr 26:18). While he raged against them, leprosy broke out on his forehead. He remained a leper, cut off from the house of the Lord, until the day he died (2 Chr 26:16–21).

The lesson of these stories is consistent. Access to God is on God’s terms. The one who mediates between God and man must be appointed by God, or his mediation is not only worthless but deadly.

The principle of 5:4 raises an obvious question. Jesus was not from the tribe of Levi, let alone from the house of Aaron. He came from Judah, “a tribe about which Moses said nothing concerning priests” (7:14). By what right, then, can he be called a high priest? Has he not taken the honor for himself, as Korah tried to do? The author’s answer comes in the next two verses. Jesus has been called by God just as truly as Aaron was, but by a different word and to a different and higher order. The same God who said “no one takes this honor for himself” is the God who said, “You are a priest forever.”

The principle also speaks to the church. Ministry in Christ’s church is not a platform for self-promotion. The apostles did not appoint themselves (Gal 1:1). Those who serve God’s people do so as stewards of a gift, not owners of a prize (1 Pet 4:10–11). Above all, the believer’s access to God never rests on self-appointed mediators. It rests on the one Mediator whom God himself has called and installed.
