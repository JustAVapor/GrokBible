7:20

And to the degree that it was not without an oath—for those men have become priests without an oath,

The author now turns to another phrase of Psalm 110:4, its opening words: “The Lord has sworn.” Verses 20–22 form one long sentence, with a parenthesis in the middle. The frame is a comparison of degree: “to the degree that” (καθ’ ὅσον kath’ hoson, 7:20) … “to that same degree” (κατὰ τοσοῦτο kata tosouto, 7:22). The point is that the superiority of Jesus’ covenant corresponds exactly to the superiority of the way he was appointed. A priesthood sworn in by God’s oath is as much greater than one appointed without an oath as the covenant it guarantees is greater than the old.

The key word is ὁρκωμοσία horkōmosia, “oath-taking, the swearing of an oath.” It appears four times in the New Testament, all in this paragraph (7:20 twice, 21, 28). The repetition is intentional: the oath is the decisive difference the author wants his hearers to see.

“For those men have become priests without an oath.” The Levitical priests were appointed by the law’s commandment, and their right passed automatically from father to son. Nowhere in the law does God swear an oath to Aaron and his sons that they would be priests forever. (When the law calls their priesthood “perpetual,” Exod 29:9; 40:15, it states a regulation, not a sworn divine pledge, and the regulation was always open to God’s further word.) The author uses the perfect participle (γεγονότες gegonotes, “have become”) to describe the established state of things under the law: every one of them held office without a divine oath.

The author has prepared his hearers for this argument in 6:13–18, where he spoke of God’s oath to Abraham: “since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself” (6:13), so that “by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement” (6:18). In Hebrews an oath of God signals finality and unchangeability. When God swears, the matter is settled forever.

7:21

but this one with an oath, through the one who said to him, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind, ‘You are a priest forever’”—

“But this one”—Jesus—became priest “with an oath” (μετὰ ὁρκωμοσίας meta horkōmosias). The oath was spoken “through the one who said to him,” that is, God, who addresses the Messiah directly in Psalm 110 (“The Lord says to my Lord,” 110:1). Here, for the first time in Hebrews, the author quotes the opening words of Psalm 110:4, which he had left out in 5:6 and 7:17: “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind.” In the Greek, “Lord” (κύριος kyrios) here stands for the divine name YHWH of the Hebrew.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 110:4a — The Oath

Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם — nishba‘ YHWH velo’ yinnachem, “The Lord has sworn and will not relent.”

The verb נִשְׁבַּע nishba‘, “he has sworn,” is the same verb used when God swore to Abraham: “By myself I have sworn (בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי bi nishba‘ti), declares the Lord” (Gen 22:16), the oath the author discussed in 6:13–17. The two great oaths of God that Hebrews highlights, to Abraham and to the Messiah, are expressed in the same terms.

The second verb, יִנָּחֵם yinnachem, from nicham, means “to be sorry, to relent, to change one’s mind.” Scripture sometimes says that God “relented” from a threatened judgment when people repented (Exod 32:14; Jonah 3:10), describing his real responsiveness within history. But Scripture is equally clear that when God has bound himself by a solemn word, he does not go back on it: “God is not a man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind (וְיִתְנֶחָם veyitnecham). Has he said, and will he not do it?” (Num 23:19; compare 1 Sam 15:29). In Psalm 110:4, the words “and will not relent” make the oath irrevocable. The Greek translation that Hebrews quotes, οὐ μεταμεληθήσεται ou metamelēthēsetai, “he will not regret, will not change his mind,” conveys the same sense. God will never withdraw this appointment.

The author stops the quotation at “You are a priest forever” and omits “according to the order of Melchizedek,” because his point here is the oath and the permanence it guarantees. (A number of later manuscripts, followed by the King James Version, add the phrase “according to the order of Melchizedek” here, but the earliest and best manuscripts lack it. It was almost certainly added by scribes to complete the familiar quotation; the meaning is not affected.)

Why does the oath matter so much? Because it shows that the priesthood of the Messiah is not a temporary or provisional arrangement, as the Levitical priesthood turned out to be. The law of Moses, given without an oath regarding the priesthood, could be superseded by a later word of God; and indeed it was, in Psalm 110. But an oath of God, sworn with the explicit declaration that he “will not change his mind,” can never be superseded. There will be no further priesthood after this one. Christ’s priesthood is God’s final and irrevocable provision for his people.

7:22

to that same degree Jesus has become the guarantor of a better covenant.

Here the long sentence reaches its climax. “To that same degree” (κατὰ τοσοῦτο kata tosouto) answers “to the degree that” in 7:20. As the oath raises his priesthood above theirs, so the covenant he guarantees is raised above theirs.

The Greek word order places the name “Jesus” at the very end of the sentence: “of a better covenant has become guarantor—Jesus.” Hebrews often uses the human name Jesus at points of special emphasis (2:9; 3:1; 4:14; 6:20; 10:19; 12:2, 24; 13:12, 20). The one sworn in by God’s oath as priest forever is the man Jesus, who suffered and died and was raised.

This is the first time the word “covenant” (διαθήκη diathēkē) appears in Hebrews. It will occur seventeen times in the letter, more than in any other New Testament book, and chapters 8–10 will make it a central theme. The “better covenant” is the new covenant promised in Jeremiah 31:31–34, which the author will quote at length in 8:8–12. Its betterness consists in what it accomplishes: God’s law written on the heart, the knowledge of God for all his people, and the complete and final forgiveness of sins (8:10–12). The author here introduces the theme by connecting it directly to Jesus’ priesthood: a new priest means a new law (7:12) and a better covenant (7:22).

Word Study: ἔγγυος engyos

The word engyos, “guarantor, surety,” occurs only here in the New Testament. In the legal and commercial world of the time, an engyos was a person who stood security for another: one who guaranteed that a debt would be paid, a contract fulfilled, or an appearance in court made, and who was personally liable if it was not. The Greek wisdom book of Sirach warns about the risks of standing surety and also urges the one who has been rescued by a guarantor not to forget his kindness, since the guarantor has put his own life on the line for him (Sir 29:14–20).

The idea has a moving Old Testament illustration in the story of Judah, the very tribe from which Jesus came (7:14). When Jacob was unwilling to let Benjamin go to Egypt, Judah said: “I myself will be surety for him (אָנֹכִי אֶעֶרְבֶנּוּ ’anoki ’e‘ervennu); from my hand you may require him” (Gen 43:9). Later he offered himself as a slave in Benjamin’s place, because “your servant became surety for the boy” (Gen 44:32–33). The author does not draw this connection, and we should not press it; but it shows that the idea of a guarantor who puts himself in another’s place was deeply rooted in Israel’s story.

In what sense is Jesus the guarantor of the better covenant? Some interpreters stress that he guarantees the covenant to us—his priesthood, sworn by God’s oath, is the pledge that the covenant’s promises will certainly be fulfilled for those who belong to him. Others stress that he stands surety for us before God, taking upon himself our obligations, having paid our debt by his death. Both ideas fit Hebrews. In 8:6 and 9:15 the author will call Jesus the “mediator” (μεσίτης mesitēs) of the new covenant, the one who stands between God and his people and establishes the covenant by his blood. Here the emphasis falls on certainty: because God has sworn, and because Jesus lives forever (7:24–25), the covenant he guarantees cannot fail. He is himself the living pledge of all it promises.

For the hearers, this verse answers a deep anxiety. If they let go of the old covenant’s visible guarantees—the temple, the priests, the sacrifices—what security would they have? The author’s answer: you have a better security. Not stone and ritual, but a living person, appointed by God’s oath, who himself guarantees the covenant. A guarantor is only as good as his ability to pay and his permanence. Jesus can pay (he offered himself, 7:27), and he lives forever (7:24).

7:23

And those men, on the one hand, have become priests in great number, because they were prevented by death from continuing;

The author now draws out the word “forever” one last time, comparing the many priests of the old order with the one priest of the new. Verses 23–24 again use the μέν … δέ men … de construction: “those men, on the one hand … but he, on the other hand.”

“Those men have become priests in great number” (πλείονές εἰσιν γεγονότες ἱερεῖς pleiones eisin gegonotes hiereis). The comparative pleiones, “more, many,” points to the long succession of high priests, one after another. Josephus, writing near the end of the first century, counted eighty-three high priests from Aaron to the destruction of the temple in AD 70 (Ant. 20.227). In the century before the temple’s fall, under Herod and the Roman governors, high priests were appointed and deposed with bewildering frequency. The hearers would have known how many men had worn the high priest’s robes in their own lifetime.

Why so many? “Because they were prevented by death from continuing” (διὰ τὸ θανάτῳ κωλύεσθαι παραμένειν dia to thanatō kōlyesthai paramenein). Death “hindered” or “prevented” them (kōlyō, “to hinder, forbid”) from “remaining on” (paramenō, “to continue, stay on in office”). Death was the one enemy no priest of Aaron’s line could overcome. Aaron himself died on Mount Hor, and the scene of his death shows how the priesthood passed on: God told Moses, “Strip Aaron of his garments and put them on Eleazar his son; and Aaron shall be gathered to his people and shall die there” (Num 20:26; Hebrew וְהַפְשֵׁט אֶת־אַהֲרֹן אֶת־בְּגָדָיו וְהִלְבַּשְׁתָּם אֶת־אֶלְעָזָר בְּנוֹ vehafshet ’et-’aharon ’et-begadav vehilbashtam ’et-’el‘azar beno, “strip Aaron of his garments and clothe Eleazar his son with them”). The priestly robes outlived the priest. They were passed from body to body down the generations.

This means that under the old covenant, no worshiper could have a permanent relationship with the priest who represented him. Every priest would eventually be taken away. The ministry of intercession and atonement was always interrupted by death.

7:24

but he, because he remains forever, holds his priesthood permanently.

The contrast is sharp: “but he” (ὁ δέ ho de). Not many priests, but one. Not prevented by death, but “because he remains forever” (διὰ τὸ μένειν αὐτὸν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα dia to menein auton eis ton aiōna). The verb “remain” (menō) was used of Melchizedek in 7:3 (“he remains a priest perpetually”) and of the Son in 1:11 (“you remain,” quoting Ps 102:26). What was true of Melchizedek in the portrait of Scripture is true of Jesus in reality. Note also the parallel construction: the former priests could not “remain on” (paramenein, 7:23), but he “remains” (menein) forever.

Because he remains, “he holds his priesthood permanently” (ἀπαράβατον ἔχει τὴν ἱερωσύνην aparabaton echei tēn hierōsynēn).

Word Study: ἀπαράβατος aparabatos

This adjective occurs only here in the New Testament. It is formed from the negative a- and the verb parabainō, “to step aside, to pass over, to transgress.” Two meanings have been proposed:

  • “Permanent, unchangeable, inviolable.” This is the meaning the word usually has in other Greek writings, where it is used, for example, of laws or decrees that cannot be broken or altered. Most lexicons (including BDAG) favor this sense.
  • “Untransferable, without a successor.” Some interpreters, ancient and modern, understand it as “not passing to another,” in contrast to the Levitical priesthood, which passed from father to son. This meaning fits the context of 7:23–24 very well, but clear examples of the word with this meaning in other Greek writings are scarce.

In practice the two meanings come to the same thing in this context. A priesthood that is permanent and unchangeable, held by one who lives forever, never passes to anyone else. Jesus will never be replaced, and he will never need a successor. His priesthood is his alone and his forever.

The word for “priesthood” here is ἱερωσύνη hierōsynē, the office of priest, the same word used in 7:11–12. The Levitical priesthood has been changed (7:12); Christ’s priesthood will never change. The pastoral significance is immense. The believer’s High Priest today is the same one who served the first Christians, and he will be the same tomorrow. “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever” (13:8).

7:25

Hence he is also able to save completely those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them.

This verse is the great practical conclusion of the argument about Christ’s permanent priesthood, and one of the most precious promises in the New Testament. Everything the author has said about Melchizedek, the oath, and the indestructible life now issues in this: Jesus is able to save. The word “hence” (ὅθεν hothen, “from which, for this reason”) ties the promise directly to 7:24: because he remains forever and holds his priesthood permanently, “he is also able to save.”

“He is able to save.” The verb “is able” (δύναται dynatai) speaks of capacity, power. Hebrews loves to speak of what Jesus is “able” to do: “he is able to help those who are being tempted” (2:18); he is not “unable to sympathize with our weaknesses” (4:15). The Levitical priests were not able to bring perfection (7:11, 19; 10:1, 11). Jesus is able. And what he is able to do is “to save” (σῴζειν sōzein). In Hebrews salvation is above all a future inheritance, which believers will receive at Christ’s return (1:14; 9:28), but it is grounded in what Christ has done and is doing now. He is “the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him” (5:9) and the “pioneer of their salvation” (2:10). The present tense of the infinitive (sōzein) suggests an ongoing activity: he keeps on saving.

Interpretive Options: εἰς τὸ παντελές eis to panteles

The phrase eis to panteles is built on the adjective παντελής pantelēs, “complete, entire” (from pan, “all,” and telos, “end, goal”). It occurs in only one other place in the New Testament: in Luke 13:11 a woman bent over for eighteen years “was not able to straighten herself up eis to panteles”—that is, “at all” or “completely.” In other Greek writings the phrase can mean either “completely, entirely” or “for all time, forever.” So there are two main ways of taking it here:

  • Qualitative: “completely, to the uttermost, fully.” Jesus saves entirely, in every respect, from every aspect of sin and its consequences, bringing his people all the way to the goal. This fits the theme of “perfection” (telos is part of the word) and the contrast with a law that “made nothing perfect” (7:19).
  • Temporal: “forever, for all time.” Jesus saves for all time, because he lives forever. This fits the immediate context, which is all about permanence: “he remains forever” (7:24), “he always lives” (7:25).

Evaluation. The context strongly emphasizes time (“forever,” “permanently,” “always”), yet the word itself contains the idea of completeness and links naturally to the theme of perfection. Many commentators suggest that the author may intend both ideas, and that seems right. The two are not really separable: a salvation that lasts forever is a complete salvation, and a salvation that is complete must be one that never fails. Because Jesus never dies and his priesthood never lapses, there is no point in time at which his saving work is interrupted, and no aspect of our need that falls outside it. He saves completely and for all time. The old English rendering “to the uttermost” captures both well.

“Those who draw near to God through him.” The beneficiaries of this salvation are described not by ethnicity, ancestry, or merit, but by what they do: they “draw near to God” (τοὺς προσερχομένους … τῷ θεῷ tous proserchomenous … tō theō). The verb proserchomai is a favorite in Hebrews for the approach of worshipers to God. The author has already urged, “Let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16), and will urge it again: “let us draw near with a true heart” (10:22); “whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists” (11:6); “you have drawn near to Mount Zion” (12:22). The present participle describes a continuing habit of life: those who keep on coming to God.

They come “through him” (δι’ αὐτοῦ di’ autou). Jesus is the way of access. No one approaches God on his own; under the old covenant, the people approached through the priests, and now all believers approach through Jesus. Jesus said, “No one comes to the Father except through me” (John 14:6); Paul writes, “through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father” (Eph 2:18). This is a qualification as well as an invitation: the promise of complete salvation is for those who come to God through Jesus, not for those who come some other way or do not come at all. The hearers, tempted to turn back to the old priests, needed to hear that salvation is found only by drawing near through this High Priest.

“Since he always lives to intercede for them.” The final clause explains why his saving power never fails. The participle “living” (ζῶν zōn) is joined to “always” (πάντοτε pantote), and the purpose of his life is stated: “to intercede for them” (εἰς τὸ ἐντυγχάνειν ὑπὲρ αὐτῶν eis to entynchanein hyper autōn). Notice the echo of 7:8, “it is testified that he lives”: the living priest of the Genesis portrait finds his fulfillment in Jesus, who truly lives always.

Word Study: ἐντυγχάνω entynchanō

The verb entynchanō means “to meet with, to approach someone, to appeal to someone on behalf of another or against another.” It was used of presenting a petition to a ruler or official. In Acts 25:24 the Jewish leaders “appealed” to Festus about Paul. In Romans 11:2 Elijah “pleads with God against Israel.” In Romans 8 it is used both of the Holy Spirit, who “intercedes for the saints according to the will of God” (8:27), and of the risen Christ: “Christ Jesus is the one who died—more than that, who was raised—who is at the right hand of God, who indeed intercedes for us” (Rom 8:34). With ὑπέρ hyper, “on behalf of,” it means to act as advocate and representative for someone before a higher authority.

What does Christ’s intercession involve? Scripture does not describe it in detail, and we should not imagine a reluctant Father who must be persuaded by a kinder Son. It is the Father who appointed the Son as priest by his oath (7:21) and who “so loved the world that he gave his only Son” (John 3:16). Nor should we picture Christ as endlessly repeating his sacrifice or pleading as though his work were incomplete; the very next verses insist that he offered himself “once for all” (7:27), and 10:12 says that after offering one sacrifice for sins he “sat down.” Rather, his intercession is the continuing ministry of the crucified and risen High Priest, who appears “in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24), whose finished sacrifice is the permanent ground of our acceptance, and who actively applies its benefits to his people in their ongoing need. John expresses the same truth with a legal image: “if anyone sins, we have an advocate (παράκλητον paraklēton) with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous; and he himself is the propitiation for our sins” (1 John 2:1–2). His advocacy and his atoning sacrifice belong together.

The Old Testament anticipated this intercessory work. The suffering Servant of Isaiah 53, who bore the sin of many, is also the one who makes intercession for transgressors:

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 53:12

Hebrew: וְהוּא חֵטְא־רַבִּים נָשָׂא וְלַפֹּשְׁעִים יַפְגִּיעַ׃ — vehu’ chet’-rabbim nasa’ velapposh‘im yafgia‘, “and he bore the sin of many, and for the transgressors he makes intercession.”

The verb יַפְגִּיעַ yafgia‘ (from paga‘, “to meet, to encounter”; in this form “to cause to meet, to intervene, to intercede”) describes the Servant stepping in on behalf of others. Remarkably, the same verse that says he “poured out his soul to death” and “bore the sin of many” says he “intercedes for the transgressors.” Sacrifice and intercession belong together in the Servant’s work, as they do in Hebrews 7:25–27. The author of Hebrews will echo the phrase “to bear the sins of many” in 9:28.

The intercession of the Levitical high priest had limits. He represented the people before God, bearing the names of the tribes on his shoulders and over his heart whenever he entered the holy place (Exod 28:9–12, 29), and once a year he entered the Most Holy Place to make atonement for them (Lev 16). But his representation lasted only as long as his ministry in the sanctuary, and it ended at his death. Even the great intercessors of Israel’s history—Abraham pleading for Sodom (Gen 18:22–33), Moses standing in the breach for the people (Exod 32:11–14, 30–32; Ps 106:23), Samuel, who said, “far be it from me that I should sin against the Lord by ceasing to pray for you” (1 Sam 12:23)—all eventually died. Jesus “always lives.” His intercession is never interrupted, never weary, never outlived.

For believers struggling with weakness, temptation, failure, and fear—and the first hearers were tempted to drift away under pressure—this verse is a fortress. Salvation does not depend on the strength of their grip on Christ but on the strength of his priesthood and the constancy of his intercession for them. Jesus prayed for Peter that his faith would not fail (Luke 22:32); he prayed for all who would believe through the apostles’ word (John 17:20). He continues that ministry at God’s right hand. The one who drew near to God through Jesus yesterday will be held by the same living intercession today and tomorrow. At the same time, the verse calls for a response: the promise is for “those who draw near to God through him.” The answer to drifting is not to try harder in one’s own strength but to keep coming to God through the High Priest who always lives.

7:26

For such a high priest was indeed fitting for us: holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and become higher than the heavens;

The final paragraph of the chapter (7:26–28) gathers up the argument in a kind of hymn of praise to the High Priest. The word “such” (τοιοῦτος toioutos) looks both backward and forward: backward to the priest who lives forever and always intercedes (7:25), and forward to the description that follows. The title also changes: the author has been speaking of a “priest” (ἱερεύς hiereus), the word of Psalm 110:4; now he says “high priest” (ἀρχιερεύς archiereus), returning to his favorite title for Jesus (2:17; 3:1; 4:14–15; 5:5, 10; 6:20) and preparing for the discussion of the high priest’s ministry in chapters 8–10.

“Was indeed fitting for us” (ἡμῖν καὶ ἔπρεπεν hēmin kai eprepen). The verb prepō, “to be fitting, appropriate,” was used in 2:10: “it was fitting for him … to make the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings.” There it described what was fitting for God; here it describes what was fitting for us—what our need required. Sinners who must draw near to a holy God need a high priest of exactly this kind. The word “indeed” (kai) adds emphasis: such a high priest is precisely what suited our situation.

Five descriptions follow. The first three are adjectives describing his character; the last two are participial phrases describing his position.

  • “Holy” (ὅσιος hosios). This word describes devout, God-pleasing piety, complete devotion to God. In the Greek Old Testament it often translates the Hebrew חָסִיד chasid, “faithful, godly one.” It is the word used in Psalm 16:10, “you will not let your Holy One see corruption” (Hebrew חֲסִידְךָ chasideka, “your faithful one”), which the apostles applied to Jesus’ resurrection (Acts 2:27; 13:35).
  • “Innocent” (ἄκακος akakos), literally “without evil,” guileless, free from malice or wrongdoing. Jesus never did harm to anyone; “he committed no sin, and no deceit was found in his mouth” (1 Pet 2:22, echoing Isa 53:9).
  • “Undefiled” (ἀμίαντος amiantos), “unstained, pure.” The word has a ritual background. The high priest had to guard himself scrupulously from ceremonial defilement; he could not even go near the dead body of his father or mother (Lev 21:10–12). Hebrews uses the same word of the “undefiled” marriage bed (13:4). Jesus touched lepers and the dead and ate with sinners, yet was never defiled; instead, his touch cleansed them. His purity was not merely ceremonial but moral and spiritual: he was “tempted in every respect as we are, yet without sin” (4:15).
  • “Separated from sinners” (κεχωρισμένος ἀπὸ τῶν ἁμαρτωλῶν kechōrismenos apo tōn hamartōlōn).
  • “Become higher than the heavens” (ὑψηλότερος τῶν οὐρανῶν γενόμενος hypsēloteros tōn ouranōn genomenos). The author has already said that Jesus “passed through the heavens” (4:14) and sat down “at the right hand of the Majesty on high” (1:3). Paul similarly speaks of Christ ascending “far above all the heavens” (Eph 4:10). Jesus’ high priesthood is exercised not in an earthly sanctuary but in the very presence of God, above every created realm.

Interpretive Options: “Separated from Sinners”

  • Moral separation. The phrase describes Jesus’ character during his earthly life: although he lived among sinners and was their friend (Luke 7:34; 15:1–2), he was entirely distinct from them in character, untouched by their sin.
  • Separation by exaltation. The phrase describes Jesus’ present position: having completed his work, he has been removed from the sphere of sinful humanity and exalted to heaven, where he is no longer exposed to the hostility of sinners (compare 12:3, “such hostility from sinners against himself”).

Evaluation. The Greek perfect participle (“having been separated” and remaining so) and the phrase that immediately follows (“become higher than the heavens”) favor the second view as the primary sense: Jesus’ separation from sinners is the result of his exaltation. But this exalted separation is possible only because of his moral separation; it is the vindication of his sinless life. Many interpreters hold both together, and rightly. There may also be a background in the high priest’s ritual separation before the Day of Atonement: the Mishnah records that seven days before that day the high priest was separated from his house and kept in a special chamber in the temple, to guard him from defilement (m. Yoma 1:1). What the ritual could only picture, Jesus is in reality.

Importantly, his separation from sinners does not mean distance from them in sympathy. The author has insisted that Jesus was made like his brothers in every respect (2:17) and can sympathize with our weaknesses (4:15). He is separated from sinners in his holiness and in his exalted place, but never in his compassion. That is precisely what makes him “fitting for us”: he is near enough to understand and help us, and holy and exalted enough to bring us to God.

7:27

who does not have need, as those high priests do, to offer up sacrifices day by day, first for his own sins, then for those of the people; for this he did once for all when he offered up himself.

Having described who Jesus is, the author contrasts what he does with what the Levitical high priests did. They had a “need” (ἀνάγκη anankē, “necessity, compulsion”) to offer sacrifices repeatedly, and to offer them first for themselves. Jesus has no such need.

“First for his own sins, then for those of the people.” The law required the high priest to make atonement for himself before making atonement for the people. On the Day of Atonement, “Aaron shall present the bull of the sin offering, which is for himself, and shall make atonement for himself and for his house” (Lev 16:6, 11; Hebrew וְכִפֶּר בַּעֲדוֹ וּבְעַד בֵּיתוֹ vekhipper ba‘ado uv‘ad beito, “and he shall make atonement for himself and for his house”), and only then did he offer the goat for the people (16:15). At the very inauguration of the priesthood Moses told Aaron, “Draw near to the altar and offer your sin offering and your burnt offering, and make atonement for yourself and for the people” (Lev 9:7). The author has already noted this in 5:3: the high priest “is obligated, as for the people, so also for himself, to offer for sins.” This was not a minor detail; it exposed the fundamental weakness of the old priesthood. The very men who represented the people before God were themselves sinners needing atonement.

Jesus has no such need, because he is “holy, innocent, undefiled” (7:26), “without sin” (4:15). He did not need to offer anything for himself. Therefore his offering could be entirely for others.

Interpretive Options: Why “Day by Day”?

A difficulty arises here. The law says that the high priest offered first for himself and then for the people on the Day of Atonement, which came “once a year” (Lev 16:34; Exod 30:10). The author knows this perfectly well; he says so explicitly in 9:7 and 9:25 and 10:3. Why then does he say the high priests offer such sacrifices “day by day” (καθ’ ἡμέραν kath’ hēmeran)? Several explanations have been offered:

  • The high priest’s daily offering. The law prescribed a regular grain offering that Aaron and his successors were to offer “continually, half of it in the morning and half in the evening” (Lev 6:20 [Hebrew 6:13]; מִנְחָה תָּמִיד מַחֲצִיתָהּ בַּבֹּקֶר וּמַחֲצִיתָהּ בָּעָרֶב minchah tamid machatsitah babboqer umachatsitah ba‘arev, “a continual grain offering, half of it in the morning and half of it in the evening”; see Lev 6:19–23 [Hebrew 6:12–16]). This offering, made by “the priest who is anointed in his place from among his sons” (6:22 [Hebrew 6:15]), that is, by each successive high priest, was wholly burned. Jewish writers of the period understood it as the high priest’s own daily offering. Sirach says of Aaron that his sacrifices were “wholly burned twice every day continually” (Sir 45:14). Josephus says that the high priest offered sacrifice twice each day at his own expense (Ant. 3.257), and Philo distinguishes the continual offering of fine flour that the priests offer for themselves from the two lambs offered for the nation (Who Is the Heir 174). Alongside it stood the daily burnt offering of two lambs, morning and evening, on behalf of the whole people (Exod 29:38–42; Num 28:3–8). So there was, in fact, a daily pattern of offerings in which the high priest’s own offering stood beside the people’s.
  • Offerings for the priest’s sins as they arose. The law also provided that “if the anointed priest sins, bringing guilt on the people,” he must offer a bull as a sin offering (Lev 4:3). Such offerings could be needed at any time.
  • A summary of the whole system. The author compresses the Levitical system into one picture: the high priest’s annual sacrifice for himself and the people, together with the never-ending daily round of sacrifices offered by the priests (10:11, “every priest stands day by day ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices”). “Day by day” characterizes the whole system as endlessly repetitive.

Evaluation. These explanations are not mutually exclusive. The author is certainly not confused about the Day of Atonement, which he describes accurately in 9:7. The law itself did require a daily offering of the high priest, alongside the daily offerings for the people, and Jewish writers of the author’s own era understood it that way. At the same time, the author’s main point is clear and does not depend on resolving every detail: the Levitical system was marked by constant repetition, and its high priests always had to deal with their own sin before they could deal with the people’s. Day after day and year after year, the offerings went on, because none of them could finish the work (10:1–4, 11). Against that background, the “once for all” of Christ’s sacrifice stands out with overwhelming force.

“For this he did once for all when he offered up himself.” What did he do? Not offer for his own sins—he had none—but offer the sacrifice for the people’s sins, which was the purpose of all priestly sacrifice. And he did it in a way no Levitical priest could have imagined.

First, he did it “once for all” (ἐφάπαξ ephapax). This is the first appearance in Hebrews of a word that becomes one of its great themes (9:12; 10:10; compare ἅπαξ hapax, “once,” in 9:26–28). The word means “once and never again,” a single act that never needs repetition because it achieved everything it was intended to achieve. Paul uses it of Christ’s death: “the death he died, he died to sin once for all” (Rom 6:10). The contrast with “day by day” could not be sharper. The old sacrifices were repeated because they were ineffective; Christ’s was not repeated because it was perfectly effective (10:1–2, 14).

Second, he offered “himself” (ἑαυτὸν ἀνενέγκας heauton anenenkas). This is the first time Hebrews explicitly says that the High Priest was also the sacrifice—a theme that will be developed fully in 9:11–14, 25–28 and 10:5–14. The Levitical priests offered animals other than themselves; Jesus offered his own life. Priest and offering are one. The verb ἀναφέρω anapherō, “to offer up,” literally “to carry up,” was used in the Greek Old Testament for bringing a sacrifice up onto the altar. James uses it of Abraham “offering up” Isaac on the altar (Jas 2:21). Peter uses it in a striking way: Christ “himself bore up our sins in his body onto the tree” (1 Pet 2:24). And in the Greek of Isaiah 53:12 the Servant “bore” the sins of many—the passage Hebrews will echo in 9:28: Christ, “having been offered once to bear the sins of many.” The aorist participle (anenenkas, “having offered up”) points to a single completed act in history: the cross.

This half-verse quietly overturns the whole sacrificial system. If the High Priest himself is the once-for-all sacrifice, then there is nothing left to offer. To return to the temple’s sacrifices, as the hearers were tempted to do, would be to treat as incomplete what God declares finished.

7:28

For the law appoints as high priests men who have weakness, but the word of the oath, which came after the law, appoints a Son made perfect forever.

The chapter ends with a summary that gathers its whole argument into one perfectly balanced sentence. In Greek the contrast is drawn point by point:

  • “The law” (ὁ νόμος ho nomos) versus “the word of the oath” (ὁ λόγος … τῆς ὁρκωμοσίας ho logos … tēs horkōmosias). The old priests were appointed by the law of Moses; Jesus was appointed by the oath of Psalm 110:4.
  • “Men” (ἀνθρώπους anthrōpous, plural) versus “a Son” (υἱόν huion, singular). The many mortal men of 7:23 are set against the one Son of God.
  • “Who have weakness” (ἔχοντας ἀσθένειαν echontas astheneian) versus “made perfect forever” (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα τετελειωμένον eis ton aiōna teteleiōmenon).

The single verb “appoints” (καθίστησιν kathistēsin) governs both halves. It is the word used in 5:1 of the high priest who is “appointed on behalf of men in matters relating to God,” and in 8:3.

“Men who have weakness.” The Levitical high priests were subject to human “weakness” (astheneia) in every sense: mortality (7:23), moral frailty and sin (7:27; 5:2–3, where the high priest “is himself beset with weakness”). The “weakness” of the priests corresponds to the “weakness” of the commandment that appointed them (7:18). Weak men appointed by a weak commandment could not bring anyone to perfection.

“The word of the oath, which came after the law.” The oath of Psalm 110 was spoken through David, centuries after Moses received the law at Sinai. The author draws a significant conclusion from the timing: the later word of God supersedes the earlier arrangement. This is the same kind of reasoning he used in 7:11 and will use in 8:13, where he says that by speaking of a “new” covenant, God “has made the first one obsolete.” Paul uses an argument from chronology in the opposite direction in Galatians 3:17: a covenant ratified by God earlier (the promise to Abraham) cannot be annulled by a law that came 430 years afterward. The principle in both cases is that God’s own sequence of revelation shows the relative standing of his arrangements. The promise to Abraham came before the law and is not undone by it; the oath to the Messiah came after the law and fulfills and replaces its priesthood. In both, the law of Sinai is shown to be a temporary arrangement within God’s larger saving purpose.

“A Son made perfect forever.” The title “Son” brings us back to the opening of the sermon, where God “has spoken to us in a Son” (1:2), and to 5:5–6, where the author linked Psalm 2:7 (“You are my Son”) with Psalm 110:4 (“You are a priest forever”). The one whom the oath appoints is not merely a better man but the Son of God, the radiance of God’s glory (1:3). Yet this Son is also “made perfect” (τετελειωμένον teteleiōmenon), a perfect passive participle: he has been brought to perfection and remains perfected. As we saw at 2:10 and 5:9, this perfection is not a movement from sin to holiness—the Son was always without sin—but the completion of his qualification as Savior and High Priest through his human obedience, suffering, death, and exaltation. “Although he was a Son, he learned obedience from what he suffered; and having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him” (5:8–9). And his perfection is “forever” (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna): it will never be lost, never diminished, never need to be supplemented.

Here the great contrasts of the chapter come to rest: law and oath, many and one, weakness and perfection, death and indestructible life, daily repetition and once for all. The last word of the chapter in Greek is “made perfect” (teteleiōmenon), echoing the question with which this section began: “If perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood …” (7:11). It was not. But it has come through the Son. The author can now move on, in chapter 8, to his “main point” (8:1): “we have such a high priest, who has sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens.”

For Reflection

  1. The author argues that the promise of a new priest in Psalm 110 proves that the Levitical priesthood could not bring “perfection.” What does this teach about how God’s later revelation in Scripture relates to his earlier revelation? How should this shape the way Christians read the Old Testament law today?
  2. Jesus became priest “not according to the law of a fleshly commandment but according to the power of an indestructible life.” In what ways are you tempted to rest your standing with God on external, “fleshly” qualifications—background, heritage, religious performance—rather than on the living Christ?
  3. God swore an oath and “will not change his mind.” How does the irrevocable character of Christ’s appointment as priest strengthen your assurance when you feel unstable or doubt God’s commitment to you?
  4. Jesus “always lives to intercede” for those who draw near to God through him. How would your prayer life, your response to failure, and your perseverance under pressure change if you lived consciously in the knowledge that the risen Christ is interceding for you now?
  5. The Levitical high priests had to offer sacrifices first for their own sins, and day by day; Jesus offered himself once for all. What practices or attitudes in your life suggest that you treat Christ’s sacrifice as though it needed supplementing? What would it mean to rest in its finality?
  6. Jesus is both “separated from sinners” and the friend of sinners who sympathizes with our weakness. How does holding both truths together guard against either a distant, fearful view of Christ or an overly casual one?
