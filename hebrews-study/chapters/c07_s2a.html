A Priest Forever (7:11–28)

Translation

11Now if perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood—for on the basis of it the people received the law—what further need would there have been for a different priest to arise according to the order of Melchizedek, and not to be named according to the order of Aaron? 12For when the priesthood is changed, of necessity a change of law also takes place. 13For the one about whom these things are said has belonged to a different tribe, from which no one has ever served at the altar. 14For it is evident that our Lord has arisen from Judah, a tribe concerning which Moses said nothing about priests.

15And it is still more abundantly evident if, according to the likeness of Melchizedek, a different priest arises, 16who has become a priest not according to the law of a fleshly commandment but according to the power of an indestructible life. 17For it is testified:

“You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.”

18For on the one hand, there is a setting aside of the former commandment because of its weakness and uselessness 19(for the law made nothing perfect); on the other hand, there is the bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God.

20And to the degree that it was not without an oath—for those men have become priests without an oath, 21but this one with an oath, through the one who said to him,

“The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind,
‘You are a priest forever’”—

22to that same degree Jesus has become the guarantor of a better covenant.

23And the former priests have become many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing; 24but he, because he remains forever, holds his priesthood permanently. 25Hence he is also able to save completely those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them.

26For such a high priest was indeed fitting for us: holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and become higher than the heavens; 27who does not have need, as those high priests do, to offer up sacrifices day by day, first for his own sins, then for those of the people; for this he did once for all when he offered up himself. 28For the law appoints as high priests men who have weakness, but the word of the oath, which came after the law, appoints a Son made perfect forever.

Setting and Structure

In 7:1–10 the author has established from Genesis 14 that Melchizedek is greater than Abraham and Levi. Now he returns to Psalm 110:4 and asks what it means that God, through David, announced a priest “according to the order of Melchizedek.” Melchizedek himself fades into the background (his name appears only in 7:11, 15, 17) and Jesus comes to the foreground: he is named at the climax of 7:22 and is the subject of every statement from 7:24 to 7:28.

The section is built almost entirely out of phrases from Psalm 110:4, each one examined in turn, like facets of a jewel turned in the light:

  • “according to the order of Melchizedek”—a different order from Aaron’s, which implies a change of priesthood and therefore of law (7:11–14);
  • “You are a priest forever”—a priesthood based not on descent but on an indestructible life (7:15–19);
  • “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind”—a priesthood confirmed by divine oath, unlike Aaron’s (7:20–22);
  • “forever” again—one permanent priest instead of a long succession of mortal ones (7:23–25).

The section then closes with a summary (7:26–28) that gathers up the whole chapter and leads into the next stage of the argument: the high priest we need is holy, exalted, and has offered himself once for all. The final verse sets out the contrast in its sharpest form: law versus oath, men versus Son, weakness versus perfection forever.

The section is also framed by the language of perfection. It opens with “if perfection (τελείωσις teleiōsis) had been through the Levitical priesthood” (7:11), states in the middle that “the law made nothing perfect (ἐτελείωσεν eteleiōsen)” (7:19), and ends with “a Son made perfect (τετελειωμένον teteleiōmenon) forever” (7:28). In Hebrews, “perfection” does not mean sinless moral flawlessness in the abstract. It means reaching the goal, being brought to completion. For worshipers, it means being brought to the goal of God’s purpose: cleansed in conscience and enabled to draw near to God in real and lasting fellowship (9:9; 10:1, 14). For Christ, it means being brought through suffering, obedience, death, and exaltation to the full completion of his vocation as Savior and High Priest (2:10; 5:8–10). The argument of 7:11–28 is that the old priesthood could not bring the people to that goal, but the new one does.

Two further themes are introduced here that will dominate the rest of the central section of Hebrews (8:1–10:18). One is “drawing near” to God (7:19, 25), the great privilege of worship that under the old covenant belonged in its fullest sense to the high priest alone, once a year (9:7), but which is now open to all who come through Jesus (10:19–22). The other is the “covenant” (διαθήκη diathēkē), named for the first time in 7:22. A change of priesthood means a change of law (7:12); a better priest means a better covenant (7:22). Chapter 8 will show from Jeremiah 31 that God himself promised this new covenant.

Historical Background: Priesthood, Law, and the Expectation of a Priestly Messiah

To a first-century Jew the Levitical priesthood was not a minor feature of religion; it was the beating heart of Israel’s life with God. The law of Moses was given together with the tabernacle and the priesthood (Exod 25–Lev 27). The priests maintained the sacrifices, taught the law (Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7), pronounced God’s blessing, and above all, through the high priest on the Day of Atonement, secured the cleansing of the people (Lev 16). The law called the Aaronic priesthood “a perpetual priesthood” (Exod 40:15; Num 25:13).

Some Jewish groups expected a future priestly figure alongside a royal one. The community of the Dead Sea Scrolls, for example, looked for “the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel” (1QS 9.11), a priestly Messiah from Aaron’s line and a royal Messiah from David’s. For them, as for all Jews of the time, any future priest would naturally come from Levi. That is what makes the argument of Hebrews 7 so radical: it claims, on the basis of Israel’s own Scriptures, that the decisive priest would come from Judah and would belong to a different order altogether. The author does not overturn the Scriptures; he shows that the Scriptures had already pointed beyond the priesthood of Aaron.

Verse by Verse

7:11

Now if perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood—for on the basis of it the people received the law—what further need would there have been for a different priest to arise according to the order of Melchizedek, and not to be named according to the order of Aaron?

The author now asks a question that lies at the heart of the chapter. The form is a “contrary-to-fact” condition: “If perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood (and it was not) … what further need would there have been (and there would have been none) …?” The words εἰ μὲν οὖν ei men oun, “now if, then,” mark a fresh stage of reasoning, drawing a conclusion from what Scripture has shown.

The logic is simple but devastating. Psalm 110 was written by David, centuries after Moses established the Levitical priesthood. If that priesthood had been capable of bringing the people to God’s goal, why would God, long after its establishment, announce through David a different priest of a different order? The very existence of the promise in Psalm 110:4 proves that the Levitical system was not God’s final word. The author will make the same kind of argument from Jeremiah 31 about the covenant (8:7, “if that first covenant had been faultless, there would have been no occasion sought for a second”) and from Psalm 95 about the rest (4:8, “if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day after these things”). In each case, a later word of God in Scripture reveals the provisional character of an earlier arrangement.

Word Study: τελείωσις teleiōsis

The noun teleiōsis, “perfection, completion, fulfillment,” is related to τέλος telos, “end, goal,” and to the verb τελειόω teleioō, “to make perfect, bring to completion.” In the Greek Old Testament the verb is used in the expression for the ordination of priests: literally “to fill the hands,” rendered “to perfect the hands” (e.g., Exod 29:9, Hebrew וּמִלֵּאתָ יַד־אַהֲרֹן umille’ta yad-’aharon, “and you shall fill the hand of Aaron,” that is, ordain him). The author may well have this priestly overtone in mind. But in Hebrews the main sense is “bringing to the goal.” The perfection the author has in view is the condition in which sinners are cleansed, forgiven, and brought into God’s presence to worship him with a clear conscience. The gifts and sacrifices of the old system “cannot perfect the worshiper with respect to the conscience” (9:9); the law “can never perfect those who draw near” (10:1); but “by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14).

The parenthesis—“for on the basis of it the people received the law”—is important. The verb νενομοθέτηται nenomothetētai means “has been given the law, has been legislated for.” The phrase “on the basis of it” (ἐπ’ αὐτῆς ep’ autēs) can mean “in connection with it” or “on the foundation of it.” The author’s point is that the law and the priesthood were bound together inseparably. Much of the law concerns the priests, sacrifices, purity, and the sanctuary; and the law as a whole assumes the priesthood as the means by which a sinful people can live in covenant with a holy God. The priesthood is not one detail of the law that might be changed while leaving the rest untouched. It is the foundation on which the law’s whole system of approach to God rests. That is why the next verse can say that changing the priesthood necessarily involves changing the law.

The author speaks of “a different priest” (ἕτερον … ἱερέα heteron … hierea). Greek has two words for “another”: allos, often “another of the same kind,” and heteros, often “another of a different kind.” The distinction is not rigid in New Testament Greek, but here the context makes the sense clear: this priest is of a different order, a different tribe, a different basis. He is not one more in the line of Aaron; he is “not … named according to the order of Aaron.”

The verb “arise” (ἀνίστασθαι anistasthai) is the ordinary word for a person appearing or coming on the scene (compare Deut 18:15, where God promises to “raise up” a prophet like Moses). The same verb is often used in the New Testament of resurrection, and Christian hearers may have caught an echo of Jesus’ rising from the dead, especially in light of 7:16, “the power of an indestructible life.” But the primary sense is the appearing of a new priest in history.

The “order” (τάξις taxis) of Melchizedek means his “kind,” “rank,” or “manner” of priesthood. The Hebrew of Psalm 110:4 reads עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק ‘al-divrati malki-tsedeq, “according to the manner of Melchizedek” or “after the pattern of Melchizedek.” The point is not that Melchizedek founded a line of successors (he had none) but that the new priest would be a priest of the same kind: a king-priest, appointed directly by God, not dependent on descent, and holding his office “forever.”

7:12

For when the priesthood is changed, of necessity a change of law also takes place.

This short verse states a principle that follows from the parenthesis of 7:11. If the law was given “on the basis of” the priesthood, then a change of priesthood carries with it “of necessity” (ἐξ ἀνάγκης ex anankēs) a change of law.

Two related words appear: the participle μετατιθεμένης metatithemenēs, “being changed,” and the noun μετάθεσις metathesis, “change.” Both come from a verb meaning “to transfer, to put in another place, to change.” The author used the verb in 11:5 of Enoch, who was “taken up” or “transferred” so as not to see death, and he will use the noun in 12:27 of the “removal” of the things that can be shaken. The word can mean either a transfer or a removal; here it signals that the priesthood is passing from one order to another—from Aaron to the order of Melchizedek—and that the legal framework built on the old priesthood must change with it.

What “law” is meant? The author’s focus throughout is the law as the system that regulated priesthood and sacrifice—what we might call the covenant arrangement of Sinai, with its priests, tabernacle, and offerings. He is not saying that God’s moral will has changed. The new covenant promise he will quote in chapter 8 says, “I will put my laws into their minds, and I will write them on their hearts” (8:10, quoting Jer 31:33). The righteousness that the law required is not abolished but internalized and fulfilled. What changes is the covenant structure: the way God’s people are constituted, cleansed, and brought near to him. That structure, founded on the Levitical priesthood, gives way to one founded on the priesthood of Christ.

For the first hearers this verse would have been sobering. It meant that they could not simply add faith in Jesus to the existing system of temple and priesthood, as if the two could stand side by side indefinitely. If Jesus is the priest of Psalm 110:4, the old order has been superseded. To go back to it is to go back to something God himself has replaced.

7:13

For the one about whom these things are said has belonged to a different tribe, from which no one has ever served at the altar.

The author now shows that the change of priesthood is not just theoretical: it has actually happened. “The one about whom these things are said”—that is, the one addressed in Psalm 110:4, whom the author has already identified as Jesus (5:5–6; 6:20)—“has belonged to a different tribe.”

The two verbs are both in the Greek perfect tense. μετέσχηκεν meteschēken, “has belonged, has had a share in,” is the same verb used in 2:14, where the Son “shared in” blood and flesh. The echo may be deliberate: by taking part in human nature, the Son took a place in a specific human family and tribe. προσέσχηκεν proseschēken, “has attended to, has served at,” describes officiating at the altar. No member of that tribe has ever done so.

The law was emphatic that only the sons of Aaron could minister at the altar: “You shall appoint Aaron and his sons, and they shall keep their priesthood; but the outsider who comes near shall be put to death” (Num 3:10; Hebrew וְהַזָּר הַקָּרֵב יוּמָת vehazzar haqqarev yumat, “and the outsider who comes near shall be put to death”; compare Num 18:7). The Levites who were not descendants of Aaron could assist, but they were forbidden to approach the altar itself (Num 18:3). A man from any other tribe had no place there at all. The tragic story of King Uzziah, a descendant of Judah who entered the temple to burn incense and was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21), showed how seriously this boundary was guarded.

So when the author says that the priest of Psalm 110 belongs to a tribe “from which no one has ever served at the altar,” he is pointing to a fact that, under the law, would disqualify Jesus from priesthood altogether. That is precisely his point. Jesus is not a priest under the law of Moses; he is a priest of another order, under a different arrangement (8:4 will say plainly, “if he were on earth, he would not be a priest at all”).

7:14

For it is evident that our Lord has arisen from Judah, a tribe concerning which Moses said nothing about priests.

The author names the tribe: Judah. He calls Jesus’ descent from Judah “evident” (πρόδηλον prodēlon), that is, “clear to all, well known, beyond dispute.” Jesus’ Davidic descent was a fixed element of early Christian preaching and tradition. Paul speaks of God’s Son “who came from the seed of David according to the flesh” (Rom 1:3); the Gospels trace his genealogy through David and Judah (Matt 1:1–16; Luke 3:23–33); Revelation calls him “the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David” (Rev 5:5). Eusebius preserves a report from the second-century writer Hegesippus that the grandsons of Jude, the Lord’s brother, were brought before the emperor Domitian as descendants of David (Eccl. Hist. 3.20). No one in the early church, friend or foe, seems to have doubted that Jesus belonged to the royal tribe.

That descent connects Jesus to the great promises of kingship. Jacob’s blessing on Judah is the foundational text:

Old Testament Text: Genesis 49:10

Hebrew: לֹא־יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה וּמְחֹקֵק מִבֵּין רַגְלָיו עַד כִּי־יָבֹא שילה וְלוֹ יִקְּהַת עַמִּים׃ — lo’-yasur shevet mihudah umechoqeq mibbein raglav ‘ad ki-yavo’ shiloh velo yiqqehat ‘ammim.

Translation: “The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler’s staff from between his feet, until he comes to whom it belongs [or: until Shiloh comes], and to him shall be the obedience of the peoples.”

The meaning of the word written shiloh has long been debated; many ancient Jewish and Christian interpreters understood it as “he to whom it belongs,” that is, the coming ruler. Either way, the verse promises enduring royal rule to Judah, culminating in a ruler to whom the nations will submit. In the promise to David (2 Sam 7:12–16) that rule was focused on David’s line, and Psalm 110 itself is a psalm of David about his “Lord” enthroned at God’s right hand. The priest of Psalm 110:4 is thus the royal Messiah from Judah—exactly the combination of king and priest that Melchizedek foreshadowed.

The verb “has arisen” (ἀνατέταλκεν anatetalken, from ἀνατέλλω anatellō) is unusual. It is used of the sun or a star rising and of a plant springing up. Both pictures were attached to messianic hope in the Old Testament. Balaam foresaw that “a star shall march forth from Jacob, and a scepter shall rise from Israel” (Num 24:17), and in the Greek version the verb for the star’s appearing is this same verb. Malachi promised that “the sun of righteousness shall rise” for those who fear God’s name (Mal 4:2 [Hebrew 3:20]). The prophets called the coming Davidic king “the Branch” (צֶמַח tsemach, “sprout, branch”; Jer 23:5; Zech 6:12), and the Greek Old Testament translated that title with the related noun ἀνατολή anatolē, “rising” or “sprouting.” Zechariah 6:12–13 is especially striking: “Behold, a man whose name is Branch … he shall build the temple of the Lord … and he shall sit and rule on his throne, and he shall be a priest on his throne.” The choice of verb thus quietly gathers up the prophetic hope of a royal—and priestly—Branch from David’s line.

“A tribe concerning which Moses said nothing about priests.” Here the author again argues from the silence of Scripture, as in 7:3, but now it is the silence of the law. Moses legislated in detail for the priesthood of Levi and Aaron. About priests from Judah he said nothing at all. Under the law of Moses, therefore, there could be no priest from Judah. If there is to be one, as Psalm 110 declares, then it must be on another basis than the law of Moses.

Notice the title: “our Lord” (ὁ κύριος ἡμῶν ho kyrios hēmōn). This is the only place in Hebrews where this exact phrase is used of Jesus without further words (13:20 has “our Lord Jesus”). It is the language of confession and devotion: the one from Judah is the Lord whom we worship and serve.

7:15

And it is still more abundantly evident if, according to the likeness of Melchizedek, a different priest arises,

The author piles up words for clarity: “still more abundantly evident” (περισσότερον ἔτι κατάδηλον perissoteron eti katadēlon). Katadēlos, “quite clear, very evident,” occurs only here in the New Testament and strengthens the prodēlos of 7:14. What is “still more evident”? Most naturally, the point stated in 7:11–12: that the Levitical priesthood could not bring perfection and that a change of priesthood and law has taken place. The fact that Jesus comes from Judah (7:14) showed it; the nature of his priesthood, described in 7:16, shows it even more clearly.

The word “if” (εἰ ei) here does not express doubt. It is a “real” condition, meaning “since” or “given that”: given that a different priest arises in the likeness of Melchizedek—as Psalm 110:4 says and as has happened in Jesus—the conclusion is even plainer.

“According to the likeness of Melchizedek” (κατὰ τὴν ὁμοιότητα Μελχισέδεκ kata tēn homoiotēta Melchisedek) restates the psalm’s “according to the order of Melchizedek” in a way that interprets it. The word homoiotēs, “likeness,” picks up the participle “made like” (aphōmoiōmenos) of 7:3. The author thereby tells us what “order” means: not a line of succession, but a resemblance in kind. What, specifically, is the likeness? The next verse answers: a priesthood resting not on descent but on life.

Once again he speaks of “a different priest” (ἱερεὺς ἕτερος hiereus heteros), echoing 7:11, and again he uses the verb “arises” (ἀνίσταται anistatai), now in the present tense, as a vivid description of what Scripture foretold and what has now come to pass.

7:16

who has become a priest not according to the law of a fleshly commandment but according to the power of an indestructible life.

This verse states the heart of the difference between the two priesthoods, in two balanced phrases introduced by “not according to … but according to” (οὐ κατὰ … ἀλλὰ κατά ou kata … alla kata).

“Not according to the law of a fleshly commandment.” The Levitical priests became priests because of a legal requirement (“law,” “commandment”) that concerned the “flesh.” The adjective σαρκίνης sarkinēs, “fleshly, made of flesh, pertaining to the flesh,” does not here mean “sinful,” as “flesh” sometimes does in Paul. It means that the commandment dealt with physical, bodily matters: above all, physical descent from Aaron. It also governed bodily qualifications: no descendant of Aaron who had a physical defect could approach to offer the food of his God (Lev 21:17–23; Hebrew 21:17, אִישׁ מִזַּרְעֲךָ לְדֹרֹתָם אֲשֶׁר יִהְיֶה בוֹ מוּם לֹא יִקְרַב ’ish mizzar‘aka ledorotam ’asher yihyeh vo mum lo’ yiqrav, “any man of your offspring throughout their generations who has a defect shall not draw near”). The basis of the old priesthood was external, genealogical, and physical. It could guarantee a man’s ancestry and the soundness of his body; it could not give him life, and it could not keep him from dying.

“But according to the power of an indestructible life.” The word ἀκατάλυτος akatalytos occurs only here in the New Testament. It is formed from the negative a- and the verb καταλύω katalyō, “to destroy, tear down, dissolve.” That verb is used of destroying a building (Mark 14:58, the charge that Jesus would “destroy this temple”), and Paul uses it of the dissolving of the body in death: “if our earthly house, this tent, is destroyed (katalythē)” (2 Cor 5:1). An “indestructible” life is a life that cannot be dissolved, broken down, or brought to an end—a life over which death has no final power.

When did Jesus become a priest according to this power? The verb is “has become” (γέγονεν gegonen), and in Hebrews the moment of Jesus’ installation as high priest is closely connected with his resurrection and exaltation. He was “designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek” after he had been “made perfect” through suffering (5:9–10); he entered “behind the curtain … having become a high priest forever” (6:20). The life by which he serves is the life that broke through death. Jesus did die—truly and fully, as Hebrews insists (2:9, 14; 9:15; 13:12). But his life was not destroyed by death. He was raised and exalted, and now “death no longer has dominion over him” (Rom 6:9). Peter declared that “it was not possible for him to be held” by death (Acts 2:24), quoting Psalm 16, “You will not abandon my soul to Sheol, nor let your Holy One see corruption” (Ps 16:10).

Some interpreters relate “indestructible life” mainly to the Son’s eternal divine nature (compare 7:3, “neither beginning of days nor end of life”). Others relate it mainly to the resurrection life of the incarnate Son. The two belong together. It is the eternal Son who became man; it is because he is who he is that death could not hold him; and it is in his risen, glorified humanity that he now serves as priest. The contrast with “fleshly commandment” suggests that the author is thinking of the whole basis of Jesus’ priesthood: not ancestry, but the indestructible life of the eternal Son, proved and manifested in his resurrection.

The point for the hearers is enormous. The old priests were made priests by a regulation about their birth, and their priesthood ended at their death. Jesus is made priest by the power of a life that death could not end. His priesthood therefore never ends. That is what makes the “forever” of Psalm 110:4 true of him as of no one else.

7:17

For it is testified: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.”

The author supports 7:16 by quoting Psalm 110:4 in full (as in 5:6). He introduces it with “it is testified” (μαρτυρεῖται martyreitai), the same verb he used of Melchizedek in 7:8. Scripture is a witness, and its testimony settles the matter. The present tense matters too: the testimony is not merely something God said once; it is something Scripture still says.

The emphasis now falls on the word “forever” (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna). An everlasting priesthood requires a priest whose life is everlasting. “Forever” in Psalm 110:4 is the scriptural proof that the priest of whom it speaks must possess “the power of an indestructible life.” No descendant of Aaron could fulfill those words, since every one of them died.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 110:4

Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק׃ — nishba‘ YHWH velo’ yinnachem ’attah-kohen le‘olam ‘al-divrati malki-tsedeq.

Translation: “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: ‘You are a priest forever according to the manner of Melchizedek.’”

The Hebrew לְעוֹלָם le‘olam means “forever, for all time, perpetually.” The phrase עַל־דִּבְרָתִי ‘al-divrati is rare; it is built on the word divrah, “matter, cause, manner,” with an old connecting vowel, and means “according to the manner of,” “after the pattern of.” (Some modern scholars have proposed other readings, such as “for my sake, O righteous king,” but the traditional understanding, reflected in the Greek “according to the order of Melchizedek,” fits the grammar well and was the reading of the Jewish translators of the Septuagint.) The Greek translation that Hebrews quotes, κατὰ τὴν τάξιν Μελχισέδεκ kata tēn taxin Melchisedek, accurately conveys the Hebrew.

A reader familiar with the law might object that the law also calls Aaron’s priesthood “perpetual” (Exod 40:15, לִכְהֻנַּת עוֹלָם likhunnat ‘olam, “for a perpetual priesthood”; compare Num 25:13). But the two are not alike. The Aaronic priesthood was “perpetual” as an institution passed from father to son “throughout their generations” (Exod 40:15); each individual priest died and was replaced. Psalm 110:4, by contrast, addresses a single person—“You are a priest forever”—and confirms it with an oath. The author’s argument follows precisely the wording of the psalm.

7:18

For on the one hand, there is a setting aside of the former commandment because of its weakness and uselessness

Verses 18–19 form a single balanced sentence, built on the Greek pair μέν … δέ men … de, “on the one hand … on the other hand.” On one side stands the “setting aside” of the former commandment; on the other, the “bringing in” of a better hope. The author draws the consequence of Psalm 110:4: something old has been removed, something better has been introduced.

Word Study: ἀθέτησις athetēsis

The noun athetēsis comes from the verb ἀθετέω atheteō, “to set aside, declare invalid, nullify.” In the legal documents of the Greek-speaking world (preserved on papyri from Egypt) the noun was a technical term for the annulment or cancellation of a decree, contract, or legal provision. The author uses the word only twice: here, of the setting aside of the commandment about priesthood, and in 9:26, where Christ has appeared “for the setting aside (athetēsin) of sin by the sacrifice of himself.” The verb appears in 10:28 of someone who “has set aside the law of Moses.” The word is strong. The former commandment has not merely been supplemented; it has been legally annulled as the basis of priesthood.

“The former commandment” (προαγούσης ἐντολῆς proagousēs entolēs, literally “the preceding commandment”) is the one mentioned in 7:5 and 7:16, the law’s commandment establishing the Levitical priesthood with its regulations of descent and service. It “preceded” in time the oath of Psalm 110, and now gives way to it.

Why was it set aside? “Because of its weakness and uselessness” (διὰ τὸ αὐτῆς ἀσθενὲς καὶ ἀνωφελές dia to autēs asthenes kai anōpheles). The two adjectives, used as nouns, are strong. Asthenēs means “weak, powerless, without strength”; anōphelēs means “unprofitable, useless, without benefit.” These words must be read carefully. The author does not say that the law was evil or that God’s commandment was a mistake. Paul, who speaks just as strongly about the law’s inability, insists that “the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good” (Rom 7:12). The weakness lies in what the commandment could not accomplish. It could appoint priests, but it could not give them life or holiness (7:23, 28). It could command sacrifices, but those sacrifices could not cleanse the conscience (9:9; 10:1–4). Paul makes a closely similar point: God did “what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh” (Rom 8:3). The law was “useless” not for every purpose—it taught, it convicted of sin, it foreshadowed Christ (10:1)—but for the one purpose the author has in view: bringing people to perfection, to real and lasting access to God.

Calvin’s observation is helpful here: the author is speaking of the priestly and ceremonial ordinances of the law insofar as they are viewed apart from Christ, as if they could achieve salvation in themselves. In that sense they are weak and unprofitable. But as pointers to Christ they had real value, and the author of Hebrews uses them extensively for exactly that purpose.

7:19

(for the law made nothing perfect); on the other hand, there is the bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God.

The first clause is a parenthesis explaining the “weakness and uselessness” of 7:18: “for the law made nothing perfect” (οὐδὲν γὰρ ἐτελείωσεν ὁ νόμος ouden gar eteleiōsen ho nomos). “Nothing” is placed first for emphasis. The law brought nothing—no person, no aspect of the relationship with God—to its intended goal. This is the answer to the question of 7:11. Perfection did not come through the Levitical priesthood, nor through the law that was founded upon it.

Then the second half of the men … de sentence: “on the other hand, there is the bringing in of a better hope.” The noun ἐπεισαγωγή epeisagōgē means “introduction, bringing in,” perhaps “bringing in in addition” or “bringing in in place of” something else. It is the positive counterpart to the athetēsis, the setting aside, of 7:18. Something has been removed; something better has been brought in.

What has been brought in is “a better hope” (κρείττονος ἐλπίδος kreittonos elpidos). In Hebrews “hope” is not a vague wish but a confident expectation grounded in God’s promise and oath. The author has just spoken of “the hope set before us,” which we hold “as an anchor of the soul, sure and firm, and which enters into the inner place behind the curtain, where Jesus has entered as a forerunner on our behalf” (6:18–20). That hope is “better” because it rests on a better priest, a better covenant, and better promises (8:6). The old order offered the hope of continual sacrifices and annual atonement; the new offers the hope of full cleansing and entrance into the very presence of God.

This hope is the one “through which we draw near to God” (δι’ ἧς ἐγγίζομεν τῷ θεῷ di’ hēs engizomen tō theō). The verb “draw near” is priestly language. In the Old Testament, to “draw near” to God was the special privilege—and danger—of the priests. When Nadab and Abihu offered unauthorized fire and were consumed, Moses said: “This is what the Lord spoke, saying, ‘Among those who draw near to me I will show myself holy, and before all the people I will be glorified’” (Lev 10:3; Hebrew בִּקְרֹבַי אֶקָּדֵשׁ biqrovai ’eqqadesh, “among those near to me I will be sanctified”). Ordinary Israelites stayed at a distance; only the priests entered the holy place; only the high priest, once a year, entered the Most Holy Place (9:7).

Now, says the author, “we draw near to God.” The pronoun is inclusive: all believers, not a priestly caste, enjoy this privilege through the hope that rests on Jesus. That is what “perfection” means in practice. The new priesthood accomplishes what the old could not: it brings God’s people into his presence. The same truth will become the great exhortation of 10:19–22: “Since we have boldness to enter the sanctuary by the blood of Jesus … let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith.” James echoes the same invitation: “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you” (Jas 4:8).
