10:11

And every priest stands day by day ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never strip away sins.

The author now paints a picture, and it is one of the most memorable in the letter. He sets two scenes side by side, linked by the Greek particles μὲν men … δέ de (“on the one hand … on the other”). On one side stands “every priest” (πᾶς … ἱερεὺς pas … hiereus). On the other, in verse 12, sits “this one.”

Notice the shift from the yearly sacrifice of the high priest (10:1–4) to the daily work of “every priest.” The author widens the lens to take in the whole priesthood and the whole round of sacrifices, not just the Day of Atonement. Every day, morning and evening, priests offered the continual burnt offering, and through the day they handled the sin offerings, guilt offerings, and other sacrifices brought by worshipers. The old order never stopped.

Historical Background: The Daily Service and the Standing Priests

The law commanded a “continual” or “regular” burnt offering, the עֹלַת תָּמִיד olat tamid: “two year-old lambs each day, continually; one lamb you shall offer in the morning, and the second lamb you shall offer at twilight” (Exod 29:38–42; Num 28:3–8). This daily sacrifice, called simply the Tamid (“the continual”), was the backbone of temple worship. The Mishnah has an entire tractate, Tamid, describing how it was performed in the last generations of the Second Temple. The priests on duty rose before dawn and cast lots for the day’s tasks: who would clear the ashes from the altar, who would slaughter the lamb, who would sprinkle the blood, who would offer incense (compare m. Yoma 2:1–4). Luke records that it was on such an occasion that Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, “was chosen by lot to enter the sanctuary of the Lord and burn incense” (Luke 1:9). The service was carried out by priests organized into twenty-four divisions, each serving in rotation (1 Chr 24:1–19; Luke 1:5, 8). In AD 70, during the Roman siege, the daily offering ceased for lack of men to offer it, as Josephus notes (J.W. 6.94).

Throughout all of this, the priests stood. The furniture God prescribed for the tabernacle included the ark, the table, the lampstand, the altar of incense, the bronze altar, and the basin (Exod 25–30), but no chair. The priest stood “to minister” before the Lord (Deut 10:8; 18:7). A later rabbinic tradition recorded in the Babylonian Talmud states that no one was permitted to sit in the temple court except the kings of David’s house (b. Yoma 25a). Even on the Day of Atonement, the Mishnah says that the high priest did not linger long in prayer in the sanctuary, so that the people would not be alarmed (m. Yoma 5:1). Whatever the precise customs, the picture was clear to anyone who knew the temple. A priest at work in God’s house was on his feet.

The verb “stands” (ἕστηκεν hestēken) is in the perfect tense. With this verb the perfect has present meaning, “stands, is standing,” but it also conveys a fixed posture: the priest has taken his stand and remains standing. Three words pile up to emphasize repetition. The priest stands “day by day” (καθ’ ἡμέραν kath’ hēmeran), “ministering” (λειτουργῶν leitourgōn, performing priestly service), and “offering repeatedly” (πολλάκις προσφέρων pollakis prospherōn, literally “often offering”) “the same sacrifices” (τὰς αὐτὰς … θυσίας tas autas … thysias). The tone is almost weary. It is the same work, again and again, never done.

Then comes the verdict: these are sacrifices “which can never strip away sins” (αἵτινες οὐδέποτε δύνανται περιελεῖν ἁμαρτίας haitines oudepote dynantai perielein hamartias). The verb περιαιρέω periaireō means to take something off from around, to strip away. It is used of casting off anchors (Acts 27:40) and of a veil being removed (2 Cor 3:16). Sin is pictured as something that clings around a person and must be peeled away. The old sacrifices could not do it. “Never” (οὐδέποτε oudepote) repeats the word of 10:1. However often they were offered, the result did not change.

There is something poignant here. These priests were not wicked. Many were devout men who served faithfully. But their posture was itself a sermon. A priest who is always standing has a job that is never finished. Every lamb they offered declared that another one would be needed tomorrow.

10:12

But this one, having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, sat down at the right hand of God,

“But this one” (οὗτος δὲ houtos de): the contrast is pointed and personal. Against “every priest” stands this one Priest. Against “the same sacrifices” offered “repeatedly” stands “one sacrifice” (μίαν … θυσίαν mian … thysian), and the word “one” is placed first for emphasis. Against priests who stand, this one “sat down.”

The participle “having offered” (προσενέγκας prosenenkas) is in the aorist tense, which here presents the offering as a single completed event that preceded his sitting down. His sacrifice was “for sins” (ὑπὲρ ἁμαρτιῶν hyper hamartiōn), the same phrase used of the high priest’s duty in 5:1. What every high priest was appointed to do, Christ did once, and did completely.

Then comes the central image: he “sat down at the right hand of God” (ἐκάθισεν ἐν δεξιᾷ τοῦ θεοῦ ekathisen en dexia tou theou). This is the language of Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament text that more than any other shapes the Christology of Hebrews. The author echoed it in his opening sentence (“having made purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high,” 1:3), quoted it in 1:13, used it to state his “main point” in 8:1, and will use it again in 12:2. The psalm’s fourth verse (“You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek”) has been the foundation of chapters 5–7. Here the two verses of the psalm meet: the priest after the order of Melchizedek is also the king enthroned at God’s right hand.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 110:1

Hebrew: נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי שֵׁב לִימִינִי עַד־אָשִׁית אֹיְבֶיךָ הֲדֹם לְרַגְלֶיךָ׃ — ne’um YHWH la’doni: shev limini, ad-ashit oyeveykha hadom leragleykha, “The declaration of the Lord to my lord: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.’”

The psalm begins with a solemn formula usually reserved for prophetic oracles, ne’um YHWH, “the declaration of the Lord.” The command is shev, “sit!”, and the place is limini, “at my right hand,” the place of highest honor and shared authority beside a king. Jesus himself used this psalm to show that the Messiah is David’s Lord as well as his son (Mark 12:35–37), and he applied it to himself before the high priest at his trial (Mark 14:62). The Greek of Hebrews follows the Septuagint faithfully here, and there is no difference in meaning from the Hebrew.

Why does sitting matter so much? Because in the old order the posture of the priest showed that his work was unfinished, while Christ’s sitting shows that his offering is complete. Westcott and many after him have drawn attention to this contrast between the standing priests and the seated Christ. His session is the sign of a finished sacrifice. It is also the sign of royal authority, since he is not seated on a bench in a sanctuary courtyard but at God’s right hand. And it speaks of rest after accomplished labor, echoing the God who rested from his works on the seventh day (4:4, 10). As Jesus cried on the cross, “It is finished” (John 19:30).

Interpretive Options: Where Does “For All Time” Belong?

The phrase εἰς τὸ διηνεκὲς eis to diēnekes (“for all time, perpetually”) stands in the middle of the sentence between the offering and the sitting, and could go with either.

  • With the offering: “having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, he sat down.” The one sacrifice has permanent effect. This is the reading of most modern translations and of many commentators.
  • With the sitting: “having offered one sacrifice for sins, he sat down for all time.” Christ has sat down permanently and will never again rise to offer another sacrifice.

Two observations favor the first reading. In verse 14 the same phrase plainly qualifies the effect of the offering (“he has perfected for all time”), and the author is building an argument about the sufficiency of the sacrifice. Furthermore, verse 13 speaks of Christ “waiting” only “until” his enemies are subdued, and Hebrews expects him to “appear a second time” (9:28), so “sat down for all time” would sit a little awkwardly. Still, both readings say something true. Because the one sacrifice is sufficient for all time, the Priest never needs to rise to offer again.

10:13

from then on waiting until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet.

The author completes the quotation of Psalm 110:1. Christ is seated, and “from then on” (τὸ λοιπὸν to loipon, “for the rest [of the time], henceforth”) he is “waiting” (ἐκδεχόμενος ekdechomenos) “until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet” (ἕως τεθῶσιν οἱ ἐχθροὶ αὐτοῦ ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν αὐτοῦ heōs tethōsin hoi echthroi autou hypopodion tōn podōn autou).

The verb “are made” (literally “are placed,” τεθῶσιν tethōsin) is passive, and in the psalm the one who does the placing is God: “until I make your enemies your footstool.” The Son’s waiting is not anxious uncertainty. It is the confident expectation of one who knows the Father’s promise will be kept. His work of atonement is complete; the full display of his victory is still to come. This is the “already and not yet” of the New Testament. Hebrews put it plainly at 2:8–9: “At present we do not yet see all things subjected to him, but we see Jesus … crowned with glory and honor.” Paul expressed the same pattern from the same psalm: “he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy to be destroyed is death” (1 Cor 15:25–26).

The footstool image would have been vivid in the ancient world. Kings sometimes had their footstools decorated with images of conquered enemies, so that they literally rested their feet on their foes; examples survive from the royal tombs of Egypt. In Israel’s own history, Joshua had his commanders put their feet on the necks of the defeated Canaanite kings as a sign of total victory (Josh 10:24). And the Lord himself says, “Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool” (Isa 66:1, הֲדֹם רַגְלָי hadom raglay). For Christ’s enemies to become his footstool means their complete and final subjugation.

Who are these enemies? Hebrews has named the devil, “who has the power of death,” whom Christ destroyed through death (2:14). Sin and death, the powers that held humanity in slavery, have been defeated at the cross, but their final removal awaits Christ’s return. The enemies also include human beings who persist in opposing God. The warning that follows in this very chapter speaks of “a fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27). The word for “expectation” there (ἐκδοχὴ ekdochē) comes from the same root as Christ’s “waiting” here. Christ waits for the full manifestation of his victory; his enemies wait for judgment. That sobering parallel prepares for the warning of 10:26–31. The readers must be sure they are not found among the enemies when the footstool is set in place.

This waiting is not idleness. Hebrews has already told us that the seated Christ “always lives to make intercession” for those who draw near to God through him (7:25), and that he appears “in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24). His priestly work of offering is finished; his priestly work of intercession and help continues (2:18; 4:15–16). He sits as one whose sacrifice needs no addition, and he is active as one whose people still need his care.

10:14

For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.

This verse is the summit of the whole central section of Hebrews. The word “for” (γὰρ gar) explains why Christ could sit down: there was nothing left to do, because by a single offering he had accomplished everything the offering was meant to accomplish. Each phrase deserves attention.

“By a single offering” (μιᾷ … προσφορᾷ mia … prosphora). Again the word “one” comes first in the sentence. The noun προσφορά prosphora, “offering,” is the same word used of “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ” in 10:10. The single offering is his self-offering in death, which he presented to God in the true sanctuary (9:11–14, 24–26).

“He has perfected” (τετελείωκεν teteleiōken). This is the perfect tense of τελειόω teleioō. The Greek perfect presents an action as complete, with a result that continues into the present. Christ’s offering is past, but its perfecting effect remains in force now. The verb takes us back to 10:1, where the law “can never make perfect those who draw near.” What the law could never do, Christ has done. In Hebrews, to be “made perfect” in this sense is to be brought to the goal of worship: cleansed in conscience from the guilt of sin (9:9, 14; 10:2), qualified to approach God (10:19–22), and placed in a right covenant relationship with him in which sins are no longer remembered (10:17). Bruce and others rightly point out that this “perfection” is not sinless moral maturity. It is the perfection of the worshiper’s standing before God. The worshiper is fully and finally fitted for God’s presence.

“For all time” (εἰς τὸ διηνεκὲς eis to diēnekes). This is the third occurrence of the phrase in this section. The priests of the old covenant offered “perpetually” and could never perfect (10:1). Christ’s single offering perfects “perpetually,” without interruption and without end. Its effect is never exhausted and never needs renewing.

“Those who are being sanctified” (τοὺς ἁγιαζομένους tous hagiazomenous). Here the grammar becomes especially important. In 10:10 the author used the perfect tense: “we have been sanctified” (ἡγιασμένοι hēgiasmenoi). Here he uses a present participle, which normally describes ongoing action: “those who are being sanctified.” How do these fit together? And how can people who “are being sanctified” already have been “perfected for all time”?

Interpretive Options: The Perfect and the Present

  • A progressive process. Some read the present participle as describing believers’ ongoing growth in holiness. On this view, the verse holds together a completed status (“perfected”) and a continuing process (“being sanctified”): those whom Christ has once for all perfected are now in the process of becoming in practice what they already are in standing.
  • A class of people over time. Others take the present participle as describing the people who receive sanctification, one after another, throughout the ages. On this view, “those who are being sanctified” means “those who come to be sanctified,” that is, all who, in each generation, are brought by faith into the benefit of Christ’s offering. The present tense covers the whole time from the cross to the end, during which people are being added to the sanctified community.
  • A timeless description. Still others regard the present participle as simply a way of naming a group by its characteristic: “the sanctified ones,” much as one might say “the believers.” The same present participle appears in 2:11: “he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified (οἱ ἁγιαζόμενοι hoi hagiazomenoi) are all of one origin.”

The context weighs most heavily on the side of the second and third views. Throughout Hebrews, “sanctify” refers chiefly to the decisive consecration Christ achieves by his blood (2:11; 9:13–14; 10:10, 29; 13:12), and the author’s point in this verse is the sufficiency of the one offering, not the believer’s growth. The present participle presents those who benefit from it as a continuing company, extending through time, each of whom receives the full and final effect of Christ’s single offering. Yet the first view also contains real truth that Hebrews teaches elsewhere. The Father disciplines his children “that we may share his holiness” (12:10), and believers are told to “pursue … holiness, without which no one will see the Lord” (12:14). The author therefore holds together what the Christian life holds together: a consecration that is complete and a holiness that is being worked out.

The relationship between the perfect tense of 10:10 and the present tense of 10:14 can be put simply. The perfect tense (“we have been sanctified”) looks at the believer’s position from the standpoint of what Christ has accomplished. It is done, and done for good. The present tense (“those who are being sanctified”) looks at believers as a people still living in history, still being gathered, and still walking toward the city to come (13:14). What unites them is the single offering. Every one of “those who are being sanctified” has already been “perfected for all time.” The perfecting is not a reward for completing the process of sanctification. It is the ground on which that process rests. The order matters: God does not wait until we are holy to accept us; he accepts us in Christ and therefore makes us holy.

This is perhaps the most important verse in Hebrews for the assurance of the believer. John Owen and Calvin both drew from it the conclusion that the conscience of the believer may rest entirely on Christ’s single offering, since nothing can be added to what he has perfected. Nothing in Christ’s work is left for us to complete. This is not an invitation to carelessness. The same author warns that the one who goes on sinning deliberately has no other sacrifice to turn to (10:26). But it is an invitation to rest, to draw near, and to serve God with a cleansed conscience. Christ sat down because his offering perfected his people. His people may stop striving to make themselves acceptable and give themselves instead to the pursuit of the holiness that his offering has made possible.

10:15

And the Holy Spirit also bears witness to us; for after saying,

The author now brings his final witness: “the Holy Spirit also bears witness to us” (μαρτυρεῖ δὲ ἡμῖν καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον martyrei de hēmin kai to pneuma to hagion). Under the law, a matter was to be established by two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15; cf. Heb 10:28). The author has already brought the testimony of Psalm 40 and of Psalm 110. Now the Spirit testifies through Jeremiah, completing the case.

Hebrews consistently presents the Scriptures as the living voice of God. God speaks in the psalms (1:5–13); the Son speaks in the psalms and prophets (2:12–13; 10:5); and the Holy Spirit speaks in them too. The author introduced Psalm 95 with “as the Holy Spirit says” (3:7) and explained the arrangement of the tabernacle as “the Holy Spirit indicating this” (9:8). The verb here is present tense, “bears witness.” The Spirit who spoke through Jeremiah six centuries earlier speaks now, in the reading of Scripture, to “us,” the author and his hearers. This is how Hebrews teaches us to read the Bible: not as an ancient record only, but as God addressing his people today (“Today, if you hear his voice,” 3:7, 15; 4:7).

The second half of the verse opens a sentence that runs into verse 17: “for after saying …” (μετὰ γὰρ τὸ εἰρηκέναι meta gar to eirēkenai). The perfect infinitive “having said” treats the first part of the quotation as already spoken and still standing. The sentence leaves the reader waiting for its completion, which is discussed under verse 17.

10:16

“This is the covenant that I will make with them after those days, says the Lord: putting my laws on their hearts, I will also write them on their minds,”

The author quotes Jeremiah 31:33 (Greek Jeremiah 38:33), which he first cited at 8:10 as part of the long quotation of Jeremiah 31:31–34. Here he gives only the verses he needs, and shapes them slightly.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:33–34

Hebrew 31:33: כִּי זֹאת הַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר אֶכְרֹת אֶת־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל אַחֲרֵי הַיָּמִים הָהֵם נְאֻם־יְהוָה נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם וְעַל־לִבָּם אֶכְתֲּבֶנָּה וְהָיִיתִי לָהֶם לֵאלֹהִים וְהֵמָּה יִהְיוּ־לִי לְעָם׃ — ki zot habberit asher ekhrot et-beit yisra’el acharei hayyamim hahem, ne’um-YHWH: natatti et-torati beqirbam we‘al-libbam ekhtavennah, wehayiti lahem lelohim wehemmah yihyu-li le‘am, “For this is the covenant that I will cut with the house of Israel after those days, declares the Lord: I will put my law within them, and on their heart I will write it; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.”

Hebrew 31:34 (end): כִּי אֶסְלַח לַעֲוֺנָם וּלְחַטָּאתָם לֹא אֶזְכָּר־עוֹד׃ — ki eslach la‘avonam ulechattatam lo ezkor-od, “for I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more.”

How Hebrews abbreviates the text. Comparing this with 8:10–12 shows the author’s purpose. (1) He replaces “with the house of Israel” with “with them,” which makes the promise apply more directly to the people of the new covenant he is addressing. (2) He puts “hearts” before “minds,” reversing the order of 8:10. This brings the wording closer to the Hebrew, which speaks of the law placed “within them” and written “on their heart.” (3) He omits the promise “I will be their God and they shall be my people,” and the promise that none will need to teach his neighbor, “Know the Lord” (31:34a). (4) In verse 17 he adds “and their lawless deeds” to “their sins,” and omits the line “I will be merciful to their iniquities” (8:12). The effect is to bring two things into sharp focus: the inward renewal of the heart and the complete forgiveness of sins. These are precisely the two blessings that the old sacrifices could not deliver.

Note the Hebrew idiom “cut a covenant” (אֶכְרֹת ekhrot, “I will cut”), which probably comes from the ancient practice of ratifying covenants with the cutting of sacrificial animals (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18). The Greek renders this with διαθήσομαι diathēsomai, “I will make, I will arrange.” The new covenant, too, was ratified by a sacrifice: not by divided animals, but by the offering of the body of Jesus Christ and the shedding of his blood (9:15–18; Luke 22:20).

The first promise is inward renewal: “putting my laws on their hearts, I will also write them on their minds.” Under the old covenant, God’s law was written on tablets of stone (Exod 31:18; 2 Cor 3:3) and kept in the ark. In the new covenant it is written on the heart. The law is not abolished; it is internalized. This is the work of the Spirit, as Ezekiel’s parallel promise makes clear: “I will put my Spirit within you and cause you to walk in my statutes” (Ezek 36:27). The same Spirit who “bears witness” through Jeremiah (10:15) is the one who writes God’s law on believing hearts. And, as noted at 10:7, what is promised here to the people was first true of their Head, who could say, “your law is within my inmost being” (Ps 40:8).

Why does the author include this half of the promise when his main concern is forgiveness? Because the two belong together. Hebrews never separates forgiveness from transformation. The sacrifice that perfects the conscience (9:14) also frees it “to serve the living God.” Those whom Christ has perfected are the same people in whom God is writing his will. This reminds the readers that the new covenant is not merely a new legal arrangement but a new heart.

10:17

he then adds, “And their sins and their lawless deeds I will by no means remember any more.”

Here the sentence that began in verse 15 reaches its goal. The Greek has no explicit verb for “he adds”; the sentence is elliptical. Having said (10:15–16) the first part, the Spirit then says this second part, and the second part is what the author wants to emphasize.

Interpretive Options: Where Does the Second Half of the Sentence Begin?

  • At “says the Lord” in verse 16. Some read: “after saying, ‘This is the covenant that I will make with them after those days,’ the Lord says, ‘I will put my laws on their hearts … and their sins … I will remember no more.’” The words “says the Lord” would then be the author’s own introduction to the second half.
  • At the beginning of verse 17. Most commentators and translations understand an implied “then he says” or “he adds” before verse 17: “after saying [all of verse 16], he then says, ‘And their sins … I will remember no more.’”

The second view is more natural. “Says the Lord” is part of the Jeremiah text itself (Hebrew נְאֻם־יְהוָה ne’um-YHWH), and verse 18 shows that the author’s emphasis falls on the promise of forgiveness, which this view highlights. On either reading, the meaning is the same: the climax of the Spirit’s testimony is God’s promise to remember sins no more.

The promise is emphatic in Greek. “I will by no means remember” translates οὐ μὴ μνησθήσομαι ou mē mnēsthēsomai. The double negative ou mē is the strongest way of denying a future possibility in Greek: “I will certainly not, under any circumstances, remember.” The adverb “any more” (ἔτι eti) makes the promise permanent.

The author adds “their lawless deeds” (τῶν ἀνομιῶν tōn anomiōn, from ἀνομία anomia, “lawlessness”) to “their sins.” The addition widens the promise. Not only sins in general, but deeds done in open defiance of God’s law, are included. Nothing is too great for the forgiveness of the new covenant to cover. It may also be that the author chose “lawless deeds” to stand alongside “my laws” in the previous verse: the same people whose acts violated God’s law will now have that law written on their hearts.

What does it mean for God not to “remember” sins? In Hebrew thought, “remembering” is not just recalling a fact; it means acting on it. When God “remembered” Noah (Gen 8:1) or his covenant (Exod 2:24), he acted to save. When he “remembers” sin, he acts to punish it (Hos 8:13; 9:9). For God to say “their sin I will remember no more” (וּלְחַטָּאתָם לֹא אֶזְכָּר־עוֹד ulechattatam lo ezkor-od) does not mean that the all-knowing God suffers from a lapse of memory. It means he will never again bring those sins up against his people, never again treat them as grounds for judgment. The contrast with 10:3 is deliberate and beautiful. In the old sacrifices there was a “reminder of sins” year after year. In the new covenant, God himself promises to remember them no more. The yearly reminding is replaced by the final forgetting.

10:18

Now where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer an offering for sin.

With this short, decisive sentence the author ends the entire central exposition that began at 8:1 (and that was being prepared for from 4:14 onward). The conclusion follows logically from the Spirit’s testimony. Jeremiah promised a covenant in which God would “forgive” (אֶסְלַח eslach, “I will pardon”) iniquity and remember sin no more. That covenant has now been inaugurated by the blood of Christ (9:15–18). Therefore, the forgiveness it promises is now a reality, and once forgiveness is real, there is nothing left for a sin offering to do.

Word Study: ἄφεσις aphesis, “forgiveness, release”

The noun aphesis comes from the verb ἀφίημι aphiēmi, “to let go, release, send away.” In the Greek Old Testament it is used for the “release” of the Jubilee year, when debts were canceled and slaves set free (Lev 25:10), and for the “release” of the seventh year (Deut 15:1–2). In the New Testament it becomes the standard word for forgiveness of sins (Matt 26:28; Luke 24:47; Acts 2:38; Eph 1:7). Jesus used it in his Nazareth sermon, quoting Isaiah: he was sent “to proclaim release to the captives” (Luke 4:18). The word thus carries the sense of being set free from a debt that could never be paid. In Hebrews it appears only twice: here, and at 9:22, “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.” The two verses together sum up the argument. Forgiveness requires the shedding of blood (9:22); Christ’s blood has been shed once for all; therefore forgiveness has been granted, and no further offering is needed (10:18).

“Of these” (τούτων toutōn) refers to the “sins and lawless deeds” of verse 17. Where these have been forgiven, “there is no longer an offering for sin” (οὐκέτι προσφορὰ περὶ ἁμαρτίας ouketi prosphora peri hamartias). The phrase “for sin” (peri hamartias) is the same expression used for the “sin offerings” of Psalm 40 in 10:6 and 10:8. The very category of sacrifice that dealt with sin is now closed. Its purpose has been fulfilled. “No longer” (οὐκέτι ouketi) marks the passing of an era.

This verse has two sides, and the author will draw out both. On one side is boundless comfort. Believers need no further sacrifice, no ritual to supplement Christ’s offering, and no anxious repetition of rites to secure their standing. The way into the sanctuary is open, and they may enter with boldness (10:19). On the other side is solemn warning. If Christ’s sacrifice is the only and final one, then there is nowhere else to go for anyone who rejects it. Shortly the author will write, in words that echo this verse, that for the one who deliberately keeps on sinning after receiving the knowledge of the truth, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins” (10:26). The temple’s altar, still smoking in Jerusalem, could not help such a person, because God himself had taken that system away (10:9). The only sacrifice that remains is the one that has already been offered, and the only way to benefit from it is to hold fast to Christ.

The great Reformers saw in this verse a decisive word against any doctrine that would add to Christ’s sacrifice. Calvin argued that if forgiveness has been secured by Christ’s offering, then to institute other offerings for sin is to deny the efficacy of his. The point stands on the plain words of the text, and it is the reason Christian faith does not rest on anything it does but on what Christ has done. The exposition of Hebrews ends, fittingly, not with a list of duties but with a declaration of completed grace. Duties will follow, in abundance, from 10:19 onward, but they flow from this fountain: where there is forgiveness, there is no longer an offering for sin.

For Reflection

  1. The law’s sacrifices were a “shadow of the good things to come” (10:1). In what ways can the Old Testament’s shadows, such as the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the Day of Atonement, deepen your understanding of what Christ has done, rather than distract from it?
  2. The old sacrifices brought “a reminder of sins year by year” (10:3), while in the new covenant God promises to “remember them no more” (10:17). Are there habits in your spiritual life that keep reminding you of guilt rather than resting in forgiveness? How does the Lord’s Supper, as a “remembrance” of Christ, reshape what you remember?
  3. The Son came saying, “Behold, I have come to do your will, O God” (10:7). How does Christ’s embodied obedience shape what it means for you to offer your body and your life to God (cf. Rom 12:1; Heb 13:21)?
  4. Every priest stands; Christ sat down (10:11–12). What would it look like for you to live from the “finished work” of Christ, rather than from a sense of constantly having to make yourself acceptable to God?
  5. Believers “have been sanctified” (10:10) and are “being sanctified” (10:14). How do you hold together the settled assurance of Christ’s perfect offering and the earnest pursuit of holiness to which Hebrews calls you (12:14)?
  6. “Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:18). Why does the finality of Christ’s sacrifice bring both deep comfort and serious warning (cf. 10:26)?
