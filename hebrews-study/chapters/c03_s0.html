Hebrews 3

Faithful over God’s House

Chapter 2 closed with a phrase that is really a promissory note: Jesus had to be made like his brothers in every respect “so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest” (2:17). The author will pay out the word “merciful” in 4:14–5:10. Here in chapter 3 he takes up the word “faithful” (πιστός pistos). To do so he sets Jesus beside the most revered figure in Israel’s history, Moses, the man of whom God himself said, “in all my house he is faithful” (Num 12:7). The comparison does not belittle Moses. Moses was faithful, and the author says so twice. But Moses was faithful as a servant in the house, while Jesus is faithful as the Son over the house, and he is even honored as its builder.

The movement of thought in Hebrews is steady and deliberate. Chapters 1–2 showed the Son superior to the angels, through whom the law was delivered (2:2). Chapter 3 shows him superior to Moses, through whom the law was given and Israel was led out of Egypt. Chapter 4 will mention Joshua, who led the next generation into Canaan and still did not give them God’s final “rest” (4:8). Chapters 5–7 will set Jesus beside Aaron and the Levitical priesthood. Each of these comparisons answers the same pastoral question. Why should a weary congregation, tempted to drift back toward the old and familiar, hold fast to Jesus? Because every mediator of the old order pointed beyond himself to him.

Structure. The chapter falls into two parts, and the second part runs over into chapter 4.

  • 3:1–6: Jesus, worthy of more glory than Moses. A short exposition built on Numbers 12:7. Its key word is “house” (οἶκος oikos), which occurs six times in six verses. It moves from the faithful servant to the faithful Son and ends with the church: “whose house we are, if we hold fast.”
  • 3:7–19: “Today, if you hear his voice.” The author quotes Psalm 95:7–11 as the present speech of the Holy Spirit. He applies it to his hearers (3:12–15) and then draws out the history behind it with a run of pointed questions (3:16–19). The section continues through 4:13, where the theme of God’s “rest” is developed and the warning reaches its climax in the living, piercing word of God.

Key words to watch. Notice how the vocabulary binds the two halves together:

  • Faithful and unbelief. Jesus is “faithful” (pistos, 3:2, 5). The wilderness generation fell through “unbelief” (ἀπιστία apistia, 3:12, 19). The faithfulness of the Son is set against the faithlessness of the fathers, and the congregation must decide which pattern it will follow.
  • Partakers (μέτοχοι metochoi). The readers are “partakers of a heavenly calling” (3:1) and “partakers of Christ” (3:14). The word looks back to the Son’s “companions” in 1:9.
  • Hold fast (κατέχω katechō). Two parallel conditional sentences frame the chapter’s application (3:6, 14): “if we hold fast….” The verb returns at 10:23.
  • Heart, voice, Today. The psalm speaks of hardened hearts, of a voice to be heard, and of a “Today” of opportunity. The author picks up all three in his application (3:12, 13, 15).
  • Enter and rest. The oath “they shall not enter my rest” (3:11, 18) sets the agenda for chapter 4.

What to watch for. Hebrews 3 shows clearly how the author reads the Old Testament. Scripture is not a record of what God once said. It is the voice of the Holy Spirit speaking now (“as the Holy Spirit says,” 3:7), and it is addressed to “you.” The wilderness generation is a mirror held up to the church. That generation was redeemed out of Egypt, led by Moses, and saw God’s works for forty years, yet it fell in the desert because it would not believe. The author does not tell this story to make his hearers despair. He tells it so they will “exhort one another every day” (3:13), lest any of them be hardened by sin’s deceit. The chapter therefore holds together two truths that Scripture always holds together. The first is the glorious security of belonging to the house over which the Son presides. The second is the solemn call to persevere, without which no one can claim that security.
