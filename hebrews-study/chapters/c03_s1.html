Jesus, Worthy of More Glory than Moses (3:1–6)

Translation

1Therefore, holy brothers, partakers of a heavenly calling, consider the apostle and high priest of our confession, Jesus, 2who is faithful to the one who appointed him, as Moses also was in his house. 3For this one has been counted worthy of more glory than Moses, just as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself. 4For every house is built by someone, but the builder of all things is God. 5And Moses was faithful in all his house as a servant, for a testimony to the things that would be spoken later, 6but Christ is faithful as Son over his house. And his house is what we are, if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope.

Setting and Structure

The opening word, “Therefore” (ὅθεν hothen, literally “from which”), ties this section closely to what has just been said. In 2:10–18 the author showed that the Son shared flesh and blood with his “brothers,” suffered, was tempted, and so became “a merciful and faithful high priest” (2:17). Now he calls those same brothers to fix their minds on that high priest, and he begins with the second of the two adjectives, “faithful.” Readers who expect the author to argue straight away about priesthood may be surprised that he turns to Moses rather than Aaron. The reason is the text that controls the paragraph, Numbers 12:7. There God himself praised Moses as the one who was “faithful in all my house.” If any human being in Israel’s history could be called “faithful,” it was Moses. So if Jesus is to be shown as the faithful one par excellence, the comparison must be made with Moses.

The setting of Numbers 12 matters. Miriam and Aaron had challenged Moses’ unique standing (“Has the Lord spoken only through Moses?” Num 12:2). God answered them by distinguishing ordinary prophets, to whom he made himself known in visions and dreams, from Moses, with whom he spoke “mouth to mouth” (Num 12:6–8). That passage was itself a defense of a mediator’s superiority, and the author uses its very words to show a mediator greater still. Hebrews 1:1–2 has already contrasted the many and various ways God spoke through the prophets with his final word in the Son. Numbers 12 shows Moses above the prophets. Hebrews 3 shows the Son above Moses.

The paragraph is tightly built around the word “house” (οἶκος oikos), which occurs in every verse from 3:2 to 3:6. In Greek, as in Hebrew (בַּיִת bayit), the word means both a building and a household: the family, servants, and possessions under one head. The author moves easily between the two senses. Its flow can be traced as follows:

  • 3:1–2. The call to consider Jesus, who is faithful as Moses was faithful in God’s house. Here the point is similarity.
  • 3:3–4. The difference in glory. The builder outranks the building, and God is the builder of all.
  • 3:5–6a. The difference in position. Moses served in the house as a servant who bore witness to what was still to come. Christ presides over the house as Son.
  • 3:6b. The application: “his house is what we are, if we hold fast.”

This is a fine example of the author’s method. A short exposition of a single Old Testament text ends in a conditional sentence aimed at the hearers. That conditional (“if we hold fast,” 3:6) then becomes the hinge into the long exhortation from Psalm 95 that follows (3:7–4:13), where the same condition is repeated almost word for word (3:14).

The historical setting also helps. Few figures were held in higher esteem in Second Temple Judaism than Moses (see the background note at 3:3). For Jewish Christians tempted to return to the synagogue’s securities, the question was urgent: had they left Moses behind by following Jesus? The author’s answer is careful and generous. He never criticizes Moses. He praises his faithfulness in the very words of Scripture. Yet he shows that Moses’ own faithfulness pointed beyond itself “for a testimony to the things that would be spoken later” (3:5). To hold fast to Jesus is not to abandon Moses. It is to receive the one to whom Moses bore witness (compare John 5:46; Luke 24:27).

Verse by Verse

3:1

Therefore, holy brothers, partakers of a heavenly calling, consider the apostle and high priest of our confession, Jesus,

This is the first time the author addresses his hearers directly with a term of affection. He calls them “holy brothers” (ἀδελφοὶ ἅγιοι adelphoi hagioi). Both words come straight from the previous paragraph. Jesus “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:11), and he is “the one who sanctifies,” that is, makes holy, while they are “those who are sanctified” (2:11). So “holy brothers” is not a polite formula. It describes what the readers are because of what Christ has done: they are made holy by him and are members of his family. (Greek used the masculine plural “brothers” for a mixed group, and the address plainly includes the sisters of the congregation.)

They are also “partakers of a heavenly calling.” The word “partakers” (μέτοχοι metochoi, from μέτοχος metochos) means sharers, partners, those who hold something in common. It was used of the Son’s “companions” in 1:9 (quoting Ps 45:7) and will recur at 3:14 (“partakers of Christ”), 6:4 (“partakers of the Holy Spirit”), and 12:8 (sharing in discipline). The calling is “heavenly” (ἐπουράνιος epouranios) because it comes from heaven and summons to heaven. It is the call of the God who is “bringing many sons to glory” (2:10). Later the author will speak of the heavenly country the patriarchs longed for (11:16) and of “the heavenly Jerusalem” to which believers have already come (12:22). Paul’s phrase “the upward call of God in Christ Jesus” (Phil 3:14) expresses the same thought. The readers may have felt the loss of earthly status and possessions (10:32–34), but their true citizenship and destiny lay above.

The main verb is a command: “consider” (κατανοήσατε katanoēsate, from κατανοέω katanoeō). The word means more than a passing glance. It means to look at something attentively, to observe it carefully, to think it through. Jesus used it when he told his disciples to “consider the ravens” and “consider the lilies” (Luke 12:24, 27), that is, to study them until their lesson sinks in. Hebrews will use it again at 10:24: “let us consider one another.” The tense is aorist, which presents the action as a whole, a decisive act of attention. The cure for drifting (2:1) is to fix the mind on Jesus. The same thought returns at the climax of the sermon: “looking to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2).

Jesus is called “the apostle and high priest of our confession.” Only one Greek article governs both nouns (τὸν ἀπόστολον καὶ ἀρχιερέα ton apostolon kai archierea), which presents the two titles as a single, unified description of one person in two complementary roles. This is the only place in the New Testament where Jesus is called an “apostle.” The word ἀπόστολος apostolos means “one who is sent,” an envoy authorized to act for the sender. The Gospel of John repeatedly describes Jesus as the one the Father “sent” (John 3:17; 5:36; 17:3, 18; 20:21 uses the related verb apostellō). In Hebrews the idea fits the opening of the sermon. God “has spoken to us in his Son” (1:2), and the salvation “began to be spoken through the Lord” (2:3). As apostle, Jesus comes from God to us with God’s final word. As high priest, he goes from us to God, representing his people and making propitiation for their sins (2:17). Many interpreters, ancient and modern, have noted that the two titles sum up the two offices the Old Testament divided between Moses and Aaron. Moses was the one God sent (Exod 3:10–15) to speak to the people, and Aaron was the priest who stood before God for them. In Jesus both offices are united, and both are carried out perfectly.

Historical Background: The Agent Who Represents His Sender

Jewish law and custom gave great weight to the authorized agent, the שָׁלִיחַ shaliach (“one sent”). The Mishnah states the principle that “a man’s agent is like himself” (m. Ber. 5:5). An agent carried the full authority of the one who commissioned him, so to receive or reject the envoy was to receive or reject the sender. The New Testament reflects the same idea: “Whoever receives me receives not me but the one who sent me” (Mark 9:37; compare John 13:20). When Hebrews calls Jesus “the apostle,” it does not mean that he was one envoy among many. He is the one Envoy in whom God himself has come and spoken. To ignore his word is to ignore God (2:1–3; 12:25).

Finally, Jesus is the apostle and high priest “of our confession” (τῆς ὁμολογίας ἡμῶν tēs homologias hēmōn). The word ὁμολογία homologia, “confession,” means a public declaration, the faith a person acknowledges openly. It appears three times in Hebrews, and each time it is something to be held fast (3:1; 4:14; 10:23). It probably refers both to the act of confessing Christ, perhaps at baptism (compare 10:22–23), and to the content of what is confessed, such as “Jesus is the Son of God” (compare 4:14). The point is that the readers have already made this confession. The danger is not that they will never confess Christ. The danger is that, under pressure, they will let go of what they once confessed. So the author places the name “Jesus” emphatically at the end of the sentence. This is the human name of the one who suffered and was tempted (2:9, 18). The one they confess is no abstraction but the man Jesus, now exalted, who knows their weakness and is faithful to save.

3:2

who is faithful to the one who appointed him, as Moses also was in his house.

The first thing to consider about Jesus is that he is “faithful” (πιστός pistos). The Greek word can mean “believing,” “trusting,” or “trustworthy,” “reliable.” Here the second sense is primary. Jesus is the one who can be relied on completely, both by God who commissioned him and by the people he represents. Yet the first sense is not far away. In 2:13 the Son said, “I will put my trust in him,” and in 12:2 Jesus is “the pioneer and perfecter of faith,” the one who ran the whole course of trusting obedience. His faithfulness toward God is the ground of our confidence in him. The present participle “being” (ὄντα onta) presents his faithfulness as an ongoing fact. He was faithful in his earthly obedience, and he is faithful now in his heavenly ministry.

He is faithful “to the one who appointed him” (τῷ ποιήσαντι αὐτόν tō poiēsanti auton). The verb ποιέω poieō usually means “make” or “do,” and some English versions render it “made.” But in contexts of office it means “appoint” or “install.” Jesus “made twelve” to be with him (Mark 3:14), meaning he appointed them. Samuel speaks of the Lord “who appointed Moses and Aaron” (1 Sam 12:6), where the Hebrew verb is the ordinary verb “make” (asah). In Hebrews the context is clearly about office: Jesus is “apostle and high priest,” and in 5:5 the author says that Christ did not exalt himself to become high priest but was appointed by the one who said, “You are my Son.” So the phrase refers to the Son’s appointment to his saving office, not to his origin as a created being. In the fourth century Arius and his followers appealed to this verse to argue that the Son was a creature. Athanasius answered that the verse speaks of the Son’s appointment as apostle and high priest in his incarnate ministry, not of his eternal being. The rest of Hebrews confirms that answer. The Son is the one through whom God made the ages (1:2), the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of his essential being (1:3), and the Lord who laid the foundations of the earth (1:10). He cannot be both creator of all and a creature.

Jesus is faithful “as Moses also was in his house.” Here the author begins the comparison by stressing likeness, not difference. He does not need to tear Moses down to lift Jesus up. Moses was faithful, and Scripture says so. The phrase “in his house” is drawn from Numbers 12:7, and “his” refers to God, since in Numbers God speaks of “my house.” The house is God’s household, his people Israel, among whom Moses served as leader, lawgiver, and mediator.

Old Testament Text: Numbers 12:7

Hebrew: לֹא־כֵן עַבְדִּי מֹשֶׁה בְּכָל־בֵּיתִי נֶאֱמָן הוּא׃ — lo-khen avdi Mosheh; bekhol-beiti ne’eman hu, “Not so with my servant Moses: in all my house he is faithful.”

The Hebrew word for “faithful,” נֶאֱמָן ne’eman, comes from the root ’mn, the root of “amen” and of the verb “believe.” It means proven reliable, trustworthy, established. God is speaking in a moment of family conflict. Miriam and Aaron had criticized Moses, and God defended him by declaring that Moses held a place of unique trust. Other prophets received visions and dreams, but with Moses God spoke “mouth to mouth” (Num 12:8).

The Greek translation of Numbers renders this verse very closely: Moses is God’s “servant” (θεράπων therapōn), and “in all my house he is faithful” (ἐν ὅλῳ τῷ οἴκῳ μου πιστός ἐστιν). Hebrews takes up all the key words of this Greek text: “servant” (3:5), “in all … house” (3:5), and “faithful” (3:2, 5). The author is not making a loose allusion. He is expounding a specific verse phrase by phrase.

The same cluster of words, “faithful” and “house,” appears in other Old Testament promises that would have been in the author’s mind. In 1 Samuel 2:35 God promises, “I will raise up for myself a faithful priest… and I will build him a sure house,” using the same Hebrew word ne’eman for both “faithful” and “sure.” In 2 Samuel 7:16, the promise to David’s son (already quoted in Heb 1:5), God says, “your house and your kingdom shall be made sure [וְנֶאְמַן vene’man] forever.” Several commentators think these texts stand in the background of Hebrews’ description of Jesus as the “faithful high priest” (2:17) who is Son “over his house” (3:6). Whether or not the author meant to evoke them, they show that Scripture had joined “faithfulness,” “priesthood,” “sonship,” and “God’s house” long before Hebrews brought them together in Jesus.

Textual Note

Two small variants appear in 3:1–2. In 3:1 many later manuscripts read “Christ Jesus” instead of simply “Jesus.” The shorter reading is supported by the oldest witnesses and is almost certainly original; scribes often filled out the names of Christ. In 3:2 a number of important manuscripts read “in all his house,” adding ὅλῳ holō, “all,” while some of the oldest witnesses (including the early papyrus P46 and Codex Vaticanus) lack it. The SBL Greek New Testament omits the word; the Nestle-Aland text prints it in brackets to signal doubt. Scribes could easily have added “all” to match 3:5 and Numbers 12:7. The meaning is not affected, since “all” appears in 3:5 in any case.

3:3

For this one has been counted worthy of more glory than Moses, just as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself.

Now the comparison turns from likeness to difference. The word “For” (γάρ gar) gives the reason the readers should “consider” Jesus (3:1). He is not merely Moses’ equal. “This one” (οὗτος houtos) “has been counted worthy of more glory than Moses.” The verb ἠξίωται ēxiōtai (from ἀξιόω axioō, “to consider worthy, deem deserving”) is in the perfect tense and the passive voice. The perfect describes a past act with lasting results: Jesus has been judged worthy, and that verdict stands. The passive points to God as the one who made the judgment, a common Jewish way of speaking of God’s action without naming him. Jesus did not seize this glory; God bestowed it. The thought recalls 2:9, where Jesus, because of the suffering of death, is “crowned with glory and honor.” Those two words, “glory” (δόξα doxa) and “honor” (τιμή timē), reappear here in 3:3. The glory and honor that Psalm 8 promised to humanity are now Jesus’ possession. They exceed even the glory of Moses.

That last phrase would have struck the first hearers. Moses was famous for glory. When he came down from Sinai, “the skin of his face shone” because he had been speaking with God, and the Israelites were afraid to come near him (Exod 34:29–35). Paul reflects on this in 2 Corinthians 3:7–18, where he contrasts the fading glory on Moses’ face with the surpassing glory of the new covenant. Hebrews makes a similar point by a different route. Jesus is “the radiance of God’s glory” (1:3). The glory Moses reflected for a time belongs to the Son by nature, and it is now publicly confirmed by his exaltation.

The author explains the difference with an illustration from everyday life: “just as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself.” The Greek is compressed. Literally it reads, “by as much as more honor than the house has the one who built it” (καθ’ ὅσον πλείονα τιμὴν ἔχει τοῦ οἴκου ὁ κατασκευάσας αὐτόν). The construction “by as much as” (kath’ hoson) is typical of Hebrews’ “how much more” style of argument (compare 1:4; 7:20–22; 8:6). The verb κατασκευάζω kataskeuazō means to construct, prepare, or furnish. It covers both building a structure and fully equipping it, and Hebrews will use it later of “preparing” the tabernacle (9:2, 6) and Noah’s “preparing” of the ark (11:7).

The logic is simple. Anyone admires a fine house, but the honor belongs to its architect and builder. Moses, for all his greatness, was part of the house. He was a member of God’s household, one of the people. Jesus stands in the relation of builder to the house. That is a remarkable claim. In the Old Testament it is God who builds his house (2 Sam 7:11, 27; Ps 127:1). The promise to David was that his son would “build a house for my name” (2 Sam 7:13). Jesus himself said, “I will build my church” (Matt 16:18). The author does not stop to define how Christ is the builder, but the next verse shows where his thought is going.

Historical Background: The Glory of Moses in Jewish Tradition

It is hard to overstate how highly Moses was regarded in the Judaism of the first century. He was the lawgiver, the prophet without equal (Deut 34:10–12), the friend with whom God spoke face to face (Exod 33:11). Ben Sira, writing around 180 BC, says that God made Moses “equal in glory to the holy ones” and “glorified him in the presence of kings” (Sir 45:2–3). Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish contemporary of the apostles, wrote a two-volume Life of Moses in which he presents Moses as king, lawgiver, priest, and prophet all in one. Drawing on Exodus 7:1 (“I have made you God to Pharaoh”), he can even say that Moses was called “god and king” of the whole nation (Mos. 1.158), and he cites Numbers 12:7 more than once to celebrate Moses’ unique trustworthiness. Josephus, writing in Rome in the 90s, can call Moses a “divine man.” A strand of later rabbinic interpretation of Numbers 12:7 took “in all my house he is faithful” to mean that Moses ranked even above the ministering angels.

Against this background, the order of Hebrews’ argument makes sense. Having shown the Son superior to the angels (chs. 1–2), the author now shows him superior to Moses. For a Jewish Christian congregation, this was not an abstract comparison. Moses stood for the whole order of the law, the covenant, and the tabernacle. To say that Jesus has “more glory than Moses” was to say that the gospel is not a supplement to Moses but the reality to which Moses pointed. Yet the author does this without a hint of contempt. He shares his hearers’ reverence for Moses (see 11:23–28), and he uses that reverence to magnify Christ.

3:4

For every house is built by someone, but the builder of all things is God.

This short verse looks at first like a truism: every house has a builder. But the second half lifts the argument to its highest level: “the builder of all things is God.” The verb is again κατασκευάζω kataskeuazō, as in 3:3. The aorist participle ὁ … κατασκευάσας ho … kataskeuasas, “the one who built,” refers to God’s completed work of creation. And “all things” (πάντα panta) is the same phrase used of the Son’s inheritance and work in chapter 1. God appointed him “heir of all things,” and the Son “upholds all things by the word of his power” (1:2–3).

Why does the author add this sentence? Its function has been understood in more than one way.

Interpretive Options

  • The Son shares God’s work as builder. Many interpreters, John Owen prominent among them, read 3:4 as an implicit claim about Christ’s divine identity. Verse 3 called Christ the builder of the house. Verse 4 says the builder of all things is God. The author does not mean two builders competing for the honor. He means that the work of the Son is the work of God, since it was “through him” that God made the ages (1:2) and it is the Son who “in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth” (1:10). On this reading, 3:4 explains why the builder-and-house comparison is fitting. Christ stands on the side of the Creator, Moses on the side of the creation.
  • A safeguard that God is the ultimate builder. Others read 3:4 more modestly. The author has spoken of Christ as builder, and he wants to make clear that this does not compete with God’s honor. All building finally traces back to God, who established the house through his Son and appointed both Moses and Jesus to their roles (3:2). On this view the verse affirms God’s ultimate authorship without making a direct statement about the Son’s deity.

The two readings are not far apart, and both preserve the main point: the house belongs to God, and Christ’s relation to it is that of builder and Son, not servant. The first reading fits Hebrews’ opening chapter especially well. There the author has already applied to the Son texts that speak of God as Creator and addressed him as “God” (1:8–10). It would be natural for the author’s hearers to understand “the builder of all things is God” as including the Son through whom all things were made (compare John 1:3; Col 1:16). The verse thus quietly reinforces the deepest reason Jesus has “more glory than Moses.” Moses was a creature serving within creation, while the Son shares the Creator’s glory.

The verse also brings the reader back to God himself. Hebrews never allows its high view of Christ to obscure the Father. It is God who speaks (1:1), God who appoints (3:2), God who builds (3:4), and God who brings many sons to glory (2:10). The Son’s glory is the glory of the Father’s own work carried out through him. For the readers, this was great encouragement. The house they belonged to was not a human institution that might collapse under persecution. God built it, and he will see it completed.

3:5

And Moses was faithful in all his house as a servant, for a testimony to the things that would be spoken later,

Verses 5 and 6 form a balanced pair, marked in Greek by the particles μέν … δέ men … de, “on the one hand … on the other.” “Moses on the one hand was faithful … as a servant, Christ on the other hand as Son.” The author now quotes Numbers 12:7 almost word for word: Moses was faithful “in all his house” (ἐν ὅλῳ τῷ οἴκῳ αὐτοῦ en holō tō oikō autou). The praise is real. Moses was faithful in all God’s house. There was no part of his commission he neglected. He made the tabernacle “according to the pattern” shown him on the mountain (8:5; Exod 25:40), and the book of Exodus repeats that he did “just as the Lord commanded Moses” (Exod 39:42–43; 40:16).

But he was faithful “as a servant.” The word is θεράπων therapōn, which occurs only here in the New Testament. It comes directly from the Greek text of Numbers 12:7, which used it to translate the Hebrew עֶבֶד eved, “servant.” The Greek Old Testament also uses it of Moses in Exodus 4:10 and 14:31 (“they believed in the Lord and in his servant Moses”). It is not a demeaning word. Unlike δοῦλος doulos, “slave,” therapōn often carried the sense of a trusted attendant, one who renders honorable, willing service, like a steward in a great household. “Servant of the Lord” was one of Moses’ highest titles (Deut 34:5; Josh 1:1–2). Still, a servant, however honored, is not the son and heir. Jesus drew the same contrast: “the slave does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever” (John 8:35).

The purpose of Moses’ service was “for a testimony to the things that would be spoken later” (εἰς μαρτύριον τῶν λαληθησομένων eis martyrion tōn lalēthēsomenōn). The participle lalēthēsomenōn is a future passive: “the things that will be spoken.” Future participles are rare in the New Testament, and this one looks ahead from Moses’ time to a later word of God. What were these things? Several answers have been proposed:

  • Some take the phrase to mean the things God would later speak to Moses himself, so that Moses was a reliable witness to Israel of the revelations he would receive. This is grammatically possible, but it makes the phrase rather flat.
  • Most interpreters understand it to refer to the revelation God would speak in the last days through his Son (1:2) and through those who heard him (2:3). Moses’ whole ministry, including his law, tabernacle, priesthood, and sacrifices, bore witness ahead of time to the gospel.

The second reading fits Hebrews’ use of the verb “speak” (λαλέω laleō) throughout the sermon. God “spoke” to the fathers by the prophets and has now “spoken” in a Son (1:1–2), and the great salvation “began to be spoken through the Lord” (2:3). It also matches the way Hebrews treats the Mosaic institutions. They are a “copy and shadow of the heavenly things” (8:5), “a shadow of the good things to come” (10:1), a parable for the present time (9:9). Moses’ faithfulness was precisely his faithfulness as a witness. He built the tabernacle exactly according to the pattern because it was meant to point beyond itself. The best way to honor Moses, then, is to receive the one to whom he testified. As Jesus said to his opponents, “If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me” (John 5:46).

This is crucial for the first readers. If they were tempted to think that returning to the synagogue would mean returning to Moses, the author shows that it would mean the opposite. To turn from Christ to Moses would be to misunderstand Moses, whose life’s work was a testimony to Christ.

3:6

but Christ is faithful as Son over his house. And his house is what we are, if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope.

The second half of the contrast is stated without a verb: “but Christ as Son over his house” (Χριστὸς δὲ ὡς υἱὸς ἐπὶ τὸν οἶκον αὐτοῦ Christos de hōs huios epi ton oikon autou). The word “faithful” carries over from 3:5. Three contrasts are packed into these few words.

  • Servant and Son. Moses was a therapōn, Christ is huios, “Son.” The title “Son” has controlled the sermon since 1:2 (“he has spoken to us in a Son”) and was confirmed by the Father’s own words in 1:5 (“You are my Son”). A servant belongs to a household by appointment. A son belongs to it by birth, and he is its heir (1:2).
  • In and over. Moses was faithful in (ἐν en) the house. Christ is faithful over (ἐπί epi, here with the accusative case, meaning “over, in charge of”) the house. Moses was a member of the household, while Christ is its head. The author will describe Jesus the same way in 10:21: “a great priest over the house of God.”
  • Moses and Christ. This is the first time the title “Christ” (Χριστός Christos, “Anointed One,” Messiah) appears in Hebrews. Up to now the author has used “Son,” “Lord,” and “Jesus.” Introducing the messianic title here is apt, for it was to the anointed son of David that God promised, “I will establish him in my house and in my kingdom forever” (1 Chr 17:14).

Old Testament Text: 1 Chronicles 17:14

Hebrew: וְהַעֲמַדְתִּיהוּ בְּבֵיתִי וּבְמַלְכוּתִי עַד־הָעוֹלָם — veha’amadtihu beveiti uvemalkhuti ad-ha’olam, “And I will establish him in my house and in my kingdom forever.”

In the Chronicler’s version of God’s promise to David, the promised son is set not only over David’s dynasty (“your house”) but in God’s own house and God’s own kingdom (“my house … my kingdom”). The same chapter contains the words quoted in Hebrews 1:5: “I will be to him a father, and he will be to me a son” (1 Chr 17:13; 2 Sam 7:14). Hebrews does not quote 1 Chronicles 17:14, but the promise explains how naturally the author can join “Son” and “God’s house.” The house over which Christ presides is God’s house (compare 3:2, 4; 10:21), and his rule over it is “forever.”

Grammatically, “his house” in 3:6a could mean God’s house, as in 3:2 and 3:5, or Christ’s own house. The difference is small. The house belongs to God, and the Son, as heir and head, rules it as his own. What matters is the next clause, where the author suddenly identifies the house: “And his house is what we are” (ὃς οἶκός ἐσμεν ἡμεῖς hos oikos esmen hēmeis, literally “whose house are we”). The house is not a building of stone. It is not the tabernacle or the temple in Jerusalem, and not even Israel considered as a nation. It is the people of God gathered around the Son. The emphatic pronoun “we” (hēmeis) stands at the end of the clause. The very household in which Moses served faithfully, God’s people across the ages, is now found in the congregation that confesses Jesus. Elsewhere in the New Testament the same truth is expressed in similar terms: believers are “the household of God” (Eph 2:19; 1 Tim 3:15) and “living stones … being built up as a spiritual house” (1 Pet 2:5). Moses and the faithful of Israel belong to this same house (compare 11:39–40). Christians have not left God’s house; they have come under the rule of its Son.

Then comes the condition: “if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope” (ἐὰν τὴν παρρησίαν καὶ τὸ καύχημα τῆς ἐλπίδος κατάσχωμεν). The verb κατέχω katechō means to hold firmly, keep possession of, not let go. The aorist subjunctive views the holding as a whole, a holding that continues to the end. Two things are to be held fast:

  • Boldness (παρρησία parrēsia). In the Greek city, this was the free citizen’s right to speak openly in the assembly, the opposite of the timidity of a slave or a foreigner. In the Bible it came to mean confident, unashamed openness before God and before people. In Hebrews it is the confidence with which believers approach God’s throne (4:16) and enter the sanctuary through Jesus’ blood (10:19). It is also the courage that refuses to be ashamed before a hostile world (10:35: “do not throw away your boldness”). Since the readers were tempted to hide their faith, “boldness” has both a Godward and a public side.
  • The boast of our hope (τὸ καύχημα τῆς ἐλπίδος to kauchēma tēs elpidos). A kauchēma is a ground of boasting, that in which one glories and takes pride. In an honor-conscious society the readers had been shamed for their faith (10:33). The author tells them that their hope, God’s promised future, is something to glory in, not to be embarrassed by. “Hope” is a major theme in Hebrews (6:11, 18–19; 7:19; 10:23), the anchor of the soul that reaches behind the curtain.

The force of the condition must be weighed carefully. The author does not say, “You will become God’s house if you hold fast,” as though perseverance earned a place in the household. Nor does he say, “You are God’s house regardless of whether you hold fast.” He says, “We are his house, if we hold fast.” Present membership is shown to be real by perseverance. The same structure appears even more clearly in 3:14, where the verb is in the perfect tense (“we have become partakers of Christ, if we hold…”), and the question will be discussed more fully there. Here it is enough to notice that the author includes himself (“we”). He does not stand above his hearers, issuing threats. He stands among them as a fellow member of the house, and he urges them to keep hold of the confidence and hope that mark the Son’s household.

Textual Note: “Firm to the End”

Many manuscripts, including Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Alexandrinus, and the majority of later Greek manuscripts, add the words “firm to the end” (μέχρι τέλους βεβαίαν mechri telous bebaian) at the end of 3:6, so that it reads “if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope firm to the end.” The King James Version follows this longer text. The words are absent from the earliest witnesses, the papyri P13 and P46 and Codex Vaticanus, along with some early translations and Latin writers.

Bruce Metzger, explaining the decision of the editorial committee of the United Bible Societies’ text, notes two reasons for regarding the words as an addition. First, they are identical to the phrase in 3:14, and a scribe could easily have imported them from there to make the two parallel conditions match. Second, the grammar betrays the borrowing. The adjective bebaian, “firm,” is feminine, as it properly is in 3:14 where it agrees with the feminine noun “beginning” (archē). In 3:6 the noun nearest to it is the neuter “boast” (kauchēma), so if the author had written the phrase here, he would most naturally have used the neuter form. The shorter text is therefore almost certainly original. The thought is not lost, for 3:14 supplies it in its proper place. A related variant in 3:6 reads “if indeed” (eanper) instead of “if” (ean), probably also under the influence of 3:14.

For Reflection

  1. The author calls his struggling readers “holy brothers, partakers of a heavenly calling” before he warns them. How does knowing who you are in Christ prepare you to hear his warnings rightly?
  2. To “consider” Jesus is to give him sustained, careful attention. What practices help you fix your mind on Jesus as “the apostle and high priest of our confession” rather than merely glancing at him?
  3. Hebrews honors Moses fully while showing Christ’s greater glory. How can Christians speak of the Old Testament and its servants with deep respect while still confessing that they pointed to Christ?
  4. Moses was faithful “in all” God’s house as a servant. In what areas of your life is God calling you to quiet, thorough faithfulness, even when no one is watching?
  5. “His house is what we are.” How does this truth shape the way you think about your local congregation, especially when it seems weak or small?
  6. What would it look like for you to hold fast your “boldness” and the “boast” of your hope in a setting where your faith brings embarrassment or loss?
