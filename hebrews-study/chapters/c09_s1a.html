The Earthly Sanctuary and Its Limits (9:1–10)

Translation

1Now the first covenant had regulations for worship and its earthly sanctuary. 2For a tent was set up, the first one, in which were the lampstand and the table and the presentation of the loaves; this is called the Holy Place. 3And behind the second curtain was the tent called the Holy of Holies, 4having a golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant, covered on every side with gold, in which were a golden jar holding the manna, and Aaron’s rod that budded, and the tablets of the covenant; 5and above it were the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat. Of these things we cannot now speak in detail.

6These things having been set up in this way, the priests enter the first tent continually as they carry out their acts of worship, 7but into the second only the high priest enters, once a year, and not without blood, which he offers for himself and for the sins the people committed in ignorance. 8By this the Holy Spirit is making clear that the way into the holy places has not yet been disclosed while the first tent still has standing. 9This is a parable for the present time, according to which gifts and sacrifices are offered that cannot make the worshiper perfect with respect to conscience, 10since they deal only with foods and drinks and various washings—regulations for the flesh, imposed until the time of setting things right.

Setting and Structure

Chapter 8 ended with a striking sentence: by calling the covenant promised through Jeremiah “new,” God “has made the first old; and what is becoming old and growing aged is near to vanishing” (8:13). Chapter 9 opens by looking closely at that “first” covenant and the sanctuary that went with it. The connection is signaled by the opening words Εἶχε μὲν οὖν eiche men oun, “Now [the first] had….” The little particle μέν men sets up a contrast that will not be completed until 9:11, where its partner δέ de, “but,” arrives: “But Christ, having arrived as high priest….” The whole of 9:1–10 is therefore the first half of a two-part comparison. The reader should hear it as a long “on the one hand,” waiting for the “on the other.”

The paragraph has two movements, matching the two things named in 9:1, “regulations for worship” and “an earthly sanctuary,” but in reverse order (a small chiasm, a mirror-pattern common in ancient writing):

  • The sanctuary itself (9:2–5). The preacher walks the reader through the tabernacle: first the outer tent with its lampstand, table, and bread, then, behind the second curtain, the inner tent with the golden incense altar, the ark, and the sacred objects in and above the ark. He then breaks off: “Of these things we cannot now speak in detail.”
  • The worship carried on in it (9:6–10). Ordinary priests go in and out of the outer tent constantly; the high priest alone enters the inner room, once a year, with blood. From this arrangement the Holy Spirit teaches a lesson (9:8), the arrangement is called a “parable” (9:9), and the whole system is judged to be limited to the “flesh” and temporary, “until the time of setting things right” (9:10).

The description is drawn from the wilderness tabernacle of Exodus 25–40 and Leviticus 16, not from Herod’s temple in Jerusalem. That is consistent with the whole sermon, which argues from the text of Scripture rather than from current temple practice (compare 8:5, quoting Exod 25:40). It also explains why the ark is mentioned, though in the Second Temple period the inner room of the sanctuary was empty: the ark had disappeared at the Babylonian destruction in 586 BC. Josephus, describing Herod’s temple, says the innermost chamber contained nothing at all and was unapproachable and unseen by anyone (J.W. 5.219); the Mishnah says that after the ark was taken away, a bare stone, called “the Foundation Stone,” stood where it had been, and the high priest set the firepan of incense on it (m. Yoma 5:2). The preacher, however, is interested in the God-given pattern, and the pattern included the ark.

Two features of the passage deserve notice at the outset. First, the preacher’s tone is reverent. He calls the cherubim “cherubim of glory,” he mentions gold three times, and he lingers over the objects as one who loves them. The argument of Hebrews is never that the old covenant was bad or that its rituals were man-made inventions; God himself commanded them (8:5). The argument is that they were preparatory, built to point beyond themselves. Second, the decisive interpretive word comes in 9:8: “the Holy Spirit is making clear.” The preacher believes that the arrangement of the tabernacle, as Scripture records it, was itself a message from God. The two rooms, the curtain between them, the restriction of access to one man on one day, and the requirement of blood were all designed to say something: the way into God’s presence was not yet open. The tabernacle preached a sermon about its own limits.

The historical setting gives this teaching its pastoral edge. The congregation addressed in Hebrews were tempted to drift back toward the visible, ancient, honored institutions of Judaism—institutions that in the mid-60s were still functioning in Jerusalem (note the present tenses of 9:6–9: the priests “enter,” the high priest “offers,” gifts “are offered”). To believers who felt the pull of those institutions, the preacher says in effect: Look carefully at the very sanctuary you are tempted to return to. Its own design confesses that it could not bring you near to God. It was a picture, and the One it pictured has come.

Verse by Verse

9:1

Now the first covenant had regulations for worship and its earthly sanctuary.

The subject of the sentence in Greek is simply ἡ πρώτη hē prōtē, “the first” (feminine). The nearest feminine noun it can refer to is “covenant” (διαθήκη diathēkē), the subject of 8:6–13, where Jeremiah’s “new covenant” made the “first” old (8:7, 13). So translators rightly supply “covenant.” (Some later manuscripts added the word “tent” here, but that is a misunderstanding; the tent, σκηνή skēnē, is not introduced until 9:2.)

Many manuscripts add the little word καί kai, “also, even” (“even the first covenant had…”); the SBL text, following other early witnesses, lacks it. Either way the sense is concessive, as the particle men already signals. The preacher grants what is true: the first covenant had real, God-given institutions. The verb εἶχε eiche, “had,” is in the imperfect tense, which describes ongoing possession in the past. It does not necessarily mean the institutions had already ceased to exist when Hebrews was written (9:6–9 will speak of them in the present tense). The past tense fits the preacher’s focus on the tabernacle in the wilderness, the original pattern given through Moses.

The first covenant had two things. (1) δικαιώματα λατρείας dikaiōmata latreias, “regulations for worship.” A δικαίωμα dikaiōma is a legal requirement or ordinance, something decreed as right. In the Greek Old Testament the word often translates Hebrew חֹק choq, “statute,” or מִשְׁפָּט mishpat, “ordinance.” The same word returns at the end of the paragraph, in 9:10: “regulations (dikaiōmata) for the flesh.” The repetition frames the paragraph and quietly announces its verdict: the regulations for worship turn out to be regulations of the flesh. λατρεία latreia means religious service or worship, especially the priestly, ritual service of the sanctuary (Rom 9:4 lists “the worship” among Israel’s privileges). The related verb λατρεύω latreuō, “to serve/worship,” appears in 9:9 and 9:14 and is key to the chapter: the old worship could not perfect “the one who worships” (9:9), but the blood of Christ purifies the conscience “to worship the living God” (9:14).

(2) τό τε ἅγιον κοσμικόν to te hagion kosmikon, “and its earthly sanctuary.” τὸ ἅγιον to hagion (singular, “the holy [place]”) here means the sanctuary as a whole. The adjective κοσμικός kosmikos comes from κόσμος kosmos, “world,” and means “belonging to this world, earthly.” It does not mean “worldly” in the moral sense of sinful. It means that the sanctuary was part of the created, material order—made of acacia wood, gold, linen, and goat hair—in contrast to the sanctuary “not made with hands, that is, not of this creation” (9:11) and “heaven itself” (9:24). Its placement at the very end of the sentence in Greek gives it weight: the sanctuary was holy, yes, but earthly.

Some early interpreters (Chrysostom among them) heard in kosmikos a hint that the tabernacle was a model of the cosmos, an idea found in Philo and Josephus, who saw the structure and furnishings as symbols of heaven, earth, and sea. But the preacher’s point in Hebrews is different and simpler. He contrasts the earthly with the heavenly (8:4–5; 9:23–24), the copy with the original, the shadow with the reality—not the tabernacle-as-universe with something else. The force of the word is “of this world,” and therefore temporary and limited.

This opening verse is a model of fairness. The preacher could have dismissed the old order; instead he gives it its due. Israel’s worship was ordered by God and centered on a sanctuary he designed (Exod 25:8–9). That makes the coming contrast all the more powerful: if even this God-ordained institution was only a preparation, how great must be the reality to which it pointed.

9:2

For a tent was set up, the first one, in which were the lampstand and the table and the presentation of the loaves; this is called the Holy Place.

The preacher now describes the sanctuary. He speaks of it as two “tents” (σκηνή skēnē): “the first” (outer) tent and, in 9:3, the inner tent. In the Exodus account the tabernacle was a single structure divided by a curtain into two rooms (Exod 26:33), but the preacher’s way of speaking—two tents, a “first” and a “second”—is deliberate. It allows him to say in 9:8 that the way into the holy places was not open “while the first tent still has standing,” using “first” in a double sense (the outer room, and the whole first-covenant order). The verb κατεσκευάσθη kateskeuasthē, “was set up, furnished, prepared,” is the same verb used of building and equipping a house in 3:3–4 (“every house is built by someone, but the one who built all things is God”). It describes the construction and outfitting of the tabernacle under Moses (Exod 35–40).

Three items were in the outer room:

  • The lampstand (λυχνία lychnia), Hebrew מְנֹרַת זָהָב טָהוֹר menorat zahav tahor, “a lampstand of pure gold” (Exod 25:31), of hammered work, made together with its utensils from a talent of gold, with seven lamps and cups shaped like almond blossoms (Exod 25:31–40). It stood on the south side of the room (Exod 26:35) and was tended every evening and morning so that it burned “continually” before the Lord (Exod 27:20–21; Lev 24:2–4). It was the only light in the windowless tent.
  • The table (τράπεζα trapeza), Hebrew שֻׁלְחָן shulchan, of acacia wood overlaid with gold, on the north side (Exod 25:23–30; 26:35).
  • The presentation of the loaves (ἡ πρόθεσις τῶν ἄρτων hē prothesis tōn artōn). πρόθεσις prothesis means “setting forth, display.” The phrase is the Greek Old Testament’s rendering of what the Hebrew calls לֶחֶם פָּנִים lechem panim, literally “bread of the face” or “bread of the Presence”: “And you shall set on the table bread of the Presence before me continually” (Exod 25:30). Twelve loaves, one for each tribe, were arranged on the table every Sabbath and eaten by the priests in a holy place when they were replaced (Lev 24:5–9). Jesus referred to this bread when David and his men ate it (Mark 2:25–26).

The preacher’s list follows Exodus closely. He does not mention the golden incense altar here, though in Exodus it stood in the outer room; he will mention it with the inner room in 9:4, a point discussed there.

“This is called the Holy Place.” The Greek is Ἅγια Hagia, a neuter plural, literally “holy things” or “holy places,” used as a name for the outer room. In the Hebrew of Exod 26:33 the outer room is הַקֹּדֶשׁ haqqodesh, “the holy [place],” and the inner room קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים qodesh haqqodashim, “the holy of holies.” Readers should be aware that Hebrews uses the plural τὰ ἅγια ta hagia with different meanings according to context: here the outer room; in 9:8, 12, 24, 25 and 10:19 the inner sanctuary or the sanctuary as a whole, especially the true, heavenly sanctuary where God dwells. This study translates accordingly (“the Holy Place” here, “the holy places” elsewhere), and the context makes the meaning clear.

Each object in the outer room spoke of fellowship with God: light (the lampstand), bread (the table and loaves), and, as we shall see, the fragrance of prayer (the incense). Yet all of this ministry was carried out by priests on behalf of the people, in a room the people never entered, and even that room stood outside a second curtain. The arrangement itself was an education in both the nearness and the distance of God.

9:3

And behind the second curtain was the tent called the Holy of Holies,

The preacher speaks of “the second curtain” (τὸ δεύτερον καταπέτασμα to deuteron katapetasma). The tabernacle had two hangings: a screen at the entrance to the tent from the courtyard (Exod 26:36–37) and an inner curtain between the two rooms (Exod 26:31–33). The Hebrew calls the inner one the פָּרֹכֶת parokhet, a heavy hanging of blue, purple, and scarlet yarn and fine twisted linen with cherubim woven into it (Exod 26:31). In the Greek Old Testament the word καταπέτασμα katapetasma, “curtain,” is used for both hangings, so the preacher clarifies that he means the second, the inner one. This is the curtain he has already mentioned in 6:19 (our hope enters “into the inner side of the curtain,” where Jesus has gone as forerunner) and will mention again in 10:20, where he says that Jesus opened “a new and living way through the curtain, that is, his flesh.” It was the curtain of the temple that the Gospels say was torn in two from top to bottom when Jesus died (Mark 15:38).

The function of the curtain is stated in Exod 26:33:

Old Testament Text: Exodus 26:33

Hebrew: וְהִבְדִּילָה הַפָּרֹכֶת לָכֶם בֵּין הַקֹּדֶשׁ וּבֵין קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים — vehivdilah haparokhet lakhem ben haqqodesh uven qodesh haqqodashim, “and the curtain shall make a separation for you between the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies.”

The verb הִבְדִּיל hivdil, “to separate, divide,” is the same verb used in Genesis 1 when God separates light from darkness and the waters above from the waters below. The curtain’s purpose was to divide, to mark a boundary that sinful people could not cross.

Behind it was “the tent called the Holy of Holies” (Ἅγια Ἁγίων Hagia Hagiōn). The Hebrew expression “holy of holies” is a superlative: in Hebrew, putting a noun with its own plural (as in “King of kings” or “Song of Songs”) expresses the highest degree. This was the most holy place, the room where the Lord said, “I will meet with you there, and I will speak with you from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim” (Exod 25:22). It was a perfect cube, ten cubits on each side (inferred from the measurements in Exodus 26; Solomon’s inner sanctuary was twenty cubits each way, 1 Kgs 6:20). It was dark, lit only by the glory of God’s presence. Into it no one went except the high priest, and he only on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:2).

The preacher’s naming of the two rooms—“Holy” and “Holy of Holies”—with the curtain between them prepares for the argument of 9:6–8. Holiness in Israel’s worship was graded: the camp, the courtyard, the Holy Place, the Most Holy Place. Each step inward meant fewer people could go. The structure taught Israel that God is holy, that he dwells among his people, and yet that sinful people cannot simply walk into his presence. That lesson was true and necessary. The gospel does not deny it; it fulfills it, by providing a high priest and a sacrifice that actually qualify sinners to enter (10:19–22).

9:4

having a golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant, covered on every side with gold, in which were a golden jar holding the manna, and Aaron’s rod that budded, and the tablets of the covenant;

The inner tent is described by what it “has” (ἔχουσα echousa, “having”). Two items are named directly, the golden incense altar and the ark; then three objects “in which” (ἐν ᾗ en hē), that is, inside the ark. Each requires comment, and the first raises one of the best-known difficulties in the letter.

1. “A golden altar of incense.” The Greek is χρυσοῦν … θυμιατήριον chrysoun … thymiatērion. A θυμιατήριον thymiatērion is literally “a place or thing for burning incense” (from θυμιάω thymiaō, “to burn incense”). The word can mean a portable censer (a pan or vessel for burning incense) or an incense altar. Older English versions rendered it “golden censer” here; most modern ones render “golden altar of incense.” The latter is more likely, for two reasons. First, Jewish writers of the period who wrote in Greek, Philo and Josephus, use thymiatērion for the golden incense altar of the sanctuary (for example, Philo, Who Is the Heir 226; Josephus, J.W. 5.218). Second, the preacher is listing the major furnishings of the tabernacle (lampstand, table, ark), and the incense altar was one of them, whereas the Old Testament never mentions a golden censer kept in the inner room. In the Greek Old Testament’s account of the Day of Atonement, the firepan the high priest carried in is called a πυρεῖον pyreion (Lev 16:12), not a thymiatērion.

But this creates the difficulty. According to Exodus, the golden altar of incense did not stand inside the Holy of Holies; it stood in the outer room, in front of the curtain:

Old Testament Text: Exodus 30:6 and 40:5, 26

Hebrew (30:6): וְנָתַתָּה אֹתוֹ לִפְנֵי הַפָּרֹכֶת אֲשֶׁר עַל־אֲרֹן הָעֵדֻת לִפְנֵי הַכַּפֹּרֶת אֲשֶׁר עַל־הָעֵדֻת אֲשֶׁר אִוָּעֵד לְךָ שָׁמָּה — venatattah oto lifne haparokhet asher al-aron ha’edut lifne hakkapporet asher al-ha’edut asher iwwa’ed lekha shammah, “And you shall put it in front of the curtain that is over the ark of the testimony, in front of the mercy seat that is over the testimony, where I will meet with you.”

Hebrew (40:5): וְנָתַתָּה אֶת־מִזְבַּח הַזָּהָב לִקְטֹרֶת לִפְנֵי אֲרוֹן הָעֵדֻת — venatattah et-mizbach hazzahav liqtoret lifne aron ha’edut, “And you shall put the golden altar for incense in front of the ark of the testimony.”

Hebrew (40:26): וַיָּשֶׂם אֶת־מִזְבַּח הַזָּהָב בְּאֹהֶל מוֹעֵד לִפְנֵי הַפָּרֹכֶת — vayyasem et-mizbach hazzahav be’ohel mo’ed lifne haparokhet, “And he put the golden altar in the tent of meeting, in front of the curtain.”

Notice the double orientation of 30:6. Physically the altar stood “in front of the curtain,” on the outer side. But theologically it was oriented to what lay behind the curtain: “in front of the mercy seat,” “in front of the ark,” at the very place “where I will meet with you.”

Did the preacher, then, make a mistake? Some modern scholars have concluded so, suggesting he knew the tabernacle only from books. But that is unlikely in a writer who otherwise shows such precise knowledge of the Levitical texts, and several considerations, drawn from Scripture itself, show that his description is defensible:

Interpretive Options: The Golden Altar and the Inner Room

  • The altar “belonged to” the inner room. The preacher does not say that the altar was in the Holy of Holies. For the ark’s contents he uses “in which” (en hē), but for the altar he uses the participle “having” (echousa). The inner room “had” the altar in the sense that the altar was functionally bound to it. This is exactly how the Old Testament speaks of Solomon’s temple: “the whole altar that belonged to the inner sanctuary he overlaid with gold” (1 Kgs 6:22). The Hebrew there describes the altar as asher laddevir, “which [was] for/belonging to the inner sanctuary (devir),” though it stood outside it. Hebrews uses the same kind of language.
  • Its liturgical function was tied to the Most Holy Place. Exod 30:6 places it “in front of the mercy seat.” Its incense rose “before the Lord” (Exod 30:8), whose throne was the mercy seat. Even the incense compounded for it was to be placed “in front of the testimony in the tent of meeting” and was called “most holy” (Exod 30:36). And once a year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest made atonement on its horns with the blood of the sin offering, so that the altar itself is called קֹדֶשׁ־קָדָשִׁים הוּא לַיהוָה qodesh-qodashim hu la-YHWH, “it is most holy”—literally “a holy of holies”—“to the Lord” (Exod 30:10).
  • Its role on the Day of Atonement. On that day the high priest took coals from the altar and incense, carried them behind the curtain, and made a cloud of incense cover the mercy seat “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:12–13). The incense ministry thus physically crossed into the inner room on the very day the preacher is about to describe (9:7). Because Hebrews is interested in the Day of Atonement, it is natural for him to associate the incense with the inner sanctuary.
  • A censer, not the altar. Older interpreters who rendered “golden censer” (following the Latin Vulgate tradition) avoided the difficulty by understanding the firepan used on the Day of Atonement, which the Mishnah says was of gold on that day (m. Yoma 4:4). But Lev 16 does not say this firepan was kept in the inner room, and it seems unlikely the preacher would omit the incense altar from his inventory while naming a minor vessel. This view is possible but less probable.

The best conclusion is that the preacher is describing the golden altar as belonging to the Holy of Holies by its function and orientation, not by its physical location—precisely as 1 Kgs 6:22 does. His choice of words (“having” rather than “in which”) supports this. This was also a natural thing for a Jewish reader of Exodus to do, since Exod 30:6 itself locates the altar in relation to the ark and mercy seat. Interestingly, a tradition preserved in 2 Maccabees lists the tent, the ark, and the altar of incense together as the objects Jeremiah hid in a cave before the Babylonian destruction (2 Macc 2:4–5), showing that the incense altar was thought of as belonging closely with the ark.

The theological point should not be lost in the technical discussion. Incense in Scripture is an image of prayer: “Let my prayer be set before you as incense” (Ps 141:2); in Revelation the incense rising before God’s throne is joined with “the prayers of the saints” (Rev 8:3–4). The incense altar stood as close to God’s throne as anything in the outer room could, yet with a curtain in between. In Christ the curtain is passed, and his people now pray, not at a distance, but at “the throne of grace” itself (4:16).

2. “The ark of the covenant, covered on every side with gold.” The ark (κιβωτός kibōtos, “chest, box”; the same Greek word is used of Noah’s ark in 11:7) was a chest of acacia wood about two and a half cubits long and one and a half wide and high (roughly 45 × 27 × 27 inches), overlaid “with pure gold, inside and outside” (Exod 25:10–11). The preacher’s “covered on every side with gold” (περικεκαλυμμένην πάντοθεν χρυσίῳ perikekalymmenēn pantothen chrysiō) reproduces the Hebrew “from inside and from outside you shall overlay it.” The Hebrew calls it אֲרוֹן הָעֵדֻת aron ha’edut, “the ark of the testimony,” because it held “the testimony,” the tablets of the law (Exod 25:21–22). The preacher calls it “the ark of the covenant,” a name also used in the Old Testament (Num 10:33; Deut 10:8), and his choice is apt: the subject of chapters 8–9 is the covenant.

3. Inside the ark: three objects.

  • “A golden jar holding the manna.” Moses told Aaron, “Take one jar and put in it a full omer of manna, and set it before the Lord to be kept throughout your generations” (Exod 16:33). The Hebrew word for the jar, צִנְצֶנֶת tsintsenet, occurs only here, and the Hebrew does not say it was golden. The Greek Old Testament, however, renders “one golden jar” (using the same word for jar, στάμνος stamnos, that Hebrews uses), and Hebrews follows that Greek wording. Nothing is implausible about a golden vessel in the inner sanctuary, where everything was overlaid with gold. The manna testified to God’s faithful provision in the wilderness (Exod 16:35; Deut 8:3), a provision Jesus said pointed to himself, “the true bread from heaven” (John 6:31–35).
  • “Aaron’s rod that budded.” When Israel rebelled against Aaron’s priesthood (Num 16), God commanded each tribe to bring a staff; Aaron’s alone sprouted, budded, blossomed, and produced ripe almonds overnight (Num 17:8). Then the Lord said, הָשֵׁב אֶת־מַטֵּה אַהֲרֹן לִפְנֵי הָעֵדוּת לְמִשְׁמֶרֶת לְאוֹת לִבְנֵי־מֶרִי hashev et-matteh aharon lifne ha’edut lemishmeret le’ot livne-meri, “Put back Aaron’s rod before the testimony, to be kept as a sign for the rebels” (Num 17:25 in Hebrew numbering; 17:10 in English Bibles). The budding rod was God’s own confirmation of Aaron’s priesthood. Its presence in this list is poignant in a letter that has just argued that the priesthood God confirmed in Aaron has now been surpassed by the priesthood God confirmed with an oath in Christ (7:11–28). Christ’s priesthood was confirmed not by a dead stick coming to life but by his own resurrection, “the power of an indestructible life” (7:16).
  • “The tablets of the covenant.” These were the two stone tablets on which God wrote the Ten Words (Exod 31:18; 34:1, 28). Moses said, “I put the tablets in the ark that I had made, and there they are, as the Lord commanded me” (Deut 10:5). They were the heart of the Sinai covenant, which is why the ark is called “the ark of the covenant.”

Exodus and Numbers say that the jar and the rod were placed “before the testimony” (לִפְנֵי הָעֵדֻת lifne ha’edut, Exod 16:34; Num 17:25). Since “the testimony” is the name for the tablets inside the ark, the phrase can mean “beside the tablets,” that is, inside the ark, or “in front of the ark.” Hebrews takes it in the first sense, as did later Jewish tradition. 1 Kings 8:9 notes that by the time of Solomon’s temple “there was nothing in the ark except the two tablets of stone,” but that is a statement about the later temple, after the wanderings and the Philistine captivity of the ark (1 Sam 4–6); the remark may even imply that other objects had once been kept with them. Hebrews is describing the original tabernacle.

The three objects together form a striking set. Each is a memorial of Israel’s rebellion and God’s faithfulness: the manna of a people who grumbled for food (Exod 16:2–3), the rod that silenced those who grumbled against God’s chosen priest (Num 17:5, 10), and the tablets of the law given anew after Israel broke the first ones with the golden calf (Exod 32:19; 34:1). All lay beneath the mercy seat. The law that condemned and the reminders of Israel’s failures were covered by the place where atoning blood was sprinkled.

9:5

and above it were the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat. Of these things we cannot now speak in detail.

“Above it” refers to the ark. On top of the ark lay its cover, and at each end of the cover stood a golden cherub, hammered from the same piece of gold, with wings spread upward and faces turned toward each other and toward the cover (Exod 25:18–20). Cherubim in Scripture are majestic heavenly beings associated with God’s throne and the guarding of his holiness: they guarded the way to the tree of life (Gen 3:24), they are woven into the curtain (Exod 26:31), and Ezekiel saw them bearing the throne-chariot of God’s glory (Ezek 10). The Lord is repeatedly called the one “enthroned upon the cherubim” (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 80:1; 99:1). The ark was understood as his footstool and the space above the cherubim as his throne-room on earth.

The preacher calls them “cherubim of glory” (Χερουβὶν δόξης Cheroubin doxēs). The Greek word Cheroubin simply transliterates the Hebrew plural כְּרֻבִים keruvim. “Of glory” probably means “the cherubim associated with the Glory,” that is, with the visible manifestation of God’s presence, the radiant glory that filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35) and appeared above the mercy seat. In Hebrew, כָּבוֹד kavod, “glory,” is the regular word for this; when the ark was captured, Eli’s daughter-in-law named her son Ichabod, “The glory has departed from Israel” (1 Sam 4:21–22). The phrase connects with Hebrews’ opening description of the Son as “the radiance of [God’s] glory” (1:3).

The cherubim were “overshadowing” (κατασκιάζοντα kataskiazonta) the mercy seat. The verb picks up the Hebrew of Exod 25:20, סֹכְכִים בְּכַנְפֵיהֶם עַל־הַכַּפֹּרֶת sokhekhim bekhanfehem al-hakkapporet, “screening the mercy seat with their wings.” Their wings formed a canopy over the place where God met with Israel.

Word Study: ἱλαστήριον hilastērion — “mercy seat”

The Hebrew name of the ark’s cover is כַּפֹּרֶת kapporet: “You shall make a kapporet of pure gold, two and a half cubits its length and a cubit and a half its width” (Exod 25:17). The word is built from the same root as כִּפֶּר kipper, “to make atonement,” and יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים yom hakkippurim, “the Day of Atonement.” Though it was physically a lid, its name identifies it by its function: it was the place where atonement was made, where the high priest sprinkled blood once a year “on the mercy seat and in front of the mercy seat” (Lev 16:14–15).

The Greek Old Testament consistently translates kapporet with ἱλαστήριον hilastērion, a word related to ἱλάσκομαι hilaskomai, “to make propitiation, to atone” (the verb used of Christ’s high-priestly work in 2:17). A hilastērion is a “place” or “means” of propitiation. William Tyndale coined the English phrase “mercy seat,” and this study keeps it as the traditional name; but the reader should understand it as “the place of atonement.”

The only other New Testament use is Rom 3:25, where Paul says that God publicly set forth Christ Jesus as a hilastērion “through faith, in his blood.” What the golden cover was in the tabernacle—the place where God, enthroned in holiness, met with a sinful people on the basis of sacrificial blood—Christ is in reality. Hebrews makes the same point in its own way: Christ is both the high priest who enters and the sacrifice whose blood atones (9:12, 14).

“Of these things we cannot now speak in detail” (περὶ ὧν οὐκ ἔστιν νῦν λέγειν κατὰ μέρος peri hōn ouk estin nyn legein kata meros). The phrase kata meros, literally “according to part,” means “item by item, in detail.” οὐκ ἔστιν ouk estin, literally “it is not,” here means “it is not possible” or “this is not the place.” The preacher is a disciplined teacher. Jewish interpreters of his day, Philo above all, wrote at length on the symbolic meaning of each furnishing: the seven lamps as the planets, the cherubim as God’s two chief powers, and so on. The preacher deliberately declines to follow that path. His concern is not the symbolic value of each object but the single great lesson of the whole arrangement, which he is about to draw: there was a barrier between the people and God, and the old order could not remove it.

This restraint is itself instructive for readers of Scripture. Hebrews does not encourage us to find hidden meanings in every tent peg. It reads the tabernacle the way the Old Testament itself presents it—as the place where a holy God dwelt among a sinful people, approachable only on his terms, through a priest and through blood—and then shows how Christ fulfills that reality. The “now” (νῦν nyn) may also suggest that these things could be discussed in detail at another time; the preacher does not despise them. But the main point (8:1) must not be buried under details.
