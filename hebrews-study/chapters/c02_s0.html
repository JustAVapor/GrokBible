Hebrews 2

So Great a Salvation

Chapter 1 set the Son on the throne of the universe. He is the heir of all things, the one through whom God made the ages, the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of his essential being, the one who made purification for sins and sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high (1:2–3). A chain of seven Old Testament quotations showed that he is as far above the angels as a Son is above servants, and as the eternal Creator is above the “winds” and “flames of fire” he made (1:5–14). The chapter ended with a question that pointed forward: are not the angels all ministering spirits, sent out to serve for the sake of those who are about to inherit salvation (1:14)?

Chapter 2 takes up that word. Its title phrase comes from 2:3—“so great a salvation” (τηλικαύτης … σωτηρίας tēlikautēs … sōtērias)—and the whole chapter explains what that salvation is, how it reached the readers, and what it cost the one who won it. The chapter falls into two movements.

2:1–4: the first warning. Hebrews is a sermon that constantly turns from exposition to exhortation (see 13:22, “word of exhortation”). Here, for the first time, the preacher stops to apply what he has taught. If the word that came to Israel at Sinai “through angels” proved legally binding, so that every transgression received its just penalty, how much more dangerous is it to neglect the word that began with the Lord himself? The danger he names is not a sudden, defiant apostasy but a slow drift, like a boat slipping past its mooring. This is the first of five warning passages in the book (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:19–39; 12:14–29), and each of them repeats the same “lesser to greater” logic in sharper form. Verse 3 also gives us one of our few windows onto the author and his audience: they did not hear the Lord in person but received the message from those who had.

2:5–18: the Son made lower than the angels. Having exalted the Son above the angels in chapter 1, the author now shows the other side of the same truth: for a little while the Son was made lower than the angels. He does this by expounding Psalm 8, the psalm that marvels that God should remember frail humanity and crown it with glory and honor, setting all things under its feet. We do not yet see that destiny fulfilled in the human race—“but we see Jesus” (2:9). The personal name Ἰησοῦς Iēsous, “Jesus,” appears here for the first time in the book, precisely where his humanity, suffering, and death come into view. From there the author explains why it was “fitting” for God to bring “many sons to glory” by making the “pioneer” of their salvation perfect through sufferings (2:10); why the Son is not ashamed to call them “brothers” (2:11–13, with quotations from Psalm 22 and Isaiah 8); why he had to share their flesh and blood in order to break the devil’s power over death and free those enslaved by fear of it (2:14–16); and why he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest who makes propitiation for the sins of the people and helps those who are being tested (2:17–18).

Chapter 1 and chapter 2 belong together as a matched pair. Chapter 1 shows the Son as God speaks to him and about him: Creator, King, and eternal Lord. Chapter 2 shows the Son as one of us: a true man, tested, suffering, dying, and so crowned. Hebrews will not let either truth swallow the other. The one who sustains all things by his powerful word (1:3) is the same one who tasted death for everyone (2:9). Only such a person can be the high priest the rest of the book describes.

Key words to watch: angels (ἄγγελος angelos; five times in this chapter, then almost disappearing from the book until 12:22 and 13:2); salvation (σωτηρία sōtēria, 2:3, 10); spoken (λαλέω laleō, 2:2, 3, 5, picking up 1:1–2, “God spoke”); subject (ὑποτάσσω hypotassō, four times in 2:5–8); glory (2:7, 9, 10); sufferings and death (2:9, 10, 14, 15); brothers (2:11, 12, 17); and, at the very end, high priest (ἀρχιερεύς archiereus, 2:17), the title that will dominate chapters 4–10.

What to watch for: the way the author reads the Old Testament as words spoken by God and by the Son himself; the deliberate movement from “all things” put under humanity’s feet to the Son who shares humanity’s weakness; the joining of victory over the devil (2:14) with atonement for sin (2:17); and the pastoral aim of everything. The chapter ends not with a doctrine to be admired but with a person who is able to help those who are being tested (2:18).
