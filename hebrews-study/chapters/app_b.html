Appendix B

Key Greek Words in Hebrews

Hebrews is written in the most polished Greek of the New Testament, and its argument often turns on a single carefully chosen word. This glossary gathers sixty of the most important terms. Each is listed by its dictionary form (lemma), with a transliteration and the English rendering used throughout this study, and arranged alphabetically by transliteration. Occurrence counts refer to Hebrews alone and were computed from the SBL Greek New Testament with the MorphGNT morphological tagging; where a word occurs more than once, the references are given in full or in representative part. Where a word renders an important Hebrew term in the Septuagint (LXX), the Hebrew is noted.

ἀγών agōn — contest, race

Used once, at 12:1: “let us run with endurance the race (agōn) set before us.” The word named any athletic contest in the Greek games and, by extension, any hard struggle; together with the “cloud of witnesses,” the laying aside of every weight, and the call to run, it pictures the Christian life as a long race in the stadium. Hebrews uses kindred athletic language elsewhere: the readers once endured “a great contest (ἄθλησις athlēsis) of sufferings” (10:32), and they have not yet resisted to blood “in your struggle (ἀνταγωνίζομαι antagōnizomai) against sin” (12:4). Jewish writers had already described the endurance of the Maccabean martyrs as a contest (4 Macc 17:11–16).

αἰώνιος aiōnios — eternal

Six times, each attached to a gift of the new covenant: “eternal salvation” (5:9), “eternal judgment” (6:2), “eternal redemption” (9:12), “the eternal Spirit” (9:14), “the eternal inheritance” (9:15), and “the blood of the eternal covenant” (13:20). The word marks what belongs to God’s age to come and therefore does not wear out, in contrast with the repeated and temporary arrangements of the first covenant. The related noun αἰών aiōn, “age,” appears fourteen times, most often in the phrase “forever” (εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna) from Ps 110:4, where it renders Hebrew le’olam.

ἀνάμνησις anamnēsis — reminder, remembrance

Used once, at 10:3: in the yearly sacrifices “there is a reminder of sins every year.” The Day of Atonement, far from removing the consciousness of sin, called it to mind again each year. The contrast is with the promise of Jer 31:34, quoted twice in Hebrews: “their sins I will remember no more” (8:12; 10:17). The same word appears in Jesus’ words at the Supper, “do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19; 1 Cor 11:24–25): the old rite recalled sins; the new remembers the sacrifice that put them away.

ἄγκυρα ankyra — anchor

Used once, at 6:19: the hope set before us is “an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, and entering into the inner place behind the curtain.” The anchor was a common ancient image of stability in danger. Hebrews bends the image boldly: this anchor is not sunk in the seabed but fixed in the heavenly sanctuary itself, where Jesus has entered as forerunner (6:20). The believer’s security is therefore as firm as Christ’s own position before God.

ἀπαύγασμα apaugasma — radiance

Used once, at 1:3: the Son is “the radiance of [God’s] glory.” The word can be active, “radiance” (light streaming out from its source), or passive, “reflection” (light thrown back); most interpreters, ancient and modern, prefer the active sense, since the Son is not a mirror distinct from the light but the shining of the light itself. The Greek Fathers saw here the truth later confessed as “light from light.” The Wisdom of Solomon had called divine Wisdom “a radiance of eternal light” (Wis 7:26); Hebrews says of a Person what Jewish writers had said of a divine attribute, and it grounds the image in the Old Testament “glory” (Hebrew kavod) that filled the tabernacle.

ἄφεσις aphesis — forgiveness, release

Twice: “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22), and “where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:18). The basic sense is “release” or “cancellation” (of a debt or a charge). In the LXX it is the word for the “release” of the Jubilee year (Lev 25:10, where it renders Hebrew דְּרוֹר deror, “liberty”), so it carries overtones of liberation. In 10:18 it gathers up the new-covenant promise of Jer 31:34 and draws the decisive conclusion: forgiveness that is complete makes further sacrifice unnecessary.

ἀπιστία apistia — unbelief; ἀπείθεια apeitheia — disobedience

Each occurs twice, and Hebrews uses them of the same failure: the wilderness generation could not enter “because of unbelief” (3:19; cf. 3:12, “an evil heart of unbelief”), and they failed to enter “because of disobedience” (4:6, 11). The related verb ἀπειθέω apeitheō, “disobey,” appears at 3:18 and 11:31. The interchange is theologically significant: unbelief is not merely an intellectual lack but a refusal to obey God’s word, just as true faith (pistis) obeys and acts (11:8). Unbelief expresses itself in “falling away from the living God” (3:12).

ἀρχηγός archēgos — pioneer

Twice: God made “the pioneer of their salvation” perfect through sufferings (2:10), and believers fix their eyes on Jesus, “the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2). The word combines the ideas of leader, founder, and one who goes first to open a way that others then follow; it was used of heroes who founded cities and of military champions. Peter calls Jesus the archēgos of life (Acts 3:15) and “Leader and Savior” (Acts 5:31). In the LXX it is used of tribal and military leaders; strikingly, when Israel refused to enter the land, the people said, “Let us appoint a leader (archēgos) and return to Egypt” (Num 14:4) — the very episode Hebrews expounds in chapters 3–4. Jesus is the leader who goes forward into glory, not back.

ἀρχιερεύς archiereus — high priest

Seventeen times; Hebrews is the only New Testament book that calls Jesus high priest. The title first appears at 2:17 (“a merciful and faithful high priest”) and is developed in 4:14–5:10 and 7:1–10:18: Jesus is a “great high priest who has passed through the heavens” (4:14), appointed by God “according to the order of Melchizedek” (5:10; 6:20), “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26), seated at the right hand (8:1). The office corresponds to the Hebrew הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל hakkohen haggadol, “the great priest” (Lev 21:10), whose supreme duty was to enter the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16; Heb 9:7).

ἀσάλευτος asaleutos — unshakable

Used once, at 12:28: “since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be grateful.” It answers the quotation of Hag 2:6 in 12:26–27, where God promises to “shake” (σείω seiō) heaven and earth, so that the things that are shaken (σαλεύω saleuō) are removed and only what cannot be shaken remains. The kingdom recalls Dan 7:14, 18, 27, the everlasting kingdom given to the Son of Man and the saints. In a world where the readers had lost property and security (10:34), the word names the one possession beyond all upheaval.

χαρακτήρ charaktēr — exact imprint, exact representation

Used once, at 1:3: the Son is “the exact imprint of [God’s] essential being.” The word first meant an engraving tool, then the impression made by a die or seal, as on a coin, which reproduces every feature of the original. Paired with “radiance,” it teaches that the Son both shares the divine glory and perfectly represents the divine nature, so that whoever sees him sees the Father (John 14:9; cf. Col 1:15). The English word “character” comes from it.

διαθήκη diathēkē — covenant

Seventeen times in Hebrews — about half of all its New Testament occurrences. The LXX uses it to translate Hebrew בְּרִית berit, the binding relationship God establishes with his people by his own initiative and promise. Hebrews contrasts the first covenant made at Sinai (8:9; 9:1, 15, 18) with the “new” covenant promised in Jer 31:31–34 (8:8, 13; 9:15; 12:24), which is “better” (7:22; 8:6) and “eternal” (13:20), and of which Jesus is guarantor and mediator. In ordinary Greek diathēkē meant a last will and testament; at 9:16–17 the author appears to draw on that sense (a will takes effect only at death), though some interpreters keep “covenant” there as well (see the commentary).

εἰκών eikōn — image, very form

Used once, at 10:1: the law has “a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image of the realities.” The contrast is between a shadow (skia), an outline cast by something solid, and the eikōn, the true form or embodiment of the thing itself. The law pointed ahead to realities it could not itself contain; those realities have now arrived in Christ, whom Paul calls “the image of God” (2 Cor 4:4; Col 1:15).

ἔλεγχος elenchos — conviction, proof

Used once, in the definition of faith at 11:1: faith is “the conviction of things not seen.” The word belongs to the language of proof and cross-examination: it is the evidence that convinces, or the resulting state of being convinced. Faith is thus not a leap in the dark but a settled persuasion, based on God’s word, about realities that cannot be seen — God himself (11:27), the created order’s origin in his word (11:3), and the promised future (11:7). The related verb ἐλέγχω elenchō appears at 12:5, “when you are reproved by him.”

ἐλπίς elpis — hope

Five times (3:6; 6:11, 18; 7:19; 10:23). In Hebrews hope is not wishful thinking but confident expectation of what God has promised, grounded in his promise and oath (6:17–18). Believers must hold fast “the boast of our hope” (3:6) and “the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful” (10:23). Through the “better hope” introduced by Christ “we draw near to God” (7:19), and this hope is an anchor fixed in the heavenly sanctuary (6:19). Faith itself is defined as the assurance of things hoped for (11:1).

ἔγγυος engyos — guarantor, surety

Used once, at 7:22: “Jesus has become the guarantor of a better covenant.” It is a legal and commercial term for one who pledges himself or his property to ensure that an obligation is fulfilled. Judah’s offer to stand surety for Benjamin with his own life (Gen 43:9; 44:32) illustrates the idea. Because Jesus was installed by God’s oath (7:20–21) and “always lives” (7:25), the new covenant is guaranteed by the person of the Son himself.

ἐπαγγελία epangelia — promise

Fourteen times, and the related verb ἐπαγγέλλομαι epangellomai, “promise,” four times (6:13; 10:23; 11:11; 12:26). The promise of entering God’s rest still stands (4:1); Abraham obtained the promise after patient endurance (6:12–15), confirmed by God’s oath (6:17); the new covenant is “enacted on better promises” (8:6); and the call to the readers is to do God’s will and “receive the promise” (10:36). Chapter 11 shows the faithful living toward promises they saw only from afar (11:13, 39). The Hebrew Bible has no single noun for “promise”; the idea is expressed by God’s “word” and “oath,” and Hebrews draws heavily on both.

ἐφάπαξ ephapax — once for all; ἅπαξ hapax — once

Ephapax occurs three times (7:27; 9:12; 10:10) and hapax eight times (6:4; 9:7, 26, 27, 28; 10:2; 12:26, 27). The words stand against the repeated rhythms of the old order: priests offered “daily” (7:27; 10:11), the high priest entered “yearly” (9:7, 25; 10:1, 3), sacrifices were offered “often” (9:25; 10:11). Christ, by contrast, offered himself “once for all” (7:27), entered the holy places “once for all” (9:12), and sanctified his people “once for all” by the offering of his body (10:10). Just as human beings die “once” and then face judgment, so Christ was offered “once” and will appear a second time (9:27–28). In 12:26–27 the “yet once” of Hag 2:6 announces the single, final shaking.

ἁγιάζω hagiazō — sanctify

Seven times (2:11 twice; 9:13; 10:10, 14, 29; 13:12). In Hebrews the verb is chiefly cultic: to consecrate, to make holy so that one may approach the holy God. It corresponds to the Hebrew verb qadash (piel “consecrate,” as in Lev 25:10, וְקִדַּשְׁתֶּם veqiddashtem, “you shall consecrate”). Jesus is “the one who sanctifies” (2:11); “by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10); “by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14); and he suffered outside the gate “to sanctify the people through his own blood” (13:12). The noun ἁγιασμός hagiasmos appears at 12:14: the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.

αἷμα haima — blood

Twenty-one times, concentrated in chapters 9–10. The background is Lev 17:11: כִּי נֶפֶשׁ הַבָּשָׂר בַּדָּם הִוא ki nefesh habbasar baddam hi, “for the life of the flesh is in the blood,” and God has given it on the altar to make atonement. Blood signifies life given up in death. “Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22); Christ entered the holy places “by his own blood” (9:12); his blood purifies the conscience (9:14); believers have boldness to enter “by the blood of Jesus” (10:19); his “sprinkled blood speaks better than the blood of Abel” (12:24); and God raised him “by the blood of the eternal covenant” (13:20).

ἱλάσκομαι hilaskomai — make propitiation for; ἱλαστήριον hilastērion — mercy seat

Each occurs once. Christ became a merciful and faithful high priest “to make propitiation for the sins of the people” (2:17); above the ark were “the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat” (9:5). In the LXX hilastērion translates the כַפֹּרֶת kapporet (Exod 25:17), the golden cover of the ark on which the high priest sprinkled blood on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:14–15), from the same root as kipper, “make atonement” (לְכַפֵּר lekhapper, Lev 17:11). In Greek usage the verb meant to turn away wrath; in Scripture it is God himself who provides the means by which sin, the cause of his righteous wrath, is dealt with (Rom 3:25; 1 John 2:2). The cognate ἵλεως hileōs, “merciful,” appears in the Jer 31 quotation at 8:12.

ὁμολογία homologia — confession

Three times: Jesus is “the apostle and high priest of our confession” (3:1); “let us hold fast the confession” (4:14); “let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering” (10:23). The word denotes the church’s shared, public acknowledgment of faith in Jesus as Son of God and high priest — probably a known form of words confessed at baptism and in worship. The related verb ὁμολογέω homologeō describes the patriarchs’ confession that they were strangers on the earth (11:13) and the praise of “lips that confess his name” (13:15). To hold fast the confession is the opposite of drifting away (2:1).

ὅρκος horkos — oath; ὁρκωμοσία horkōmosia — oath-taking

Horkos occurs twice (6:16, 17), horkōmosia four times (7:20 twice, 21, 28), and the verb ὀμνύω omnyō, “swear,” seven times. Among human beings an oath ends every dispute (6:16); God, having no one greater to swear by, swore by himself to Abraham (Gen 22:16; Heb 6:13), so that by “two unchangeable things,” his promise and his oath, we might have strong encouragement (6:18). The same God swore in Ps 110:4, “The Lord has sworn and will not relent” — an oath the Levitical priests never received (7:20–21). “The word of the oath, which came after the law, appoints a Son, made perfect forever” (7:28). But God also swore in wrath that the unbelieving would not enter his rest (3:11, 18; 4:3).

ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma — copy; example

Three times. At 4:11 it means “example” — the example of disobedience set by the wilderness generation. At 8:5 and 9:23 it means “copy” or “sketch”: the priests serve “a copy and shadow of the heavenly things,” and “the copies of the things in the heavens” had to be purified with animal sacrifices. The earthly tabernacle was a true but provisional representation, built according to the pattern shown to Moses (Exod 25:40), of the heavenly reality into which Christ has entered.

ὑπομονή hypomonē — endurance

Twice: “you have need of endurance” (10:36) and “let us run with endurance the race” (12:1). The verb ὑπομένω hypomenō, “endure,” appears four times: the readers once “endured a great contest of sufferings” (10:32); Jesus “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2) and “endured such hostility from sinners” (12:3); and believers must “endure for discipline” (12:7). The word means remaining steadfast under a heavy load — not passive resignation but active, hopeful perseverance. Jesus’ endurance is both the pattern and the source of theirs.

ὑπόστασις hypostasis — essential being; confidence; assurance

Three times, with three related senses. Literally “that which stands underneath,” it can mean the underlying reality or substance of a thing, its foundation, or a firm confidence. At 1:3 the Son is the exact imprint of God’s “essential being” — what God really is (later theology would use the word for the distinct “persons” of the Trinity, but that is not its sense here). At 3:14 believers must hold “the beginning of our confidence” firm to the end. At 11:1 faith is “the assurance of things hoped for”: faith gives present substance and firmness to what is still future. In the LXX the word sometimes renders words for “hope” or “expectation” (e.g., Ps 39:7 [LXX 38:8]).

ὑποστολή hypostolē — shrinking back

Used once, at 10:39: “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul.” It answers the verb ὑποστέλλω hypostellō in the quotation of Hab 2:4 just before: “if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him” (10:38). The word describes drawing back in fear, timidly withdrawing from a commitment. It is the opposite of the parrēsia, “boldness,” the readers must not throw away (10:35), and it introduces the gallery of those who did not shrink back (chapter 11).

κατανοέω katanoeō — consider attentively

Twice: “consider Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession” (3:1), and “let us consider one another to stir up love and good works” (10:24). The verb means to fix one’s mind on something, to observe it carefully and think it through. The remedy for drifting (2:1) and sluggishness (5:11) is sustained attention — first to Jesus, then to one another. Hebrews uses similar verbs at the climax of its exhortation: “looking away to (ἀφοράω aphoraō) Jesus” (12:2) and “consider (ἀναλογίζομαι analogizomai) him who endured” (12:3).

κατάπαυσις katapausis — rest

Eight times, all in 3:11–4:11, and the related verb καταπαύω katapauō, “rest,” three times (4:4, 8, 10). The word comes from Ps 95:11, “They shall not enter my rest,” where it renders Hebrew מְנוּחָתִי menuchati, “my resting place,” a term used of the promised land (Deut 12:9). By linking Ps 95 with Gen 2:2 (“God rested on the seventh day”), Hebrews shows that the rest God offers is his own Sabbath rest, which Joshua did not give (4:8), which still remains “today,” and which believers enter by faith (4:3) and must strive to enter (4:11).

καταπέτασμα katapetasma — curtain

Three times (6:19; 9:3; 10:20). In the LXX it is the usual word for the paroket, the woven curtain that separated the holy place from the Most Holy Place (Exod 26:31–33); the high priest alone entered “inside the curtain” (מִבֵּית לַפָּרֹכֶת mibbet lapparokhet, Lev 16:2), once a year. Hebrews speaks of the hope that enters “within the curtain” where Jesus has gone as forerunner (6:19–20), of the second curtain in the tabernacle (9:3), and of “the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh” (10:20). Compare the tearing of the temple curtain at Jesus’ death (Mark 15:38).

καθαρισμός katharismos — purification, cleansing

Used once, at 1:3: “having made purification for sins, he sat down.” The verb καθαρίζω katharizō, “cleanse,” appears four times (9:14, 22, 23; 10:2): the blood of Christ will “cleanse our conscience from dead works” (9:14). The language is that of the Day of Atonement, “for on this day atonement shall be made for you, to cleanse you” (לְטַהֵר אֶתְכֶם מִכֹּל חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם letaher etkhem mikkol chattoteikhem, “to cleanse you from all your sins,” Lev 16:30). In 1:3 the aorist participle presents the purification as complete before the Son took his seat — the theme of the whole central section in a single phrase.

καθίζω kathizō — sit down; κάθημαι kathēmai — be seated

Kathizō occurs four times, always of Christ taking his seat at God’s right hand (1:3; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2); kathēmai occurs once, in the quotation of Ps 110:1, “Sit at my right hand” (1:13). The “session” at the right hand (the adjective δεξιός dexios, “right,” appears five times) signifies two things: the completion of his sacrifice — the Levitical priests “stand” daily, but he “sat down” after a single offering (10:11–12) — and his enthronement in supreme authority, awaiting the subjection of his enemies (10:13).

κεφάλαιον kephalaion — main point

Used once, at 8:1: “Now the main point in what we are saying is this: we have such a high priest, who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens.” The word could mean a summary, but here it means the chief point, the crown of the argument. The author pauses at the center of his sermon to tell his hearers what matters most: not merely that Christ is a superior priest, but that he is a seated priest, ministering in the true tabernacle.

κληρονόμος klēronomos — heir; κληρονομία klēronomia — inheritance

Klēronomos occurs three times (1:2; 6:17; 11:7), klēronomia twice (9:15; 11:8), and the verb κληρονομέω klēronomeō, “inherit,” four times (1:4, 14; 6:12; 12:17). God appointed the Son “heir of all things” (1:2; cf. Ps 2:8), and he “has inherited” a name better than the angels (1:4). Believers are “heirs of the promise” (6:17) who “inherit the promises” through faith and patience (6:12) and receive “the eternal inheritance” (9:15). Esau, by contrast, despised his birthright and later could not inherit the blessing (12:16–17). In the LXX the noun often renders Hebrew nachalah, the allotted inheritance of the land (הַנַּחֲלָה hannachalah, Deut 12:9).

κρείττων kreittōn — better

Thirteen times in Hebrews — more than in any other New Testament book — and the signature word of its argument. The Son is “better than the angels” (1:4); the readers are persuaded of “better things” concerning them (6:9); the lesser is blessed by “the better” (7:7); Christ brings “a better hope” (7:19), is guarantor of “a better covenant” (7:22), and mediates “a better covenant, enacted on better promises” (8:6); heavenly things require “better sacrifices” (9:23); believers have “a better possession” (10:34) and desire “a better country” (11:16); martyrs sought “a better resurrection” (11:35); God provided “something better” for us (11:40); and Jesus’ sprinkled blood “speaks better” than Abel’s (12:24). “Better” does not mean that the old was bad, but that it was provisional and has been surpassed by its fulfillment.

λειτουργός leitourgos — minister; λειτουργία leitourgia — ministry, service

Leitourgos occurs twice: angels are God’s “ministers” (1:7, from Ps 104:4, where it renders Hebrew מְשָׁרְתָיו mesharetav, “his ministers”), and Christ is “a minister of the holy places and of the true tabernacle” (8:2). Leitourgia occurs twice: Christ has obtained “a more excellent ministry” (8:6), and Moses sprinkled “the vessels of the ministry” (9:21). Related words are λειτουργέω leitourgeō, “minister” (10:11), and λειτουργικός leitourgikos, “ministering” (1:14). In Greek civic life a leitourgia was a public service; in the LXX the word family became the standard language for priestly and Levitical service in the sanctuary.

λύτρωσις lytrōsis — redemption

Used once, at 9:12: Christ entered the holy places “by his own blood, having obtained eternal redemption.” The word means release secured by the payment of a price (a lytron, “ransom”; cf. Mark 10:45), and it recalls Israel’s redemption from Egypt. The compound ἀπολύτρωσις apolytrōsis appears at 9:15 (“redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant”) and 11:35 (martyrs who refused “release”). Christ’s redemption is “eternal” because it rests on a single sacrifice never to be repeated.

μάρτυς martys — witness; μαρτυρέω martyreō — testify, attest

Martys occurs twice: at 10:28 (death “on the testimony of two or three witnesses,” Deut 17:6) and at 12:1 (“so great a cloud of witnesses”). Martyreō occurs eight times: Scripture “testifies” that Melchizedek lives (7:8) and that Christ is a priest forever (7:17), the Holy Spirit “testifies to us” (10:15), and the people of faith “were attested” or “commended” by God (11:2, 4, 5, 39). The heroes of chapter 11, who received God’s testimony, become in 12:1 witnesses who testify by their lives to the faithfulness of God — not merely spectators in the stands, though the athletic picture suggests that too.

μεσίτης mesitēs — mediator

Three times, always of Jesus as “mediator of a better covenant” (8:6), “of a new covenant” (9:15; 12:24). A mediator stands between two parties to establish, secure, or guarantee an agreement. Moses had mediated the covenant at Sinai (cf. Gal 3:19); Jesus mediates the new covenant not merely by transmitting its terms but by his death, which redeems from transgressions under the first covenant (9:15). Compare 1 Tim 2:5, “one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus.”

μέτοχος metochos — partner, sharer

Five times (1:9; 3:1, 14; 6:4; 12:8). In ordinary Greek it could mean a business partner (as in Luke 5:7). In 1:9 (Ps 45:7) it refers to the anointed king’s “companions”; then Hebrews applies it to believers: “partners in a heavenly calling” (3:1), “partners of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end” (3:14), those who have become “partakers of the Holy Spirit” (6:4), and those who share in the Father’s discipline (12:8). The related verb μετέχω metechō describes the Son’s sharing in our “flesh and blood” (2:14): he shared our nature so that we might share his.

νωθρός nōthros — sluggish

Twice, forming the frame of the warning in 5:11–6:12: “you have become sluggish in hearing” (5:11), and “that you may not become sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12). The word describes dullness, torpor, a lazy slowness of mind. The readers’ problem was not lack of ability but a spiritual drowsiness that had stunted their growth; its cure is “earnestness” (σπουδή spoudē) to the end (6:11).

ὀνειδισμός oneidismos — reproach

Three times: the readers were once “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:33); Moses “considered the reproach of the Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt” (11:26); and believers are called to go out to Jesus “outside the camp, bearing his reproach” (13:13). In an honor-and-shame culture, public insult and loss of standing were among the heaviest pressures a community could apply. Hebrews redefines honor: the shame borne for Christ is true wealth. The language echoes the Psalms, where the Lord’s anointed and his servant bear reproach (Ps 69:9; 89:50–51).

παιδεία paideia — discipline

Four times in 12:5–11, with the verb παιδεύω paideuō, “discipline” (12:6, 7, 10), and παιδευτής paideutēs, “one who disciplines” (12:9). In Greek culture paideia was the whole formation of a child — education, training, and correction. Hebrews takes the word from Prov 3:11 (LXX), where it renders Hebrew מוּסַר musar, “discipline, instruction.” The readers’ hardships are not signs of God’s rejection but proof of their sonship, for God disciplines “for our good, that we may share his holiness” (12:10), and it yields “the peaceful fruit of righteousness” (12:11).

παράκλησις paraklēsis — exhortation, encouragement

Three times: God’s oath gives “strong encouragement” to those who have fled for refuge (6:18); the readers have forgotten “the exhortation” of Prov 3:11–12 (12:5); and the author calls his whole work “the word of exhortation” (13:22), the same phrase used of a synagogue sermon in Acts 13:15. The verb παρακαλέω parakaleō appears four times, including the call to “exhort one another every day, as long as it is called ‘Today’” (3:13; cf. 10:25). The word unites comfort and urging: to strengthen someone by calling them forward.

παρρησία parrēsia — boldness

Four times (3:6; 4:16; 10:19, 35). In Greek civic life parrēsia was the free citizen’s right to speak openly in the assembly. In Hebrews it is both the confident access to God that Christ has won — “let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16); “we have boldness to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus” (10:19) — and the open, unashamed confession that must be held fast (3:6). “Do not throw away your boldness, which has a great reward” (10:35).

πίστις pistis — faith

Thirty-two times, twenty-four of them in chapter 11. Faith in Hebrews is defined as “the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1); without it “it is impossible to please God,” for the one who draws near must believe “that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him” (11:6). It is forward-looking trust in God’s promises that acts in obedience and endures under trial, grounded in the faithfulness of the God “who promised” (10:23; 11:11). Its background is Hab 2:4, “my righteous one will live by faith” (10:38), where Hebrew emunah (בֶּאֱמוּנָתוֹ be’emunato, “by his faithfulness”) denotes steadfast reliance. Jesus is “the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2).

πρόδρομος prodromos — forerunner

Used once, at 6:20: Jesus has entered within the curtain “as a forerunner on our behalf.” The word was used of one who runs ahead, such as a scout or the advance party of an army; it implies that others will follow where the forerunner has gone. On the Day of Atonement the high priest entered alone and came out again; Jesus has entered the heavenly sanctuary “on our behalf,” to open the way for his people to follow him into the presence of God.

προσέρχομαι proserchomai — draw near

Seven times (4:16; 7:25; 10:1, 22; 11:6; 12:18, 22). In the LXX the verb is used of priests and worshipers approaching God and his altar. Under the old covenant, sacrifices could not perfect “those who draw near” (10:1); now Christ is able to save completely “those who draw near to God through him” (7:25). Hence the two great exhortations: “let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (4:16) and “let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith” (10:22). The church has not “come” to terrifying Sinai, but to Mount Zion (12:18, 22). Compare ἐγγίζω engizō, “draw near” (7:19).

προσφορά prosphora — offering

Five times, all in chapter 10 (10:5, 8, 10, 14, 18); the related verb προσφέρω prospherō, “offer,” occurs twenty times throughout the book. In the quotation of Ps 40 (10:5, 8) it represents Hebrew וּמִנְחָה uminchah, “and grain offering,” standing for the whole system of offerings. The chapter then speaks of “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10), “a single offering” by which he has perfected the sanctified (10:14), and concludes: where there is forgiveness, “there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:18).

πρωτότοκος prōtotokos — firstborn

Three times: God brings “the firstborn” into the world (1:6); the destroyer of the firstborn in Egypt (11:28); and “the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven” (12:23). The term speaks of rank and inheritance rather than biological origin. Israel was God’s firstborn son (Exod 4:22, בְּנִי בְכֹרִי beni vekhori, “my son, my firstborn”), and the Davidic king was to be made “firstborn, highest of the kings of the earth” (Ps 89:27). Those united to the Son share the status of firstborn heirs — in contrast to Esau, who sold his birthright (πρωτοτόκια prōtotokia, 12:16).

σαββατισμός sabbatismos — Sabbath-rest

Used once, at 4:9: “So then, there remains a Sabbath-rest for the people of God.” The word is rare; it does not occur in the LXX and is formed from the verb “to keep Sabbath” (sabbatizō, as in LXX Exod 16:30). By changing from katapausis to sabbatismos, the author makes explicit that the rest offered is a share in God’s own seventh-day rest (Gen 2:2), and the “-ismos” form suggests the keeping or celebration of Sabbath: rest filled with festive worship. Verse 10 explains it: whoever enters God’s rest rests from his own works as God did from his.

σήμερον sēmeron — today

Eight times. Twice it is the “today” of the Son’s enthronement in Ps 2:7 (1:5; 5:5); five times it is the “today” of Ps 95:7 (3:7, 13, 15; 4:7 twice), where it renders Hebrew הַיּוֹם hayyom. Hebrews treats this “today” as still open: God “again sets a certain day, ‘Today,’” long after Joshua (4:7), and believers must exhort one another “every day, as long as it is called ‘Today’” (3:13). The word’s final appearance gives the ground of every “today”: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever” (13:8).

σκηνή skēnē — tabernacle, tent

Ten times. Hebrews always speaks of the wilderness tabernacle, never of the Jerusalem temple. The word renders Hebrew mishkan, “dwelling” (תַּבְנִית הַמִּשְׁכָּן tavnit hammishkan, “the pattern of the tabernacle,” Exod 25:9), and ohel, “tent.” Christ ministers in “the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched, not man” (8:2), “the greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands” (9:11). The earthly tent had a “first” and a “second” compartment (9:2–3, 6–8), and while “the first tent” was still standing, the way into the holy places was not yet disclosed (9:8). Abraham lived “in tents” as a pilgrim (11:9), and those who still serve the earthly tabernacle have no right to eat from the Christian altar (13:10).

σκιά skia — shadow

Twice: the priests serve “a copy and shadow of the heavenly things” (8:5), and the law has “a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image of the realities” (10:1). A shadow is real — it is cast by something solid and shows its outline — but it is not the substance. Hebrews’ contrast is not a philosophical disdain for the material world but a contrast between promise and fulfillment: the shadow is of good things “to come,” and they have come in Christ (9:11; cf. Col 2:17).

σωτηρία sōtēria — salvation

Seven times (1:14; 2:3, 10; 5:9; 6:9; 9:28; 11:7). The emphasis falls on salvation as a future inheritance: angels serve “those who are about to inherit salvation” (1:14), and Christ “will appear a second time … for salvation to those who eagerly await him” (9:28). Yet it was first declared by the Lord himself and confirmed to the readers (2:3), so neglecting “such a great salvation” is perilous. Jesus is the pioneer (2:10) and the “source of eternal salvation to all who obey him” (5:9). In the LXX the word often renders Hebrew yeshu’ah (יְשׁוּעָתִי yeshu’ati, “my salvation,” Isa 12:2).

συνείδησις syneidēsis — conscience

Five times (9:9, 14; 10:2, 22; 13:18). The conscience is the inner awareness of one’s guilt or innocence before God, and an accusing conscience is the barrier to drawing near to him. The old sacrifices “could not perfect the worshiper with respect to conscience” (9:9), and had they really cleansed, the worshipers would “no longer have any consciousness of sins” (10:2). But “the blood of Christ … will cleanse our conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14), so that we draw near “with hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience” (10:22). The author himself has “a good conscience” (13:18).

συντέλεια synteleia — consummation, completion

Used once, at 9:26: “now, once for all, at the consummation of the ages he has appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself.” The word denotes the bringing of something to its appointed end; the phrase matches “the end of the age” in Jesus’ teaching (Matt 13:39–40; 28:20). With Christ’s coming the ages have reached their goal; the readers live “in these last days” (1:2). The related verb συντελέω synteleō appears in the Jeremiah quotation at 8:8: God will “bring to completion” a new covenant.

τελειόω teleioō — make perfect, bring to perfection; τέλειος teleios — mature, perfect; τελειωτής teleiōtēs — perfecter

The verb occurs nine times (2:10; 5:9; 7:19, 28; 9:9; 10:1, 14; 11:40; 12:23), the adjective twice (“the mature,” 5:14; “the more perfect tabernacle,” 9:11), the noun τελείωσις teleiōsis, “perfection,” once (7:11), and teleiōtēs once, in “the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2). “Perfection” means bringing someone or something to its intended goal. Of Christ, it is not moral improvement — he was without sin (4:15) — but his full qualification as Savior and high priest through suffering, obedience, death, and exaltation (2:10; 5:9; 7:28). Of believers, it is the cleansed conscience and full access to God that the law could not give (7:19; 9:9; 10:1) but Christ’s single offering has accomplished (10:14), consummated in glory (11:40; 12:23). The LXX uses teleioō in the idiom for the consecration of priests, rendering Hebrew “fill the hand” (וּמִלֵּאתָ יַד־אַהֲרֹן umille’ta yad-Aharon, Exod 29:9; cf. Lev 16:32), a background that suits Hebrews’ priestly use of the word.

θυσία thysia — sacrifice

Fifteen times. The repeated sacrifices of the old covenant could not take away sins (10:1–4, 11), but Christ “appeared once for all … to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself” (9:26) and “offered one sacrifice for sins for all time” (10:12). In the Ps 40 quotation it represents Hebrew זֶבַח zevach, the slaughtered offering (10:5). Abel offered “a better sacrifice” than Cain (11:4). Under the new covenant God’s people still offer sacrifices, but of a new kind: “a sacrifice of praise,” and doing good and sharing, “for with such sacrifices God is pleased” (13:15–16).

τύπος typos — pattern

Used once, at 8:5, in the quotation of Exod 25:40: Moses was to make everything “according to the pattern shown you on the mountain.” The word means the impression left by a blow, a mold, a model, or a design; here it renders Hebrew tavnit, “pattern, plan” (בְּתַבְנִיתָם betavnitam, “according to their pattern”). Related is ἀντίτυπος antitypos at 9:24: Christ did not enter holy places made with hands, “copies of the true things,” but heaven itself. In Hebrews’ usage the heavenly reality is the original and the earthly sanctuary the copy — the reverse of the later Christian habit of calling the Old Testament figure the “type” and its fulfillment the “antitype.”
