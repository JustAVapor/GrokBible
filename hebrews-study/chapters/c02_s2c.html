2:14

Since, then, the children share in blood and flesh, he himself likewise partook of the same things, so that through death he might render powerless the one who has the power of death—that is, the devil—

“Since, then” (Ἐπεὶ οὖν Epei oun) draws a conclusion from the last quotation. The Son stands before God with “the children” (2:13). But what are these children like? They are creatures of “blood and flesh.” If he is to stand with them, he must become what they are.

The two verbs in the first half of the verse are carefully chosen and carefully contrasted.

  • The children “share” (κεκοινώνηκεν kekoinōnēken, from κοινωνέω koinōneō, “to have in common, to share”) in blood and flesh. The perfect tense describes their settled condition: they have always been, and remain, creatures of flesh and blood. It is their nature by birth.
  • The Son “partook” (μετέσχεν meteschen, from μετέχω metechō, “to take a share in, to partake of”) of the same things. The aorist tense points to a definite event in time: the incarnation. Flesh and blood were not his by origin; he took them on. The eternal Son, who existed before all things (1:2–3), at a particular moment entered human existence.

The phrase “blood and flesh” (αἵματος καὶ σαρκός haimatos kai sarkos) is a biblical expression for human nature in its frailty, mortality, and creatureliness, as distinct from God and from spiritual beings. The more common order is “flesh and blood” (Matt 16:17; 1 Cor 15:50; Gal 1:16), but the reverse order appears also in Eph 6:12. Sirach uses the phrase to describe the passing of human generations: like leaves on a tree, some fall and others grow, “so is the generation of flesh and blood: one dies and another is born” (Sir 14:18). Flesh and blood means weakness and death. That is exactly why the author uses it here. The Son took on a nature that could suffer and die.

The adverb παραπλησίως paraplēsiōs, “likewise, in just the same way,” stresses the reality of his human nature. It was not an appearance or a costume. He shared “the same things” (τῶν αὐτῶν tōn autōn) that the children have. Early in the church’s history some teachers (later called Docetists, from the Greek dokeō, “to seem”) claimed that Christ only seemed to have a body and only seemed to suffer. Hebrews leaves no room for such a view. The Son shared real blood and real flesh. Verse 17 will add that he was made like his brothers “in every respect,” and 4:15 will add the one exception: “yet without sin.” His human nature was real and complete, but it was not sinful. Sin is not essential to humanity; it is humanity’s corruption.

Why did he do this? The purpose clause (ἵνα hina, “so that”) gives the answer: “so that through death he might render powerless the one who has the power of death.” The phrase “through death” (διὰ τοῦ θανάτου dia tou thanatou) is placed first for emphasis. Here lies the great irony of the gospel. The Son became mortal in order to die, and by dying he defeated death’s master. He used death, the enemy’s own weapon, to destroy the enemy’s power. The early church loved to celebrate this paradox: death was swallowed up by the one it tried to swallow. The prophet had promised that the Lord would do this: בִּלַּע הַמָּוֶת לָנֶצַח billa‘ hammavet lanetsach, “he will swallow up death forever” (Isa 25:8; quoted in 1 Cor 15:54).

Word Study: καταργήσῃ katargēsē, “render powerless”

The verb καταργέω katargeō is built on argos, “idle, inactive, ineffective.” It means “to make ineffective, to nullify, to render inoperative, to put out of action.” Paul uses it often: God chose the things that are not “to bring to nothing” the things that are (1 Cor 1:28); Christ will hand over the kingdom after he has “destroyed” every rule and authority, and “the last enemy to be destroyed is death” (1 Cor 15:24, 26); Christ Jesus “abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel” (2 Tim 1:10).

The word does not necessarily mean “annihilate.” The devil still exists and still prowls (1 Pet 5:8). But his power over death—his ability to hold the children in its grip—has been broken. He has been disarmed. Like a defeated army that still has soldiers in the field but has lost the war, he can still harm, but he cannot finally hold those whom Christ has set free. Paul uses the same image of disarming: God “disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them, triumphing over them in him” (Col 2:15).

The one rendered powerless is “the one who has the power of death” (τὸν τὸ κράτος ἔχοντα τοῦ θανάτου ton to kratos echonta tou thanatou). κράτος kratos means “might, dominion, ruling power.” The author then names him explicitly: “that is, the devil” (τὸν διάβολον ton diabolon). The Greek word diabolos means “slanderer, accuser.” It translates the Hebrew שָׂטָן satan, “adversary, accuser,” in the Greek Old Testament (for example, Job 1:6–12; Zech 3:1–2).

In what sense does the devil have the “power of death”? Scripture is clear that ultimate authority over life and death belongs to God alone: אֲנִי אָמִית וַאֲחַיֶּה ’ani ’amit va’achayyeh, “I put to death and I make alive” (Deut 32:39). The risen Christ holds “the keys of Death and Hades” (Rev 1:18). The devil’s power over death is therefore not sovereign but derived and usurped. It operates through sin. Death entered the world as the penalty for sin (מוֹת תָּמוּת mot tamut, “you shall surely die,” Gen 2:17; Rom 5:12; 6:23), and the devil was the tempter who led humanity into that sin (Gen 3:1–6). Jesus called him “a murderer from the beginning” (John 8:44). The Wisdom of Solomon says that “through the envy of the devil death entered the world” (Wis 2:24). And as the accuser, he presses the just claim of the law against sinners, holding them under the sentence of death (compare Zech 3:1; Rev 12:10). As Paul puts it, “the sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law” (1 Cor 15:56).

This explains how Jesus’ death breaks the devil’s power. The devil’s hold on humanity rests on sin and its just penalty. When Christ dies as the sacrifice that makes propitiation for sins (2:17), the ground of the accuser’s claim is removed. Sin is forgiven; the penalty is borne; the sentence is satisfied. The devil has nothing left to hold. Paul makes the same connection: God forgave our trespasses, “canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands,” nailing it to the cross, and thereby disarmed the rulers and authorities (Col 2:13–15). Hebrews holds together two pictures of the cross that are sometimes set against each other: the cross as Christ’s victory over the devil (2:14–15) and the cross as Christ’s atoning sacrifice for sin (2:17). They are not rival theories. The victory is won by means of the atonement. The tyrant is overthrown because the debt is paid.

Readers who know the whole of Scripture will hear an echo of the first promise of the gospel. After the fall, God said to the serpent: “I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed; he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel” (Gen 3:15). The seed of the woman would be wounded, but in the wounding he would crush the serpent. The author of Hebrews does not quote Genesis 3:15, but the pattern is the same: through his own death the Son crushes the one who held the power of death. In 2:16 the author will speak of “the seed of Abraham,” the line through which that promised seed would come.

2:15

and set free all those who through fear of death were held in slavery all their lives.

The second purpose of the incarnation and death of Christ is the liberation of those whom the devil held. The verb ἀπαλλάξῃ apallaxē, from ἀπαλλάσσω apallassō, means “to set free, to release, to deliver.” In ordinary Greek it was used of release from a legal obligation, a debt, a disease, or bondage. Luke uses it of being released from an opponent in court (Luke 12:58). The aorist tense presents the release as a decisive act accomplished through Christ’s death.

Those set free are “all those who” (ὅσοι hosoi, “as many as”) were in bondage. The Greek text uses a demonstrative and a relative together, “these … as many as,” which stresses that the liberation reaches every one of the children, without exception.

Their condition is described in three phrases.

  • “Through fear of death” (φόβῳ θανάτου phobō thanatou). The dative case indicates the means or cause: it was fear of death that held them captive. The devil’s power over death (2:14) worked on them through their dread of it.
  • “All their lives” (διὰ παντὸς τοῦ ζῆν dia pantos tou zēn, literally “through the whole of living”). The articular infinitive “the living” describes life as an ongoing activity. Fear of death is not an occasional anxiety; it shadows every moment of a life lived without hope.
  • “Were held in slavery” (ἔνοχοι ἦσαν δουλείας enochoi ēsan douleias). The adjective ἔνοχος enochos means “held in, subject to, liable to.” It is often a legal word: “liable to judgment” (Matt 5:21–22), “guilty of death” (Matt 26:66). These people were bound over to slavery, legally subject to it. The imperfect tense “were” describes their condition before Christ set them free.

Historical Background: The Fear of Death in the Ancient World

The fear of death was a constant theme of ancient thought. Greek and Roman philosophers tried to free their followers from it. Epicurus taught that “death is nothing to us,” since where death is, we are not; the Roman poet Lucretius wrote his great poem On the Nature of Things largely to liberate his readers from the terror of death and the gods. The Stoic Seneca, a contemporary of the readers of Hebrews and himself a resident of Rome, wrote that the one who has learned to die has unlearned how to be a slave (Epistles 26.10). The philosophers recognized the connection that Hebrews makes: fear of death enslaves. A person terrified of death can be controlled by anyone who threatens his life, and will compromise anything to preserve it.

The philosophers’ cures, however, were thin. They offered either the claim that death is simply the end and therefore nothing to fear, or the counsel of resignation to fate. Jewish writers felt the bitterness of death keenly: “O death, how bitter is the remembrance of you to one who lives at peace among his possessions” (Sir 41:1). The Old Testament contains bright glimpses of hope beyond death, such as אַךְ־אֱלֹהִים יִפְדֶּה נַפְשִׁי מִיַּד־שְׁאוֹל כִּי יִקָּחֵנִי ’akh-’Elohim yiphdeh naphshi miyyad-she’ol ki yiqqacheni, “But God will ransom my life from the hand of Sheol, for he will receive me” (Ps 49:15; Hebrew 49:16). But only with the resurrection of Christ did the full answer come.

Hebrews does not tell people to stop fearing death by convincing themselves it is nothing. Death is real, and it is an enemy, bound up with sin and judgment (9:27, “it is appointed for human beings to die once, and after that comes judgment”). The fear of death is, in that sense, rational for a sinner. What Christ does is remove the cause of the fear. He has dealt with sin, so that death is no longer the doorway to condemnation. He has gone through death and come out the other side, so that death is no longer the end. He has become the pioneer who leads his people through death to glory (2:10). Paul exults in the same victory: “O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting? … But thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Cor 15:55, 57).

This verse had pressing relevance for the first readers. They had already endured persecution (10:32–34), and the author warns them that they have “not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood” (12:4). The threat of death—or of losing everything that makes life secure—was a real pressure pushing them to drift away and compromise their confession. The author reminds them that Christ has set them free from that slavery. They need not be ruled by fear. He will later quote Psalm 118: “The Lord is my helper; I will not be afraid; what can man do to me?” (13:6; Hebrew Ps 118:6, יְהוָה לִי לֹא אִירָא מַה־יַּעֲשֶׂה לִי אָדָם YHWH li lo’ ’ira’ mah-ya‘aseh li ’adam, “The Lord is for me; I will not fear; what can man do to me?”). In chapter 11 he will celebrate those who “were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might obtain a better resurrection” (11:35). Freedom from the fear of death is the freedom to be faithful.

It is worth noticing that Jesus himself did not face death with Stoic indifference. Hebrews tells us that “in the days of his flesh, he offered up prayers and supplications with loud cries and tears to the one who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence” (5:7). He felt the full horror of death. He did not conquer death by pretending it was nothing, but by going through it in obedient trust. The freedom he gives is not the freedom of denial but the freedom of hope.

2:16

For surely it is not angels that he takes hold of; rather, he takes hold of the seed of Abraham.

For the last time in this section the author mentions angels, closing the frame he opened in 2:5 (“not to angels”). The verse explains (“for,” γάρ gar) why the Son had to share flesh and blood: those he came to help are not angels but human beings.

The little word δήπου dēpou, found only here in the New Testament, means “surely, of course, as everyone knows.” The author appeals to something his readers will readily grant. Obviously the Son’s saving work is not directed toward angels. Chapter 1 described angels as servants sent to minister to the heirs of salvation (1:14); they are helpers, not the ones being helped. Scripture nowhere speaks of a redemption for angels; those angels who sinned are held for judgment (2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6). The Son’s concern is for humanity.

The Greek order is emphatic and balanced: “For not, surely, angels does he take hold of, but the seed of Abraham he takes hold of.” The verb is repeated at the end of each half, and the contrast falls on the two objects.

Word Study: ἐπιλαμβάνεται epilambanetai, “he takes hold of”

The verb ἐπιλαμβάνομαι epilambanomai means “to take hold of, to grasp, to seize.” It is used of physically grasping someone, whether to arrest them (Acts 21:30, 33) or to help them. When Peter began to sink in the sea, Jesus “reached out his hand and took hold of him” (Matt 14:31). Jesus “took hold of” the blind man and led him (Mark 8:23). The verb can also describe taking hold of something in order to keep it: Paul urges Timothy to “take hold of eternal life” (1 Tim 6:12).

Hebrews uses the same verb again in 8:9, quoting the new covenant promise of Jeremiah 31: the old covenant was made “on the day when I took them by the hand (ἐπιλαβομένου μου τῆς χειρὸς αὐτῶν epilabomenou mou tēs cheiros autōn) to lead them out of the land of Egypt.” The Hebrew of Jeremiah reads בְּיוֹם הֶחֱזִיקִי בְיָדָם לְהוֹצִיאָם מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם beyom hecheziqi veyadam lehotsi’am me’erets mitsrayim, “on the day I grasped their hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt” (Jer 31:32). There the verb describes God’s act of rescue: he took hold of his people to deliver them.

Interpretive Options: “Take Hold Of” or “Take On”?

  • “He takes on the nature of.” Many church fathers, and the older English translations that followed them, understood the verb to refer to the incarnation: the Son did not assume the nature of angels but the nature of Abraham’s seed. The King James Version, for example, supplied the words “the nature of” in italics. This reading fits the theme of 2:14 (he “partook” of flesh and blood) and 2:17 (he was “made like his brothers”).
  • “He takes hold of in order to help.” Most modern interpreters understand the verb in its ordinary sense of grasping someone to help or rescue them. The Son reaches out to rescue not angels but Abraham’s descendants.

The second view is more likely, for several reasons. First, the verb does not elsewhere mean “to assume a nature”; it regularly means “to take hold of” a person. Second, the present tense (epilambanetai, “he takes hold of”) describes an ongoing activity rather than the single, past event of the incarnation, which the author described with an aorist in 2:14 (“he partook”). Third, the use of the same verb in 8:9 for God taking Israel by the hand in the exodus supports the sense of rescue. Fourth, the next verses are full of the language of help: he makes propitiation (2:17) and “is able to help” (2:18). The first view is not wrong in its theology—verse 17 clearly states that the Son was made like his brothers—but it reads into this verb more than it says. The best understanding is that the Son takes hold of his people to rescue them, and the incarnation is the means by which he does so.

Old Testament Background: Isaiah 41:8–10

The combination of “the seed of Abraham,” being “taken hold of,” and being “helped” recalls one of the most tender promises in the Old Testament:

וְאַתָּה יִשְׂרָאֵל עַבְדִּי יַעֲקֹב אֲשֶׁר בְּחַרְתִּיךָ זֶרַע אַבְרָהָם אֹהֲבִי אֲשֶׁר הֶחֱזַקְתִּיךָ מִקְצוֹת הָאָרֶץ — ve’attah Yisra’el ‘avdi, Ya‘aqov ’asher bechartika, zera‘ ’Avraham ’ohavi, ’asher hechezaqtika miqtsot ha’arets, “But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen, the seed of Abraham my friend, you whom I took hold of from the ends of the earth” (Isa 41:8–9).

And then: אַל־תִּירָא כִּי עִמְּךָ־אָנִי … אַף־עֲזַרְתִּיךָ ’al-tira’ ki ‘immeka-’ani … ’aph-‘azartika, “Do not fear, for I am with you … I will surely help you” (Isa 41:10).

The Hebrew verb הֶחֱזִיק hecheziq, “to take hold of, to grasp firmly,” is the same verb used in Jeremiah 31:32. Here in Isaiah, God takes hold of the seed of Abraham, tells them not to fear, and promises to help them. Hebrews 2:15–18 contains every one of these elements: the seed of Abraham, taking hold, freedom from fear, and help (2:18). Whether or not the author consciously alluded to Isaiah 41, his readers, steeped in the Scriptures, could hear in his words an echo of God’s ancient promise to his chosen people, now fulfilled in the Son.

Who is “the seed of Abraham” (σπέρματος Ἀβραὰμ spermatos Abraam)? The phrase naturally refers to the people of God descended from Abraham, and for Jewish Christian readers it would have carried rich associations. They were the heirs of the promises made to Abraham, which Hebrews will discuss at length (6:13–20; 11:8–19). But the New Testament makes clear that the true seed of Abraham is defined not by physical descent alone but by faith. Paul writes, “know that those who are of faith are the sons of Abraham” and “if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise” (Gal 3:7, 29). Hebrews never discusses the inclusion of Gentiles directly, but its focus on faith (chapter 11) and on the people of God as those who hold fast their confession points in the same direction. The “seed of Abraham” here is the same group as the “many sons,” the “brothers,” and the “children” of 2:10–14: the people of God, whom the Son came to rescue.

The comparison with angels also contains a note of wonder. The Son did not come to the aid of the glorious, powerful beings of chapter 1. He came to the aid of weak, mortal, sinful human beings. The words of Psalm 8 come back with new force: “What is man, that you remember him?” The answer of the gospel is that God not only remembers humanity; in his Son he reaches down and takes hold of it, as Jesus took hold of the sinking Peter, to lift it up.

2:17

Therefore he was obligated to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the things that pertain to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people.

This verse gathers up everything from 2:10 onward and states its conclusion. “Therefore” (ὅθεν hothen, literally “from where,” “hence”) is a favorite word of the author for drawing inferences (3:1; 7:25; 8:3; 9:18). Because the Son came to take hold of Abraham’s seed, to set free those enslaved by fear of death, and to bring many sons to glory, he had to become like them. And with that conclusion the author introduces, for the first time, the title that will dominate the central chapters of the book: “high priest.”

“He was obligated.” The verb ὤφειλεν ōpheilen, from ὀφείλω opheilō, means “to owe, to be under obligation, to be bound.” It is a stronger word than “it was fitting” in 2:10. Yet the obligation did not come from outside God, as if some higher law forced his hand. It arose from the purpose God had freely chosen and from the nature of the work to be done. If the Son was to be a high priest for human beings, he had to be human. As the author will explain, “every high priest taken from among men is appointed on behalf of men in the things that pertain to God” (5:1). A priest represents his people before God; he must belong to them. An angel could not be a priest for humanity, nor could the Son, without becoming a man.

“To be made like his brothers in every respect.” The phrase κατὰ πάντα kata panta, “in every respect, in all things,” is placed first for emphasis. The aorist passive infinitive ὁμοιωθῆναι homoiōthēnai, “to be made like,” describes the incarnation as something that happened to the Son, a likeness he received. The likeness was complete. He did not take a partial humanity—a human body with a divine mind in place of a human soul, as some later teachers suggested—but a full human nature: body and soul, mind and will, emotions and growth. Jesus grew in wisdom and stature (Luke 2:52). He hungered (Matt 4:2), grew weary (John 4:6), wept (John 11:35), was troubled in spirit (John 12:27; 13:21), and was in agony in prayer (Luke 22:44). He was tested (2:18; 4:15). He died (2:9). Hebrews 4:15 will repeat that he was “tested in every respect (κατὰ πάντα) in the same way as we are,” and add the crucial qualification: “yet without sin.” The likeness is complete in everything that belongs to true humanity; sin is not part of true humanity but its distortion.

The word “brothers” (τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς tois adelphois) recalls 2:11–12. He did not become like human beings in the abstract; he became like his brothers, the specific family he came to save.

“So that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest.” The purpose of the likeness is priesthood. The verb γένηται genētai, “he might become,” suggests that his high priesthood was the outcome of a path he walked. The Son did not need to become anything in order to be God; but in order to be a high priest for humanity, he needed to pass through the experience of human life, suffering, and death (compare 5:8–10, where, “having been made perfect,” he was “designated by God a high priest after the order of Melchizedek”; and 6:20, “having become a high priest forever”). Chapters 5–10 will explain this priesthood in detail. Here it is announced in two words.

Word Study: ἐλεήμων eleēmōn, “merciful,” and πιστός pistos, “faithful”

ἐλεήμων eleēmōn, “merciful,” describes one who feels compassion for the needy and acts to help them. In the Greek Old Testament it is above all a description of God himself. When the Lord proclaimed his name to Moses, he declared himself אֵל רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן ’el rachum vechannun, “a God compassionate and gracious” (Exod 34:6), and the Greek translators used eleēmōn among the words for these attributes. The high priest of the new covenant shares God’s own mercy. And he shares it not from a distance but as one who knows from experience what his people endure. Hebrews will develop this in 4:15 (“we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses”) and 5:2 (he can “deal gently with the ignorant and wayward”). In the Greek word order, eleēmōn stands first and is separated from the rest of the phrase by the verb, giving it special emphasis: “so that merciful he might become, and a faithful high priest.”

πιστός pistos can mean “faithful” (loyal, keeping faith with someone) or “trustworthy, reliable” (one in whom others can have faith). Both senses apply. Jesus is faithful to God who appointed him, and he is trustworthy for his people who depend on him. The next paragraph takes up this word directly: Jesus “was faithful to the one who appointed him, just as Moses also was in all God’s house” (3:2), alluding to God’s word about Moses: לֹא־כֵן עַבְדִּי מֹשֶׁה בְּכָל־בֵּיתִי נֶאֱמָן הוּא lo’-khen ‘avdi Mosheh bekhol-beiti ne’eman hu’, “Not so my servant Moses; in all my house he is faithful” (Num 12:7). The readers may also have remembered God’s promise after the failure of Eli’s sons: “I will raise up for myself a faithful priest” (kohen ne’eman), who would do according to what was in God’s heart (1 Sam 2:35). The old priesthood had often been unfaithful. Jesus is the priest God had promised.

The two qualities belong together. Mercy without faithfulness would be sentiment without reliability; faithfulness without mercy would be duty without compassion. In Jesus they are perfectly united. Toward his people he is merciful; toward God he is faithful; and so his people can trust him completely.

“In the things that pertain to God.” The phrase τὰ πρὸς τὸν θεόν ta pros ton theon is an accusative of respect, meaning “with respect to the things directed toward God,” that is, the sphere of worship, sacrifice, and intercession. It appears again in the definition of a high priest in 5:1. The same expression is used in the Greek of Exodus 18:19, where Jethro advises Moses to represent the people before God (Hebrew הֱיֵה אַתָּה לָעָם מוּל הָאֱלֹהִים heyeh ’attah la‘am mul ha’Elohim, “you be for the people before God”). A high priest stands between God and the people, representing the people before God.

“To make propitiation for the sins of the people.” Finally, the author names the high priest’s central task. The construction εἰς τὸ ἱλάσκεσθαι eis to hilaskesthai, “for the purpose of making propitiation,” expresses purpose. The object is “the sins of the people” (τὰς ἁμαρτίας τοῦ λαοῦ tas hamartias tou laou). “The people” (λαός laos) is the standard Old Testament term for Israel as God’s covenant people; Hebrews applies it to the people of God gathered around Christ (4:9; 8:10; 13:12).

The picture is of the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), when once a year the high priest entered the Most Holy Place with blood to make atonement for himself and for the people (Heb 9:7). The author will develop this comparison fully in chapter 9. Here he anticipates it. Jesus is the high priest whose work is to deal with sin before God.

Word Study: ἱλάσκεσθαι hilaskesthai, “to make propitiation”

The verb ἱλάσκομαι hilaskomai belongs to a family of words built on hilaos, “gracious, favorable.” In ordinary Greek religion, hilaskomai meant “to make a god favorable,” “to appease, to propitiate.” Worshipers offered sacrifices to calm an angry deity. In that usage the god was the object of the verb: one propitiated the god.

In the Bible, the usage is different in an important way. The Greek Old Testament uses this word family chiefly (especially the compound exilaskomai) to translate the Hebrew verb כִּפֶּר kipper, “to make atonement.” And in Scripture God is almost never the object of the verb. Human beings do not “propitiate God” as if they could manipulate him. Instead, the priest makes atonement for sins, or for people, or for the sanctuary; or God himself atones for sin. In a few places the Greek translators did use the verb with God as object, for example where the Hebrew speaks of “entreating the face of the Lord” (לְחַלּוֹת אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה lechallot ’et-penei YHWH, Zech 7:2; compare Mal 1:9), but these are exceptions. In Hebrews 2:17 the object is “the sins of the people,” in line with the biblical pattern.

Old Testament Background: כִּפֶּר kipper, Expiation, and Propitiation

English theology uses two words for what atonement accomplishes. Expiation is directed toward sin: it removes guilt, cleanses defilement, cancels the offense. Propitiation is directed toward God: it turns away his just wrath and secures his favor. In the twentieth century the British scholar C. H. Dodd argued that the biblical words of this family should be understood only as expiation, since in the Septuagint they take sin rather than God as their object; he held that the idea of averting divine wrath belonged to pagan religion, not to the Bible. Other scholars, notably Leon Morris and Roger Nicole, answered that the Old Testament contexts in which atonement occurs very often speak of God’s wrath, and that the biblical idea of atonement includes the turning away of that wrath. The evidence of the Hebrew text supports the latter view, while showing why both ideas belong together.

The meaning of kipper. The origin of the verb is debated. Some connect it with a root meaning “to cover,” others with a word meaning “to wipe away, to purge.” A third and important connection is with the noun כֹּפֶר kopher, “ransom price,” the payment that redeems a forfeited life. When Israel was counted, each man was to give כֹּפֶר נַפְשׁוֹ kopher naphsho, “a ransom for his life,” to the Lord, “so that there will be no plague among them” (Exod 30:12; compare Exod 21:30). In such texts the atoning payment protects the guilty from the judgment they deserve.

Atonement that cleanses (expiation). On the Day of Atonement the high priest sprinkled blood on and before the כַּפֹּרֶת kapporet, the “mercy seat” or “place of atonement” on the ark (Exod 25:17; Lev 16:14–15)—a noun from the same root. By this blood וְכִפֶּר עַל־הַקֹּדֶשׁ מִטֻּמְאֹת בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל וּמִפִּשְׁעֵיהֶם לְכָל־חַטֹּאתָם vekhipper ‘al-haqqodesh mittum’ot benei Yisra’el umippish‘eihem lekhol-chatto’tam, “he shall make atonement for the holy place, because of the uncleannesses of the sons of Israel and because of their transgressions, all their sins” (Lev 16:16). And כִּי־בַיּוֹם הַזֶּה יְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיכֶם לְטַהֵר אֶתְכֶם מִכֹּל חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם לִפְנֵי יְהוָה תִּטְהָרוּ ki-vayyom hazzeh yekhapper ‘aleikhem letaher ’etkhem mikkol chatto’teikhem, liphnei YHWH titharu, “For on this day atonement shall be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins you shall be clean before the Lord” (Lev 16:30). Here atonement cleanses and removes sin. That is expiation.

Atonement that averts wrath (propitiation). But the same verb is used in contexts where God’s wrath is explicitly in view. When the Lord’s anger broke out against Israel after Korah’s rebellion, Moses told Aaron to take incense and וְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיהֶם כִּי־יָצָא הַקֶּצֶף מִלִּפְנֵי יְהוָה הֵחֵל הַנָּגֶף vekhapper ‘aleihem ki-yatsa’ haqqetseph millipnei YHWH hechel hannagef, “make atonement for them, for wrath has gone out from before the Lord; the plague has begun” (Num 16:46; Hebrew 17:11). Aaron did so, standing between the dead and the living, and the plague stopped. When Phinehas acted against flagrant sin, God said that he הֵשִׁיב אֶת־חֲמָתִי heshiv ’et-chamati, “turned back my wrath,” and וַיְכַפֵּר עַל־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל vaykhapper ‘al-benei Yisra’el, “made atonement for the sons of Israel” (Num 25:11, 13). After the golden calf, when the Lord had said, “let my anger burn against them” (Exod 32:10), Moses went up, saying, אוּלַי אֲכַפְּרָה בְּעַד חַטַּאתְכֶם ’ulai ’akhapperah be‘ad chatta’tkhem, “perhaps I can make atonement for your sin” (Exod 32:30). Even in a human setting, Jacob, fearing his brother’s anger, said of his gifts, אֲכַפְּרָה פָנָיו בַּמִּנְחָה ’akhapperah panav bamminchah, literally “I will cover his face with the gift,” that is, “I will appease him” (Gen 32:20; Hebrew 32:21). And the Day of Atonement itself was surrounded by warnings of death: Aaron must not enter the Most Holy Place at will, וְלֹא יָמוּת velo’ yamut, “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:2).

Both together. One psalm brings the two sides together in a single sentence: וְהוּא רַחוּם יְכַפֵּר עָוֺן וְלֹא־יַשְׁחִית וְהִרְבָּה לְהָשִׁיב אַפּוֹ וְלֹא־יָעִיר כָּל־חֲמָתוֹ vehu’ rachum yekhapper ‘avon velo’-yashchit, vehirbah lehashiv ’appo velo’-ya‘ir kol-chamato, “But he, being compassionate, atoned for iniquity and did not destroy; often he turned back his anger and did not stir up all his wrath” (Ps 78:38). Here God is merciful; he atones for iniquity; and as a result his anger is turned back. Atonement deals with sin (expiation), and in doing so it removes the cause of God’s righteous wrath (propitiation). The two are not alternatives but two sides of one act. Sin is the problem; God’s holy wrath is his just response to it; atonement removes the sin and so averts the wrath.

Another psalm joins the same ideas with a prayer for help: עָזְרֵנוּ אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעֵנוּ … וְכַפֵּר עַל־חַטֹּאתֵינוּ לְמַעַן שְׁמֶךָ ‘ozrenu ’Elohei yish‘enu … vekhapper ‘al-chatto’teinu lema‘an shemeka, “Help us, O God of our salvation … and atone for our sins for your name’s sake” (Ps 79:9). In the Greek Psalter this verse uses the verb “help” (boētheō) and the very verb of Hebrews 2:17 (hilaskomai). Hebrews 2:17–18 joins exactly these two ideas: he makes propitiation for sins, and he is able to help.

The source of atonement. Crucially, in the Old Testament God himself provides the means of atonement. “The life of the flesh is in the blood, and וַאֲנִי נְתַתִּיו לָכֶם עַל־הַמִּזְבֵּחַ לְכַפֵּר עַל־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם va’ani netattiv lakhem ‘al-hammizbeach lekhapper ‘al-naphshoteikhem, I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your lives” (Lev 17:11). Atonement is not a human bribe offered to a reluctant deity. It is God’s gracious gift, by which he deals with sin so that his holy presence can dwell among a sinful people without destroying them.

The New Testament carries this teaching forward. Paul writes that God “put forward” Christ Jesus “as a propitiation (ἱλαστήριον hilastērion) through faith in his blood, to demonstrate his righteousness, because in his forbearance he had passed over sins previously committed” (Rom 3:25). The word hilastērion is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the kapporet, the mercy seat, and Hebrews uses it in that sense in 9:5. Paul’s context is the revelation of God’s wrath against all ungodliness (Rom 1:18; 2:5; 3:5). Christ’s death shows that God is righteous in forgiving, because the sins he had passed over have now been justly dealt with: he is “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Rom 3:26). John writes, “In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation (ἱλασμόν hilasmon) for our sins” (1 John 4:10; compare 2:2). And the tax collector in Jesus’ parable prays with the passive form of our verb: “God, be propitious to me, the sinner” (ἱλάσθητί μοι hilasthēti moi, Luke 18:13)—that is, “be merciful to me, turning from your just displeasure.”

Hebrews itself leaves no doubt that God’s wrath is real. It speaks of the generation with whom God was angry and against whom he swore “in my wrath” (3:11, 17; 4:3), of “a fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27), of how “fearful” it is “to fall into the hands of the living God” (10:31), and it closes its final warning with “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29). In such a book, “make propitiation for the sins of the people” cannot mean merely cleansing a stain. It means dealing with sins in such a way that the people are no longer under God’s judgment but may draw near to him with confidence (4:16; 10:19–22).

For this reason this study renders hilaskesthai “to make propitiation for.” The phrase must be understood biblically. The sins are the object: Christ deals with them, removing their guilt (expiation). The result is that God’s just wrath against those sins is satisfied and turned away (propitiation). And the whole action flows from God’s own love: it was “by the grace of God” that Jesus tasted death for everyone (2:9), and it was “fitting” for God to bring many sons to glory in this way (2:10). Father and Son are not at odds. The Father does not have to be persuaded to love by the Son’s sacrifice; the Son’s sacrifice is the Father’s love in action, meeting the demands of the Father’s holiness.

The present tense of the infinitive (hilaskesthai) deserves a comment. Hebrews insists that Christ’s sacrifice was offered “once for all” (7:27; 9:12, 26–28; 10:10–14); it is never repeated. The present tense here does not suggest a repeated sacrifice. It describes the purpose and function of his priesthood in general terms, and it fits the author’s conviction that the one sacrifice has continuing effect. The exalted high priest “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through him (7:25). His once-for-all offering remains permanently effective, and he applies its benefits to his people as they draw near.

The verse thus unites what the Son is and what he does. He became like his brothers so that he could be merciful toward them; he remained faithful to God so that he could be trusted by them; and he made propitiation for their sins so that they could stand before God without fear. The rest of Hebrews is, in a sense, an extended commentary on this one verse.

2:18

For because he himself has suffered when he was tested, he is able to help those who are being tested.

The chapter ends not with a doctrinal formula but with a pastoral promise. The “for” (γάρ gar) explains how Jesus is a “merciful” high priest: he has been through what his people are going through, and so he is able to help them.

The opening words ἐν ᾧ en hō can be understood in two ways. They may mean “because, inasmuch as” (a causal sense, as in Rom 8:3): “because he himself has suffered when tested, he is able to help.” Or they may mean “in that which,” referring to the sphere of his suffering: “in the very things in which he suffered when tested, he is able to help.” The difference is slight. Either way, his own experience of testing equips him to help others in theirs.

“He himself has suffered” (πέπονθεν αὐτὸς peponthen autos). The verb πάσχω paschō, “to suffer,” recalls the “suffering of death” (2:9) and the “sufferings” through which he was perfected (2:10). The perfect tense is significant. It describes a past experience whose effects continue. Jesus’ suffering is over; he is now crowned with glory. But the experience remains part of who he is. The exalted high priest has not forgotten what it was like. The scars remain (John 20:27). The emphatic “he himself” (αὐτός autos) stresses that this was his own personal experience, not something he observed from outside.

Word Study: πειράζω peirazō, “to test, to tempt”

The verb πειράζω peirazō appears twice in this verse: Jesus was “tested” (πειρασθείς peirastheis, aorist participle), and he helps “those who are being tested” (τοῖς πειραζομένοις tois peirazomenois, present participle). The word has two related meanings that English must usually separate: “to test, to put to the proof,” and “to tempt, to entice to sin.” The same situation can be both at once, depending on who is acting and with what purpose.

When God tests his people, he does so to prove and strengthen their faith, never to lead them into sin: וְהָאֱלֹהִים נִסָּה אֶת־אַבְרָהָם veha’Elohim nissah ’et-’Avraham, “and God tested Abraham” (Gen 22:1). Hebrews 11:17 recalls this: “By faith Abraham, when he was tested (πειραζόμενος peirazomenos), offered up Isaac.” James insists that God “tempts no one” to evil (Jas 1:13). But the devil uses the same circumstances as temptations, aiming to draw people away from God; he is called “the tempter” (ὁ πειράζων ho peirazōn, Matt 4:3; 1 Thess 3:5). In Hebrews 3:8–9 the Israelites “tested” God in the wilderness, putting him to the proof in unbelief.

The readers’ trials were exactly this kind of double-edged experience. Persecution, loss, shame, and weariness tested the genuineness of their faith (compare 1 Pet 1:6–7). At the same time, these pressures tempted them to give up, to drift away, to return to safer ground. This study renders the verb “tested” in 2:18 to keep both aspects in view, since Jesus’ trials, like theirs, were at once God’s proving and the devil’s enticement.

Jesus was tested throughout his life. The Gospels record his testing by the devil in the wilderness, where he was tempted to turn stones into bread, to throw himself from the temple, and to gain the kingdoms of the world by worshiping Satan (Matt 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13). Luke adds that the devil departed from him “until an opportune time” (Luke 4:13). Jesus told his disciples, “You are those who have stayed with me in my trials” (πειρασμοῖς peirasmois, Luke 22:28). In Gethsemane he faced the supreme test, praying in agony for the cup to pass from him and yet submitting to his Father’s will (Mark 14:32–42; compare Heb 5:7–8). On the cross he was tempted again with the taunt, “come down from the cross” (Matt 27:40). Notice how the author links the testing directly to suffering: “he suffered when he was tested.” His temptations were not abstract moral puzzles. They came in the form of pain, rejection, and the threat of death—the very things his readers faced.

Does Jesus’ sinlessness mean that his testing was less real than ours? Hebrews answers no. He was tested “in every respect in the same way, yet without sin” (4:15). Westcott made the penetrating observation that sympathy with the sinner in his trial does not depend on the experience of sin but on the experience of the strength of the temptation to sin—and only the sinless can know the full intensity of that strength, because only he never yielded. The one who gives in early never feels the full force of the pressure. Jesus felt it to the end. He knows the strength of temptation better than any of us.

“He is able to help” (δύναται … βοηθῆσαι dynatai … boēthēsai). The present tense “he is able” describes his present capacity as exalted high priest. The verb βοηθέω boētheō means “to come to the aid of, to help.” It was traditionally explained as combining boē, “a cry,” and theō, “to run”—to run in answer to a cry for help. Whether or not the explanation is right, it captures the sense. It is the word of the desperate father who cried to Jesus, “help us!” and “help my unbelief!” (Mark 9:22, 24), and of the man of Macedonia in Paul’s vision: “Come over to Macedonia and help us” (Acts 16:9). The God who said to Abraham’s seed, “Do not fear … I will help you” (Isa 41:10) now helps through his Son.

The same word family appears at two important later points in Hebrews. In 4:16 the readers are urged to “draw near with boldness to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace for timely help” (βοήθειαν boētheian). In 13:6 they are given words to say with confidence: “The Lord is my helper (βοηθός boēthos); I will not be afraid; what can man do to me?” The help Jesus gives is not that he removes all trials—he did not remove his own—but that he provides mercy, grace, strength, and his own presence in the midst of them, so that his people can endure without falling away.

This last verse is the pastoral heart of the chapter. The readers were being tested. They were tempted to drift (2:1). The author has shown them a Savior who is greater than the angels, yet who became lower than the angels; who is crowned with glory, yet who suffered death; who is the sanctifier, yet who calls them brothers; who conquered the devil, yet who was tested as they are; who made propitiation for their sins, and who now stands ready to help them. The answer to the danger of drifting is not merely to try harder but to look to Jesus (2:9; 3:1; 12:2) and to draw near to him for the help he is able and willing to give.

For Reflection

  1. Psalm 8 asks, “What is man, that you remember him?” How does 2:5–9 answer that question in light of Jesus? How should this shape the way you view your own dignity and destiny, and that of every other human being?
  2. The author freely admits, “we do not yet see all things subjected to him” (2:8), yet says, “but we do see Jesus” (2:9). Where in your life or in the world do you most feel the “not yet”? What does it mean in practice to fix your eyes on Jesus there?
  3. It was “fitting” for God to perfect the pioneer of salvation “through sufferings” (2:10). How does this change the way you interpret your own sufferings as one of the “many sons” he is bringing to glory?
  4. The exalted Son “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:11). In what situations are you tempted to be ashamed of Christ or of his people? How might his willingness to acknowledge you change your response?
  5. Hebrews joins Christ’s victory over the devil (2:14–15) to his propitiation for sins (2:17). Why is it important to hold these together? In what ways does fear of death, or fear of loss, still exercise power over you, and how does this passage speak to it?
  6. Jesus “is able to help those who are being tested” (2:18). What specific test are you facing now, and what would it look like to bring it to him as a merciful and faithful high priest (see 4:14–16)?
