Redemption through the Blood of Christ (9:11–28)

Translation

11But when Christ arrived as high priest of the good things that have come, through the greater and more perfect tabernacle not made with hands—that is, not of this creation— 12and not through the blood of goats and calves but through his own blood, he entered once for all into the holy places, having obtained an eternal redemption. 13For if the blood of goats and bulls, and the ashes of a heifer sprinkled on those who have been defiled, sanctify for the purity of the flesh, 14how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God!

15And for this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that, since a death has taken place for redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who have been called may receive the promised eternal inheritance. 16For where there is a covenant (or “will”), the death of the one who made it must be established. 17For a covenant (or “will”) is made firm over the dead, since it is never in force while the one who made it is living. 18Therefore not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood. 19For when every commandment according to the law had been spoken by Moses to all the people, he took the blood of the calves, with water and scarlet wool and hyssop, and sprinkled both the book itself and all the people, 20saying,

“This is the blood of the covenant that God commanded for you.”

21And in the same way he sprinkled with the blood both the tabernacle and all the vessels of the ministry. 22Indeed, according to the law almost everything is purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.

23It was necessary, then, for the copies of the things in the heavens to be purified with these, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these. 24For Christ did not enter holy places made with hands, copies of the true ones, but into heaven itself, now to appear before the face of God on our behalf. 25Nor did he enter in order to offer himself repeatedly, as the high priest enters the holy places year by year with blood not his own, 26for then he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world. But as it is, he has been manifested once, at the consummation of the ages, to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself. 27And just as it is appointed for human beings to die once, and after this comes judgment, 28so also Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, apart from sin, to those who eagerly await him, for salvation.

Setting and Structure

With 9:11 the long-awaited “but” arrives. The preacher has described the earthly sanctuary with its two tents and its annual ritual (9:1–10), concluding that it was a “parable” whose regulations were imposed “until the time of setting things right.” Now he announces that the time has come: “But Christ, having arrived….” Everything that follows answers point by point to what came before:

  • The earthly sanctuary “of this world” (9:1) gives way to the “greater and more perfect tabernacle not made with hands, not of this creation” (9:11).
  • The high priest entering “once a year” (9:7) gives way to Christ entering “once for all” (9:12).
  • “Not without blood” (9:7)—the blood of animals—gives way to “his own blood” (9:12).
  • Gifts and sacrifices that “cannot perfect the worshiper with respect to conscience” (9:9) give way to the blood that will “purify our conscience” (9:14).
  • “Regulations for the flesh” (9:10) and sanctification “for the purity of the flesh” (9:13) give way to cleansing from dead works “to serve the living God” (9:14).
  • The way into the holy places “not yet disclosed” (9:8) gives way to Christ entering “heaven itself” to appear before God “for us” (9:24).

The section unfolds in three movements:

1. The sacrifice and its effect (9:11–14). Two long sentences state the thesis. Verses 11–12 describe Christ’s high-priestly entry: through a better tabernacle, by a better blood, once for all, securing an eternal redemption. Verses 13–14 then argue from the lesser to the greater (a form of reasoning Jewish teachers called qal wachomer, “light and heavy”): if animal blood and the ashes of the red heifer could restore ritual purity, how much more will the blood of Christ cleanse the conscience. Verse 14 is one of the richest sentences in the New Testament, bringing together the Son, the eternal Spirit, and God in a single act of self-offering.

2. The sacrifice and the covenant (9:15–22). Christ’s death is now connected with the “new covenant” of chapter 8. A death was necessary for the new covenant to take effect and for its promised inheritance to be received (9:15). The preacher illustrates this with a play on the Greek word διαθήκη diathēkē, which can mean both “covenant” and “will, testament” (9:16–17)—a much-discussed passage that will be examined carefully below. He then returns to Scripture: the first covenant also was inaugurated with blood, when Moses sprinkled the book and the people at Sinai (Exod 24:3–8), and indeed under the law almost everything is purified with blood, for “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:18–22).

3. The sacrifice and the end of the ages (9:23–28). The final movement draws the conclusion. The heavenly realities require better sacrifices than the earthly copies (9:23). Christ has entered not a hand-made sanctuary but heaven itself, to appear before God on our behalf (9:24). Unlike the high priest, he does not need to repeat his offering, for his sacrifice, offered once at the climax of history, has put away sin (9:25–26). And just as every human being dies once and then faces judgment, so Christ, offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time—not to deal with sin, but to bring salvation to those who wait for him (9:27–28).

The historical and cultural background of this section is almost entirely the world of Israel’s sacrificial worship, which the original hearers knew intimately. Four Old Testament passages lie close beneath its surface: the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), the ritual of the red heifer (Num 19), the covenant ceremony at Sinai (Exod 24), and the fourth Servant Song (Isa 52:13–53:12). A fifth background is the Greco-Roman world of wills and inheritances, which the preacher briefly borrows in 9:16–17.

Finally, the reader should notice the shape of time in this section. Christ’s work has a past (he “offered himself,” “entered,” “has been manifested” once at the end of the ages), a present (he is “now” appearing before God for us, 9:24), and a future (he “will appear a second time,” 9:28). This threefold pattern mirrors the Day of Atonement: the sacrifice was slain in the courtyard, the high priest went in to God’s presence with the blood, and the people waited outside for him to come out and bless them. Christians live in the middle period. Our High Priest has offered his sacrifice and entered the sanctuary; we wait for him to come out. The section ends on that note of eager expectation.

Verse by Verse

9:11

But when Christ arrived as high priest of the good things that have come, through the greater and more perfect tabernacle not made with hands—that is, not of this creation—

Verses 11–12 form a single sentence in Greek, whose main verb, “he entered” (εἰσῆλθεν eisēlthen), does not come until 9:12. The sentence is built to keep the reader waiting while it piles up the ways Christ’s entry surpasses the high priest’s: a better office, a better sanctuary, a better blood, a better result.

“But Christ” (Χριστὸς δέ Christos de). The name stands first, emphatically. The δέ de completes the μέν men of 9:1: on the one hand the first covenant had its earthly sanctuary; on the other hand, Christ. The preacher uses the title “Christ” (Messiah, “Anointed One”) rather than “Jesus” here, perhaps because the anointed high priest of Lev 16:32 (הַכֹּהֵן אֲשֶׁר־יִמְשַׁח אֹתוֹ hakkohen asher-yimshach oto, “the priest whom one anoints”) is in view, and Christ is the truly Anointed One.

“Arrived as high priest” (παραγενόμενος ἀρχιερεύς paragenomenos archiereus). παραγίνομαι paraginomai means “to come, arrive, appear on the scene.” It is used of John the Baptist’s appearance (Matt 3:1) and of Jesus coming to be baptized (Matt 3:13). The aorist participle marks a decisive historical arrival. The “time of setting things right” (9:10) has come because he has come.

“Of the good things that have come” (τῶν γενομένων ἀγαθῶν tōn genomenōn agathōn). Christ is high priest “of good things,” that is, the one who mediates and administers the blessings of the new covenant: forgiveness, a cleansed conscience, access to God, eternal redemption, an eternal inheritance. The participle γενομένων genomenōn, “having come into being, having come,” says these blessings are already here.

Textual Note: “Good Things That Have Come” or “Good Things to Come”?

The manuscripts divide here. Some read τῶν γενομένων ἀγαθῶν tōn genomenōn agathōn, “of the good things that have come”; others read τῶν μελλόντων ἀγαθῶν tōn mellontōn agathōn, “of the good things to come” (the reading behind the King James Version). The difference in Greek is small but the sense differs: are the blessings present or future?

Bruce Metzger’s Textual Commentary explains why the editors of the standard Greek text (followed by the SBL text used in this study) preferred “that have come”: it has the stronger and more diverse early support (including the early papyrus P46 and Codex Vaticanus), and the alternative reading can easily be explained as a scribe’s assimilation to 10:1, which speaks of the law as “a shadow of the good things to come” (τῶν μελλόντων ἀγαθῶν tōn mellontōn agathōn). Scribes naturally harmonized similar phrases. The reverse change, from the familiar “to come” to the unusual “that have come,” would be hard to explain.

The two readings are not in theological conflict. From the standpoint of the old covenant (as in 10:1), the good things were future; from the standpoint of Christ’s arrival (as here), they have come. Hebrews holds both together: believers have already “tasted … the powers of the age to come” (6:5), yet they still await the city that is to come (13:14) and the salvation Christ will bring when he appears a second time (9:28).

“Through the greater and more perfect tabernacle not made with hands.” The preposition “through” (διά dia with the genitive) can be spatial (“passing through”) or instrumental (“by means of”). The tabernacle is described in three ways: “greater” (μείζονος meizonos), “more perfect” (τελειοτέρας teleioteras, related to the key verb “make perfect”), and “not made with hands” (οὐ χειροποιήτου ou cheiropoiētou). The last phrase is then explained: “that is, not of this creation” (οὐ ταύτης τῆς κτίσεως ou tautēs tēs ktiseōs). In the Greek Old Testament “made with hands” is often used of idols (for example, Lev 26:1), and Stephen applied it to the temple: “the Most High does not dwell in things made with hands” (Acts 7:48). In Hebrews the contrast is not between true and false worship, but between the earthly copy built by human craftsmen according to God’s pattern and the true sanctuary “which the Lord set up, not man” (8:2).

Interpretive Options: What Is the “Greater Tabernacle”?

  • Christ’s body or human nature. A number of ancient and Reformation interpreters, including Chrysostom and Calvin, took the “tabernacle” here as Christ’s flesh, the “tent” in which the Word dwelt (compare John 1:14, “the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us”; Mark 14:58, the temple “not made with hands” that Jesus would raise in three days; and Heb 10:20, “through the curtain, that is, his flesh”). On this view, Christ entered God’s presence by means of his incarnate life and death.
  • The church. Some have taken it to mean the body of Christ, the people of God as his dwelling (3:6, “whose house we are”). This has little support in the immediate context.
  • The heavenly sanctuary (outer region). Most recent commentators understand the phrase spatially: just as the high priest passed through the outer tent to reach the inner sanctuary, so Christ passed through the heavenly realm to the very presence of God. This matches 4:14, where Jesus is the great high priest “who has passed through the heavens,” and 7:26, where he has become “higher than the heavens.”
  • The heavenly sanctuary as a whole. Similar to the previous view, but without dividing heaven into an outer and inner tent: the “greater and more perfect tabernacle” is simply the true sanctuary of 8:2, the realm of God’s presence, and Christ ministers “by way of” it rather than the earthly one.

The context strongly favors a reference to the heavenly sanctuary. The explanation “not of this creation” is hard to apply to Christ’s human body, which was truly part of this creation (2:14, 17); his flesh was genuine human flesh, not something “not of this creation.” The same expression “not made with hands” in 9:24 clearly refers to the heavenly sanctuary, “heaven itself.” And 8:2 has already introduced “the true tabernacle which the Lord set up, not man.” The tabernacle of 9:11 is that true sanctuary, the reality of which Moses’ tent was a copy. Whether the preacher thinks of Christ passing through an outer heavenly region before entering the inner one, or simply of his ministry being carried out in the heavenly sanctuary, the point is the same: Christ’s priesthood belongs to the realm of ultimate reality, God’s own dwelling, and not to the earthly, created order. The body-interpretation contains a genuine biblical truth (10:20 does link the curtain with his flesh), but it is not what this verse says.

The contrast between “made with hands” and “not made with hands” is also a contrast between what human beings can build and what only God can provide. The deepest need of sinners—access to God—cannot be met by anything human hands construct, however beautiful or divinely patterned. It can be met only by what God himself has established in Christ.

9:12

and not through the blood of goats and calves but through his own blood, he entered once for all into the holy places, having obtained an eternal redemption.

The main verb finally arrives: “he entered” (εἰσῆλθεν eisēlthen). But first comes one more contrast, between two kinds of blood.

“Not through the blood of goats and calves.” On the Day of Atonement the high priest took the blood of a bull (Hebrew פַּר par) for himself and his household and the blood of a goat (שָׂעִיר sa’ir) for the people (Lev 16:11, 15). The preacher’s “calves” (μόσχων moschōn) is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the young bull in Lev 16; in 9:13 he will say “bulls” (ταύρων taurōn), referring to the same animals. The plurals (“goats,” “calves”) generalize: the yearly rite required new animals every year.

“But through his own blood” (διὰ δὲ τοῦ ἰδίου αἵματος dia de tou idiou haimatos). The word ἴδιος idios, “his own,” is emphatic. Christ did not bring another’s blood; he gave his own life. Here again the preposition is dia with the genitive, as in 9:11, but now in its instrumental sense: “by means of.” The preacher seems to enjoy the double use—Christ entered through (by way of) the greater tabernacle and through (by means of) his own blood.

This raises a question that has been asked since early times: Did Christ literally carry his blood into heaven, as the high priest carried blood in a basin into the inner room? The preacher does not say Christ entered “with” his blood, as he says of the high priest in 9:25 (“with blood not his own,” ἐν αἵματι ἀλλοτρίῳ en haimati allotriō). He says Christ entered “through” or “by means of” his blood. The meaning is that Christ’s entry into God’s presence was grounded on, and made possible by, his death. His shed blood—his life laid down in sacrificial death—is the basis on which he appears before God and on which his people are accepted. To press the analogy into a picture of a physical vessel of blood carried into heaven is to mistake the parable for the reality. As many interpreters have observed, the efficacy of his blood, not its physical transport, is the point.

“He entered once for all into the holy places” (εἰσῆλθεν ἐφάπαξ εἰς τὰ ἅγια eisēlthen ephapax eis ta hagia). ἐφάπαξ ephapax strengthens the simple hapax (“once”) of 9:7: it means “once and for all, once with permanent effect.” The same word describes Christ’s self-offering in 7:27 and the sanctification of believers “through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” in 10:10. The high priest’s entry was once a year and had to be repeated; Christ’s entry happened once and never needs repeating. “The holy places” (τὰ ἅγια ta hagia) here means the true, heavenly sanctuary, God’s own presence—“heaven itself” (9:24). The aorist “entered” refers to Christ’s ascension, his entry into heaven after his death and resurrection (compare 4:14; 6:19–20).

“Having obtained an eternal redemption” (αἰωνίαν λύτρωσιν εὑράμενος aiōnian lytrōsin heuramenos). The participle εὑράμενος heuramenos is from εὑρίσκω heuriskō, “to find,” in the middle voice, which here means “to obtain, secure, procure.” Grammatically, an aorist participle joined to an aorist main verb usually describes action that is part of, or coincides with, the main action. Christ’s obtaining redemption is thus bound up with his entry: his death, resurrection, and entry into God’s presence form one saving movement, by which redemption was secured. The participle does not suggest that redemption was obtained only after he arrived in heaven, as though the cross accomplished nothing until then; nor does it suggest that Christ needed redemption for himself. The middle voice simply marks the action as Christ’s own achievement, carried out by him for the sake of his people.

Word Study: λύτρωσις lytrōsis — “redemption”

λύτρωσις lytrōsis comes from λύτρον lytron, “ransom price,” the payment that sets a captive or slave free. Jesus used the word lytron when he said that the Son of Man came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). Lytrōsis means the act of release by payment, “redemption, liberation.” In the Greek Old Testament it and related words translate Hebrew terms from the roots פָּדָה padah, “to ransom,” and גָּאַל ga’al, “to redeem (as a kinsman).” They were used for buying back a firstborn son (Exod 13:13–15), redeeming family land (Lev 25:24–26), and supremely for God’s rescue of his people: “He sent redemption to his people; he commanded his covenant forever” (Ps 111:9, Hebrew פְּדוּת שָׁלַח לְעַמּוֹ צִוָּה־לְעוֹלָם בְּרִיתוֹ pedut shalach le’ammo tsivvah-le’olam berito). Psalm 130 ties redemption directly to sin: “with him is abundant redemption, and he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities” (Ps 130:7–8). In Luke, the faithful in Israel were “waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem” (Luke 2:38; compare 1:68).

In Hebrews, the redemption is “from the transgressions” (9:15, using the compound ἀπολύτρωσις apolytrōsis), that is, release from the guilt and bondage of sin, purchased at the cost of Christ’s life. And it is “eternal.” Israel’s Day of Atonement provided a cleansing that lasted for a year (Lev 16:34). The redemption Christ obtained belongs to the age to come; it never expires and never needs renewing.

The word “eternal” (αἰωνίαν aiōnian) is the first of three “eternals” in four verses: eternal redemption (9:12), the eternal Spirit (9:14), the eternal inheritance (9:15). Hebrews has already spoken of “eternal salvation” (5:9) and “eternal judgment” (6:2), and will speak of “the eternal covenant” (13:20). What Christ has done is not provisional, partial, or temporary. It is final in quality and unending in duration. For anxious consciences this is the deepest comfort: the redemption on which we rest cannot run out.

9:13

For if the blood of goats and bulls, and the ashes of a heifer sprinkled on those who have been defiled, sanctify for the purity of the flesh,

Verses 13–14 explain (“for,” γάρ gar) why Christ’s blood secures an eternal redemption. The argument moves from the lesser to the greater: “if … how much more.” Verse 13 states the lesser case, and it is important to see that the preacher states it positively. The old rites really did something. They “sanctify” (ἁγιάζει hagiazei), present tense: they made the defiled person fit once again to take part in the worshiping community. The preacher’s point is not that they did nothing, but that what they did was limited to “the purity of the flesh.”

Two rituals are named. “The blood of goats and bulls” points again to the Day of Atonement (Lev 16) and, more broadly, to the sin offerings of the law. “The ashes of a heifer” points to a less familiar but very important rite, the red heifer of Numbers 19.

Historical Background: The Red Heifer (Numbers 19)

Contact with death was the most serious source of ritual impurity in Israel. “The one who touches the dead body of any person shall be unclean seven days” (Num 19:11). Such a person could not take part in worship, and if he entered the sanctuary without being cleansed he defiled it and was to be cut off: אֶת־מִשְׁכַּן יְהוָה טִמֵּא et-mishkan YHWH timme, “he has defiled the tabernacle of the Lord” (Num 19:13).

God provided a remedy. The people were to bring פָרָה אֲדֻמָּה תְּמִימָה אֲשֶׁר אֵין־בָּהּ מוּם אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָלָה עָלֶיהָ עֹל farah adummah temimah asher en-bah mum asher lo-alah aleha ol, “a red heifer, perfect, in which there is no blemish, on which no yoke has come” (Num 19:2). It was slaughtered outside the camp; the priest sprinkled some of its blood seven times toward the front of the tent of meeting; then the whole animal—hide, flesh, blood, and dung—was burned, and cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet yarn were thrown into the fire (19:3–6). The ashes were stored outside the camp in a clean place, לְמֵי נִדָּה חַטָּאת הִוא leme niddah chattat hi, “for the water of impurity; it is a sin offering” (19:9). When someone was defiled by a corpse, some ashes were mixed with “living” (running) water in a vessel, and a clean person dipped hyssop in it and sprinkled the unclean person on the third and seventh days, after which he washed and was clean (19:17–19).

The rite was rare and precious. The Mishnah, in the tractate devoted to it (Parah), preserves a tradition that only a handful of red heifers were ever prepared from Moses to the destruction of the Second Temple (m. Parah 3:5), because a single heifer’s ashes, greatly diluted, could serve for a very long time. The rite was also mysterious: the priest and the others who prepared the ashes themselves became unclean until evening (Num 19:7–10), even though the ashes made others clean. Later rabbis regarded it as the supreme example of a commandment whose reason is hidden.

The preacher’s phrase “sprinkled on those who have been defiled” (ῥαντίζουσα τοὺς κεκοινωμένους rhantizousa tous kekoinōmenous) uses the perfect passive participle of κοινόω koinoō, “to make common, profane, defile.” “Common” in Jewish usage meant ritually unclean, unfit for holy use (compare Mark 7:2, “defiled, that is, unwashed, hands”; Acts 10:14, “I have never eaten anything common or unclean”). The perfect tense describes people in a state of defilement. The participle “sprinkling” (rhantizousa) grammatically goes with the ashes, since it was the water of the ashes that was sprinkled.

The result is that these rites “sanctify for the purity of the flesh” (ἁγιάζει πρὸς τὴν τῆς σαρκὸς καθαρότητα hagiazei pros tēn tēs sarkos katharotēta). “Sanctify” here means to restore to a state of holiness that permits participation in worship. “The purity of the flesh” means outward, bodily, ritual cleanness, the realm of the “regulations for the flesh” of 9:10. This was a real benefit: an Israelite defiled by death could once again come to the sanctuary and worship among God’s people. But it was a benefit at the level of the body and the community’s ceremonial life. It did not reach the conscience.

Why does the preacher bring the red heifer in at this point? Probably because of the link between death and defilement, which he will apply to the conscience in 9:14: Christ’s blood cleanses “from dead works.” As contact with a corpse defiled the body and barred a person from worship, so “dead works” defile the conscience and bar a person from serving the living God. Christ’s blood is the true “water of purification.” There may also be a further echo: the heifer was “perfect, in which there is no blemish,” and was slain “outside the camp” (Num 19:2–3), just as Christ offered himself “without blemish” (9:14) and suffered “outside the gate” (13:11–12).

9:14

how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God!

This verse is the summit of the paragraph and one of the great sentences of the Bible. It brings together the person of Christ, the manner of his offering, the Trinity, the purpose of his sacrifice, and its effect in the believer’s life.

“How much more” (πόσῳ μᾶλλον posō mallon). This is the language of the argument from the lesser to the greater, a form of reasoning Jesus himself used (“if you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven…,” Matt 7:11) and which Hebrews employs repeatedly (2:2–3; 10:28–29; 12:9, 25). If the God-appointed animal rites could restore bodily purity, then the self-offering of the Son of God can do incomparably more. The “much more” is not a matter of degree only; it is a difference of kind. Animal blood touched the flesh; Christ’s blood reaches the conscience.

“The blood of Christ” (τὸ αἷμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ to haima tou Christou). In Scripture “blood” in a sacrificial context means life poured out in death. “The life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your lives” (Lev 17:11; see on 9:22). When Hebrews speaks of the blood of Christ, it is not thinking of a substance with magical properties, but of Christ’s life given up in sacrificial death. Why is his blood so much more effective? The rest of the verse gives three reasons: the Spirit through whom he offered it, the willing self-giving with which he offered it, and the perfect sinlessness of the one offered.

“Who … offered himself” (ὃς … ἑαυτὸν προσήνεγκεν hos … heauton prosēnenken). Here is the first great difference. In every Levitical sacrifice, priest and victim were different: a man offered an animal. In Christ’s sacrifice, priest and victim are the same person. He was not merely put to death; he “offered himself.” The animals went to the altar without understanding and without consent; Christ went to the cross knowingly, willingly, out of obedience to the Father and love for his people. Hebrews has already shown his obedience (5:7–8) and will soon quote his words, “Behold, I have come to do your will, O God” (10:7, from Ps 40). Jesus said, “No one takes [my life] from me, but I lay it down of myself” (John 10:18). The aorist “offered” refers to a single, completed act, his death on the cross (compare 9:26, 28; 10:10, 12). Some have argued that in Hebrews Christ’s offering takes place only in heaven, after the ascension. But Hebrews links the offering to his suffering and death (9:26, “to suffer”; 9:28, “offered once to bear sins”; 10:10, “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ”; 13:12, “he suffered outside the gate”). On the Day of Atonement the slaughter of the victim and the presentation of its blood in the sanctuary were two parts of one rite; so Christ’s death on the cross and his entry into heaven are two parts of one sacrifice, of which the death is the decisive center.

“Without blemish” (ἄμωμον amōmon). This is the technical sacrificial term. In the Greek Old Testament ἄμωμος amōmos regularly translates the Hebrew תָּמִים tamim, “whole, perfect, without defect,” the requirement for any sacrificial animal: “If his offering is a burnt offering from the herd, he shall offer a male without blemish” (זָכָר תָּמִים יַקְרִיבֶנּוּ zakhar tamim yaqrivennu, Lev 1:3); and, as we have seen, the red heifer was to be temimah, “without blemish” (Num 19:2). A lame, blind, or diseased animal was an insult to God (Mal 1:8). In animals “without blemish” meant physical soundness. In Christ it means moral and spiritual perfection: he was “tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin” (4:15), “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26). Peter uses the same imagery: “you were redeemed … with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish (amōmos) and without spot” (1 Pet 1:18–19). Only a sinless victim could die for others rather than for his own sins.

“To God” (τῷ θεῷ tō theō). The offering was directed to God the Father. The cross was not only a display of love toward humanity (though it was that); it was first a sacrifice offered to God, satisfying the demands of his holiness and opening the way into his presence.

Interpretive Options: “Through the Eternal Spirit”

The phrase διὰ πνεύματος αἰωνίου dia pneumatos aiōniou, “through [the] eternal Spirit,” has no article in Greek and is found nowhere else in the Bible. Some manuscripts changed “eternal” to “holy,” showing how early readers understood it, but “eternal” is certainly original. Three main interpretations have been offered.

  • The Holy Spirit. Christ offered himself in the power of the Holy Spirit. This is the interpretation of many ancient and modern writers; John Owen gave it careful attention in his work on the Holy Spirit. It fits the consistent New Testament witness that the Spirit empowered Jesus throughout his ministry: the Spirit descended on him at his baptism (Luke 3:22), led him into the wilderness (Luke 4:1), anointed him to preach (Luke 4:18, quoting Isa 61:1), and empowered his works (Matt 12:28; Acts 10:38). It especially fits the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah, of whom God says, נָתַתִּי רוּחִי עָלָיו natatti ruchi alav, “I have put my Spirit upon him” (Isa 42:1), and who “poured out his soul to death” and “bore the sin of many” (Isa 53:12)—the very passage Hebrews will echo in 9:28. F. F. Bruce draws attention to this Servant background. On this reading the Spirit who anointed Jesus for his life of obedience also sustained him in the supreme act of obedience, his self-offering on the cross.
  • Christ’s own divine nature. Others take “eternal spirit” as a description of Christ’s own divine spirit or nature, in contrast to the “flesh.” The offering had eternal value because it was made by one who possessed “the power of an indestructible life” (7:16), who “remains forever” (7:24). Compare Rom 1:3–4, where Christ is “of the seed of David according to the flesh” and “declared Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness,” and 1 Pet 3:18, “put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit.”
  • Christ’s human spirit and inner disposition. A third view takes the phrase to describe the spiritual character of his offering: unlike the animals, who died without will or understanding, Christ offered himself with his whole inner being—willingly, consciously, in perfect devotion. On this view “eternal” marks the offering as belonging to the realm of the eternal and spiritual rather than the bodily and temporary.

Evaluation. The first view is the most probable. The word “spirit” standing alone with a divine attribute (“eternal”) most naturally refers to the Spirit of God, who alone is eternal in the full sense. The lack of an article does not weigh against this; Greek often omits the article in prepositional phrases, and the omission throws weight on the quality “eternal.” Hebrews refers to the Holy Spirit elsewhere in various ways: “the Holy Spirit” (3:7; 9:8; 10:15), “gifts of the Holy Spirit” (2:4), “the Spirit of grace” (10:29). The strong connection between this chapter and the Servant Song of Isaiah 52–53, together with Isa 42:1, makes the Spirit-anointed Servant a natural background. Why “eternal”? Probably to contrast with the temporary, repeated animal sacrifices and the “regulations for the flesh,” and to explain why Christ’s blood secured an eternal redemption (9:12) and an eternal inheritance (9:15): the sacrifice was offered through the eternal Spirit of God, so its effect is eternal.

The second and third views contain real truths—Christ is the eternal Son (1:2–3, 8–12), and his offering was supremely willing and spiritual—but they are better seen as implications of the verse than as its meaning. In any case, the views agree on the vital point: Christ’s death was not merely a physical event but a spiritual act of infinite worth, carried out in the realm of the eternal and therefore of eternal effect.

The first reading also brings out something remarkable: the verse is unmistakably trinitarian. Christ (the Son) offered himself through the eternal Spirit to God (the Father). The work of redemption is the work of the one God, Father, Son, and Spirit, each acting in his own way. The cross was not a transaction in which a loving Son persuaded a reluctant Father, nor an event in which the Spirit was absent; it was the united act of the triune God for our salvation. The same pattern appears in other New Testament summaries of salvation (for example, 1 Pet 1:2; Titus 3:4–6).

“Will purify our conscience” (καθαριεῖ τὴν συνείδησιν ἡμῶν kathariei tēn syneidēsin hēmōn). The verb καθαρίζω katharizō, “to cleanse, purify,” belongs to the language of ritual purity (it is used of cleansing lepers and defiled objects) but is applied here to the inner person. Hebrews opened by saying that the Son “made purification for sins” (1:3). Now the preacher shows where that purification takes effect: in the conscience, the very place the old sacrifices could not reach (9:9). The future tense “will purify” expresses the sure result that follows from the argument (“if this … how much more will that”); it also looks to the ongoing experience of every believer who comes to God through Christ. Some manuscripts read “your conscience” instead of “our conscience”; the difference is slight, but “our” (the text followed here) includes the preacher with his hearers.

To purify the conscience means to remove the guilt that stains it and the accusing awareness of sin that drives a person away from God. It does not mean that believers will never again feel the sting of sin, but that the root of guilt has been dealt with by a sacrifice that God has accepted, so that the believer may draw near “with hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience” (10:22).

“From dead works” (ἀπὸ νεκρῶν ἔργων apo nekrōn ergōn). The same phrase appears in 6:1, where “repentance from dead works” is listed among the foundational teachings. What are “dead works”? Some interpreters, especially in the Reformation tradition, have taken them to include religious works done to earn God’s favor—works that are lifeless because done without faith. Others take them as sinful deeds, works that lead to death (compare Rom 6:21, 23; Jas 1:15). In 6:1 the phrase is the object of “repentance,” which suggests that sinful conduct is primarily in view. Here, following the mention of corpse defilement in 9:13, the image is vivid: as touching a dead body made an Israelite unclean and unfit to worship, so sinful works—works belonging to the realm of death—defile the conscience and make a person unfit to serve the living God. The thought may well include any works, religious or otherwise, done apart from faith and life in God, since all such works belong to the realm of death. Either way, Christ’s blood is the only cleansing for them.

“To serve the living God” (εἰς τὸ λατρεύειν θεῷ ζῶντι eis to latreuein theō zōnti). The construction eis to with an infinitive expresses purpose. The goal of cleansing is not merely relief from guilt but worship. The verb λατρεύω latreuō means to serve God in worship, the priestly service of 9:1, 6, and 9. The old rites could not perfect “the worshiper” (ton latreuonta, 9:9); now all believers become worshipers in the full sense, serving God with cleansed consciences. This service includes praise and thanksgiving, “the fruit of lips that confess his name,” and also doing good and sharing with others, “for with such sacrifices God is well pleased” (13:15–16; compare 12:28).

The contrast between “dead works” and “the living God” is deliberate. Defilement comes from death; worship belongs to life. God is “the living God” (3:12; 10:31; 12:22), the source of all life, and he cannot be served by those still stained with the defilement of death. Christ’s blood moves us from the sphere of death to the sphere of life, from guilt to worship, from distance to access.

This verse speaks directly to the troubled conscience. Many believers carry a lingering sense of guilt for past sins that seems never to go away. They may try to quiet it by religious effort, self-punishment, or forgetfulness. Hebrews points elsewhere: to the blood of the one who offered himself without blemish to God through the eternal Spirit. If God has accepted that sacrifice—and Christ’s presence at his right hand proves that he has (1:3; 10:12)—then the conscience may rest. And a conscience at rest is free to serve.
