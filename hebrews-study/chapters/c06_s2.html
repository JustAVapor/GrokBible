The Certainty of God’s Promise (6:13–20)

Translation

13For when God made his promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself, 14saying,

“Surely, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

15And so, having patiently waited, Abraham obtained the promise. 16For people swear by someone greater than themselves, and for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute. 17In the same way God, wanting to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath, 18so that through two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to take hold of the hope set before us. 19This hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast, and entering into the inner place behind the curtain, 20where Jesus has entered as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

Setting and Structure

The exhortation of 5:11–6:12 ended with a call to imitate “those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises” (6:12). The opening “for” of 6:13 shows that what follows explains and supports that call. The author offers the supreme example of faith and patience, Abraham, and then—more importantly—he lays bare the foundation on which Abraham’s faith and patience rested: the promise of God, confirmed by God’s own oath. The hearers, he insists, stand on that same foundation. Their hope is anchored where Abraham’s was, in the unchangeable purpose of God, and it reaches even further, into the heavenly sanctuary where Jesus has gone ahead of them.

The paragraph unfolds in three steps:

  1. God’s oath to Abraham (6:13–15). Having no one greater to swear by, God swore by himself (Gen 22:16–17). Abraham waited patiently and obtained the promise.
  2. What an oath does, and why God gave one (6:16–18). Among human beings an oath settles every dispute. God, wishing to show the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, added his oath to his promise, so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we might have strong encouragement.
  3. The anchor and the forerunner (6:19–20). This hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, reaching behind the curtain, where Jesus has entered as forerunner, a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

The section is a hinge in the architecture of the whole sermon. Its final words, “a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” repeat Psalm 110:4, which the author had quoted at 5:6 and alluded to at 5:10 just before his long exhortation. He now returns to that point and is ready to expound it in chapter 7. The theme of the divine oath also bridges the two chapters. Psalm 110:4 itself begins, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind,” and in 7:20–22 and 7:28 the author will argue that Jesus’ priesthood is superior precisely because it was established by an oath. The oath to Abraham in chapter 6 prepares the hearers to appreciate the oath to the Messiah in chapter 7.

There is also a deliberate counterpoint to the warning of 6:4–8. There, the author spoke of what is “impossible” for those who fall away (6:4); here he speaks of what is “impossible” for God (6:18). There, the barren land was “near to a curse”; here, God swears to “bless” (6:14). There, the apostates crucify the Son again; here, the Son has entered the sanctuary on our behalf. The warning showed the hearers the abyss; this paragraph shows them the rock. The author is not undoing his warning; he is completing it. The way to avoid falling away is to lay hold of the hope that God has set before us with such overwhelming assurance.

The historical setting gives the language of oaths and anchors special force. In the ancient world, oaths were the solemn guarantee of truthfulness in law courts, treaties, and contracts; they called a higher power to witness and to punish falsehood. Jewish writers wrestled with the question of why God should swear at all; Philo of Alexandria, puzzling over Genesis 22:16, reasoned that God swears by himself because there is nothing greater, and that the oath is given as a help to human weakness rather than because God’s word needs strengthening. Hebrews says something similar but grounds it more warmly in God’s pastoral will “to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose” (6:17). As for anchors, the hearers lived in a Mediterranean world whose commerce depended on ships, and many of them, if they lived in or near Rome, would have seen anchors and sailors in the harbors of Ostia or Puteoli. The image of an anchor holding a ship steady in a storm would have needed no explanation. Within a century or two, the anchor would be one of the most common Christian symbols, carved on tombs in the Roman catacombs as a sign of hope beyond death.

Verse by Verse

6:13

For when God made his promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself,

Abraham is the natural first example of those who “through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12). He is the father of Israel, the first recipient of the covenant promises, and the model believer of both Testaments (Gen 15:6; Rom 4; Gal 3:6–9; Jas 2:21–23). Hebrews will give him the longest treatment of any figure in its gallery of faith (11:8–19). The name stands emphatically at the front of the Greek sentence: Τῷ γὰρ Ἀβραάμ Tō gar Abraam, “For to Abraham ….”

The participle ἐπαγγειλάμενος epangeilamenos, “having promised” or “when he made his promise,” uses the verb from which the noun “promise” (ἐπαγγελία epangelia) comes. Promise is one of the great words of Hebrews (the noun and its verb together occur some eighteen times in the letter). God first spoke his promise to Abraham when he called him from Haran: “I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you and make your name great … and in you all the families of the earth will be blessed” (Gen 12:2–3). He renewed it many times (Gen 13:14–17; 15:1–6; 17:1–8). But the author points specifically to the moment when God confirmed the promise with an oath, after Abraham had offered Isaac on Mount Moriah (Gen 22:15–18).

The reason given for God’s swearing by himself is simple: ἐπεὶ κατ’ οὐδενὸς εἶχεν μείζονος ὀμόσαι epei kat’ oudenos eichen meizonos omosai, “since he had no one greater by whom to swear.” In an oath, a person invokes something greater than himself—a god, the life of the king, heaven, the temple (compare Matt 23:16–22)—as witness and guarantor, calling down that power’s judgment if he speaks falsely. God has no one above him to invoke. So ὤμοσεν καθ’ ἑαυτοῦ ōmosen kath’ heautou, “he swore by himself.” He staked his own being, his own name and nature, on the fulfillment of his word.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:16

Hebrew: בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי נְאֻם־יְהוָה כִּי יַעַן אֲשֶׁר עָשִׂיתָ אֶת־הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה וְלֹא חָשַׂכְתָּ אֶת־בִּנְךָ אֶת־יְחִידֶךָ — bi nishbati neum-YHWH ki yaan asher asita et-haddavar hazzeh velo chasakhta et-binkha et-yechidekha, “By myself I have sworn, declares the Lord: because you have done this thing and have not withheld your son, your only son ….”

This is the only place in Genesis where the narrative records God’s oath in the act of swearing it; later passages look back to it (Gen 24:7; 26:3; 50:24). The phrase bi nishbati, “by myself I have sworn,” places the emphasis on bi, “by me.” The same formula appears in Isaiah 45:23 (בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי bi nishbati, “By myself I have sworn … to me every knee shall bow”) and in Jeremiah 22:5 and 49:13. Elsewhere God swears “by my holiness” (Ps 89:35) or by his own life: “As I live forever” (Deut 32:40). Later Scripture remembers this oath as the bedrock of Israel’s hope: God brought Israel to the land “that I swore to give to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob” (Exod 6:8, where the Hebrew literally says “that I lifted my hand” in oath).

The setting of this oath in Genesis 22 is significant. It came after the supreme test of Abraham’s faith, when he was ready to offer Isaac, the very son through whom the promise was to be fulfilled. Hebrews 11:17–19 will reflect on that moment: Abraham “considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead.” It was precisely when Abraham had shown that he would trust God even against the apparent destruction of the promise that God bound himself by an oath. The oath did not create the promise—it had been given decades earlier—but it confirmed it beyond all possibility of change.

Philo, the Jewish philosopher of Alexandria and an older contemporary of the apostles, also reflected on this verse in his Allegorical Interpretation. He observed that God swears by himself because nothing is greater than God, and that whatever God says is in fact an oath, since his words are always fulfilled. The author of Hebrews shares the first observation, but where Philo moves toward philosophical reflection on the nature of God’s speech, Hebrews moves toward pastoral application: God swore so that we might have strong encouragement (6:18).

6:14

saying, “Surely, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

The author now quotes the content of the oath from Genesis 22:17, in the form found in the Greek Old Testament, with one small but significant change.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:17

Hebrew: כִּי־בָרֵךְ אֲבָרֶכְךָ וְהַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה אֶת־זַרְעֲךָ כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם וְכַחוֹל אֲשֶׁר עַל־שְׂפַת הַיָּם — ki-varekh avarekhka veharbah arbeh et-zarakha kekhokhve hashamayim vekhachol asher al-sefat hayyam, “that I will surely bless you, and I will surely multiply your offspring as the stars of the heavens and as the sand that is on the seashore.”

The Hebrew uses a distinctive construction: an infinitive absolute followed by a finite form of the same verb, barekh avarekhka, literally “blessing I will bless you,” and harbah arbeh, “multiplying I will multiply.” This doubling is a common Hebrew way of adding emphasis and certainty—“I will most certainly bless you.” The Greek translators reproduced the Hebrew idiom closely, using a participle with a finite verb: εὐλογῶν εὐλογήσω σε καὶ πληθύνων πληθυνῶ eulogōn eulogēsō se kai plēthynōn plēthynō. Hebrews quotes these words exactly.

The Greek Old Testament introduces the oath with the strong particle of affirmation ἦ μήν ē mēn, “truly, surely,” which was the standard formula for an oath in Greek. The best text of Hebrews has the variant spelling εἰ μήν ei mēn, which is found in the Hellenistic Greek of the period with the same meaning; some later manuscripts of Hebrews have corrected it to the more familiar spelling. In either form it means “surely, most certainly,” and it marks the words as an oath.

The one change is at the end. Genesis says “I will multiply your offspring (zarakha, your seed)”; Hebrews says “I will multiply you.” The author is not misquoting; he has abbreviated the promise and focused it on Abraham himself, because his point is about Abraham as the one who received the promise and waited for it (6:15). The meaning is the same—Abraham would be multiplied in his descendants—but the personal focus suits the author’s purpose of holding Abraham up as an example to be imitated.

The two verbs—bless and multiply—sum up the promises to Abraham. “Bless” stands first. In Genesis 12:2–3 the word “bless” occurs five times: God will bless Abram, make him a blessing, bless those who bless him, and in him all the families of the earth will be blessed. In Genesis 22:18 the oath continues, “in your offspring all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you obeyed my voice.” The New Testament sees this blessing fulfilled in Christ, the true “offspring” of Abraham (Gal 3:16), through whom “the blessing of Abraham” comes to the Gentiles (Gal 3:14; compare Acts 3:25–26). The hearers of Hebrews, as “offspring of Abraham” whom Christ helps (2:16), are heirs of that blessing.

Notice the link with the parable of 6:7–8. The fruitful land “receives a share of blessing (εὐλογίας eulogias) from God”; now the author shows where that blessing comes from and how certain it is: God has sworn, “Blessing I will bless.” The double expression in the oath answers the doubled warning. Those who are tempted to drift are reminded that God’s promise is not grudging or uncertain but emphatic, doubled, and sworn.

6:15

And so, having patiently waited, Abraham obtained the promise.

This short verse sums up Abraham’s life and applies the lesson of 6:12. “And so” (καὶ οὕτως kai houtōs) means “and in this way”—that is, on the basis of God’s sworn promise. The participle μακροθυμήσας makrothymēsas, “having patiently waited,” is the verb form of the noun “patience” in 6:12. Abraham displayed the very quality the hearers need.

How long did Abraham wait? He was seventy-five years old when he left Haran in response to the promise (Gen 12:4), and he was a hundred when Isaac was born (Gen 21:5). For twenty-five years he lived as a sojourner in the land God had promised, without owning any of it except, at the end, a burial plot (Gen 23), and without the son through whom the promise would come. Those years included failures—his attempts to secure the promise by his own means, through Hagar (Gen 16)—but God was faithful, and Abraham’s faith, though tested, endured. Then, having received Isaac, he was asked to give him back (Gen 22), and he obeyed, trusting God to keep his word even through death (Heb 11:17–19).

“He obtained the promise” (ἐπέτυχεν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας epetychen tēs epangelias). The verb ἐπιτυγχάνω epitynchanō means “attain, obtain, reach.” In what sense did Abraham obtain the promise? Hebrews 11:13 says that the patriarchs “died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them and greeted them from afar,” and 11:39 says that all the heroes of faith “did not receive what was promised.” The answer lies in the different levels of fulfillment. Abraham did obtain the promise in its first installment: he received Isaac, the son of promise, and received him back again on Mount Moriah (11:19, “figuratively speaking, he did receive him back”). He also received the sworn guarantee of the promise’s ultimate fulfillment. But the full inheritance—the city with foundations, the heavenly country (11:10, 16)—awaited the coming of Christ and the consummation, when the faithful of all ages will be “made perfect” together (11:40). Abraham obtained enough to show that God keeps his word, and enough to die in hope of the rest.

The lesson for the hearers is direct. They too are waiting. The promised rest, the city to come, the return of Christ (9:28; 10:37) have not yet arrived, and the delay tempts them to grow weary. Abraham shows that faith and patience are rewarded, not because the waiting is easy, but because the God who promises cannot fail.

6:16

For people swear by someone greater than themselves, and for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute.

The author now explains, from ordinary human practice, why God’s oath matters. “People swear by someone greater” (κατὰ τοῦ μείζονος ὀμνύουσιν kata tou meizonos omnyousin)—that is, they call on a higher authority, usually God, to witness the truth of what they say and to punish them if they lie. Among Israelites the proper oath was by the name of the Lord (Deut 6:13; 10:20); among Greeks and Romans it was by the gods, the emperor’s genius, or other sacred things. The point is universal: an oath appeals upward, beyond the speaker, to a guarantor whose judgment no one can escape.

And “for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute.” Three words carry the thought. ἀντιλογία antilogia, literally “speaking against,” means contradiction, dispute, or opposition. Hebrews uses it again at 7:7 (“without any dispute, the lesser is blessed by the greater”) and 12:3 (Jesus endured such “hostility” from sinners). πέρας peras means “end, limit, boundary”: the oath is the point beyond which argument cannot go. And βεβαίωσις bebaiōsis, “confirmation,” is a legal term. In the Greek papyri from Egypt it is used for the legal guarantee by which a seller warranted a buyer’s title to property; Paul uses it of the “confirmation” of the gospel (Phil 1:7). Its related adjective βέβαιος bebaios, “firm, steadfast,” recurs through Hebrews (2:2; 3:14; 9:17) and will describe the anchor of the soul in 6:19.

Old Testament Text: The Oath That Settles a Dispute (Exod 22:11)

The law of Moses itself used the oath in exactly this way. If a man entrusted an animal to his neighbor and it died or was taken while no one was watching, the dispute was settled by an oath. Hebrew (Exod 22:10 in Hebrew numbering, 22:11 in English Bibles): שְׁבֻעַת יְהוָה תִּהְיֶה בֵּין שְׁנֵיהֶם אִם־לֹא שָׁלַח יָדוֹ בִּמְלֶאכֶת רֵעֵהוּ וְלָקַח בְּעָלָיו וְלֹא יְשַׁלֵּם — shevuat YHWH tihyeh ben shenehem im-lo shalach yado bimlekhet reehu velaqach bealav velo yeshallem, “an oath by the Lord shall be between the two of them, that he has not laid his hand on his neighbor’s property; and its owner shall accept it, and he shall not make restitution.” Once the oath was sworn, the case was closed. No further argument was possible, because the matter had been placed in the hands of God himself.

The author’s argument is from the lesser to the greater, a favorite pattern in Hebrews (2:2–3; 9:13–14; 10:28–29; 12:9, 25). If, among fallible human beings, an oath is accepted as the end of all dispute, how much more should the oath of God, who cannot lie, end every doubt in the hearts of his people? The hearers may have been arguing with themselves—wondering whether the Christian hope was worth the cost, whether God’s promises in Christ would really come to pass, whether they had been foolish to suffer for so uncertain a reward. The author answers: God has sworn. The dispute is closed.

Jesus taught his disciples not to swear oaths in daily speech but to let their “yes” be yes and their “no” be no (Matt 5:33–37; compare Jas 5:12), because oaths are needed only where truthfulness is in doubt. It is all the more striking, then, that God should swear. He does not need an oath to make his word true; his word is already truth itself. He swears because we need it. The oath is a condescension to our weakness, a gift to slow hearts.

6:17

In the same way God, wanting to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath,

The opening phrase ἐν ᾧ en hō, literally “in which,” links this verse to the human practice just described: “in this way,” or “accordingly,” God used the same means that people use to end dispute. The motive is God’s own will: περισσότερον βουλόμενος ὁ θεὸς ἐπιδεῖξαι perissoteron boulomenos ho theos epideixai, “God, wanting to show even more abundantly.” The comparative adverb perissoteron means “more abundantly, more convincingly, beyond what was necessary.” The promise alone would have been enough; God went further. This is the generosity of a Father who knows the frailty of his children and wants them not merely to be safe but to know they are safe.

The recipients of this demonstration are “the heirs of the promise” (τοῖς κληρονόμοις τῆς ἐπαγγελίας tois klēronomois tēs epangelias). This phrase reaches beyond Abraham. In 11:9 Isaac and Jacob are called “fellow heirs of the same promise,” and Paul teaches that “if you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s offspring, heirs according to promise” (Gal 3:29). The oath sworn on Mount Moriah was sworn for every believer who would inherit Abraham’s blessing, including the hearers of this sermon. That is why the author can say in the next verse that “we” have strong encouragement.

Word Study: ἀμετάθετος ametathetos and μεσιτεύω mesiteuō

Ametathetos, “unchangeable,” is formed from the negative prefix a- and the verb μετατίθημι metatithēmi, “to change, transfer, alter.” It describes something that cannot be moved from its place or altered. It was used of decisions and arrangements that could not be revoked. The noun with which it is joined, βουλή boulē, “purpose, counsel, plan,” refers to God’s settled will and design, as in Acts 2:23 (“the definite plan and foreknowledge of God”) and Eph 1:11 (“who works all things according to the counsel of his will”). The phrase “the unchangeableness of his purpose” stands in deliberate contrast to the instability of the hearers. They might waver; God’s purpose does not. The same truth appears in Psalm 33:11 (“the counsel of the Lord stands forever”) and Isaiah 46:10 (“my counsel shall stand”). Hebrews will later say that the Levitical priesthood and the law were subject to “change” (μετάθεσις metathesis, 7:12; 12:27, a noun from the same root); God’s purpose to bless his people in Christ is not.

Mesiteuō, “to mediate, act as guarantor,” is related to μεσίτης mesitēs, “mediator,” the title Hebrews gives to Jesus as “mediator of a better covenant” (8:6; 9:15; 12:24). A mesitēs was a go-between, and sometimes a guarantor who stood surety for an agreement. Here the verb means that God “interposed with an oath,” stepping in as his own guarantor. Since there was no one greater to stand between himself and Abraham, God himself stood in that place, so that he was at once the one who promised and the one who guaranteed the promise. Many commentators draw out this sense: God, as it were, brought himself in as the third party to the transaction.

The author may have another oath in mind as well. Psalm 110:4, which he is about to expound, begins, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind.” In 7:20–22 he will argue that Jesus became priest “not without an oath,” and that this makes him “the guarantor of a better covenant.” The oath to Abraham and the oath to the Messiah belong together. God bound himself by oath to bless Abraham’s offspring, and he bound himself by oath to establish a priest forever who would secure that blessing. Both oaths express the same unchangeable purpose.
