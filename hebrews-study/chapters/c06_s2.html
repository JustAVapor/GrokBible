The Certainty of God’s Promise (6:13–20)

Translation

13For when God made his promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself, 14saying,

“Surely, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

15And so, having patiently waited, Abraham obtained the promise. 16For people swear by someone greater than themselves, and for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute. 17In the same way God, wanting to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath, 18so that through two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to take hold of the hope set before us. 19This hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast, and entering into the inner place behind the curtain, 20where Jesus has entered as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

Setting and Structure

The exhortation of 5:11–6:12 ended with a call to imitate “those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises” (6:12). The opening “for” of 6:13 shows that what follows explains and supports that call. The author offers the supreme example of faith and patience, Abraham, and then—more importantly—he lays bare the foundation on which Abraham’s faith and patience rested: the promise of God, confirmed by God’s own oath. The hearers, he insists, stand on that same foundation. Their hope is anchored where Abraham’s was, in the unchangeable purpose of God, and it reaches even further, into the heavenly sanctuary where Jesus has gone ahead of them.

The paragraph unfolds in three steps:

  1. God’s oath to Abraham (6:13–15). Having no one greater to swear by, God swore by himself (Gen 22:16–17). Abraham waited patiently and obtained the promise.
  2. What an oath does, and why God gave one (6:16–18). Among human beings an oath settles every dispute. God, wishing to show the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, added his oath to his promise, so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we might have strong encouragement.
  3. The anchor and the forerunner (6:19–20). This hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, reaching behind the curtain, where Jesus has entered as forerunner, a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

The section is a hinge in the architecture of the whole sermon. Its final words, “a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” repeat Psalm 110:4, which the author had quoted at 5:6 and alluded to at 5:10 just before his long exhortation. He now returns to that point and is ready to expound it in chapter 7. The theme of the divine oath also bridges the two chapters. Psalm 110:4 itself begins, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind,” and in 7:20–22 and 7:28 the author will argue that Jesus’ priesthood is superior precisely because it was established by an oath. The oath to Abraham in chapter 6 prepares the hearers to appreciate the oath to the Messiah in chapter 7.

There is also a deliberate counterpoint to the warning of 6:4–8. There, the author spoke of what is “impossible” for those who fall away (6:4); here he speaks of what is “impossible” for God (6:18). There, the barren land was “near to a curse”; here, God swears to “bless” (6:14). There, the apostates crucify the Son again; here, the Son has entered the sanctuary on our behalf. The warning showed the hearers the abyss; this paragraph shows them the rock. The author is not undoing his warning; he is completing it. The way to avoid falling away is to lay hold of the hope that God has set before us with such overwhelming assurance.

The historical setting gives the language of oaths and anchors special force. In the ancient world, oaths were the solemn guarantee of truthfulness in law courts, treaties, and contracts; they called a higher power to witness and to punish falsehood. Jewish writers wrestled with the question of why God should swear at all; Philo of Alexandria, puzzling over Genesis 22:16, reasoned that God swears by himself because there is nothing greater, and that the oath is given as a help to human weakness rather than because God’s word needs strengthening. Hebrews says something similar but grounds it more warmly in God’s pastoral will “to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose” (6:17). As for anchors, the hearers lived in a Mediterranean world whose commerce depended on ships, and many of them, if they lived in or near Rome, would have seen anchors and sailors in the harbors of Ostia or Puteoli. The image of an anchor holding a ship steady in a storm would have needed no explanation. Within a century or two, the anchor would be one of the most common Christian symbols, carved on tombs in the Roman catacombs as a sign of hope beyond death.

Verse by Verse

6:13

For when God made his promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself,

Abraham is the natural first example of those who “through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12). He is the father of Israel, the first recipient of the covenant promises, and the model believer of both Testaments (Gen 15:6; Rom 4; Gal 3:6–9; Jas 2:21–23). Hebrews will give him the longest treatment of any figure in its gallery of faith (11:8–19). The name stands emphatically at the front of the Greek sentence: Τῷ γὰρ Ἀβραάμ Tō gar Abraam, “For to Abraham ….”

The participle ἐπαγγειλάμενος epangeilamenos, “having promised” or “when he made his promise,” uses the verb from which the noun “promise” (ἐπαγγελία epangelia) comes. Promise is one of the great words of Hebrews (the noun and its verb together occur some eighteen times in the letter). God first spoke his promise to Abraham when he called him from Haran: “I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you and make your name great … and in you all the families of the earth will be blessed” (Gen 12:2–3). He renewed it many times (Gen 13:14–17; 15:1–6; 17:1–8). But the author points specifically to the moment when God confirmed the promise with an oath, after Abraham had offered Isaac on Mount Moriah (Gen 22:15–18).

The reason given for God’s swearing by himself is simple: ἐπεὶ κατ’ οὐδενὸς εἶχεν μείζονος ὀμόσαι epei kat’ oudenos eichen meizonos omosai, “since he had no one greater by whom to swear.” In an oath, a person invokes something greater than himself—a god, the life of the king, heaven, the temple (compare Matt 23:16–22)—as witness and guarantor, calling down that power’s judgment if he speaks falsely. God has no one above him to invoke. So ὤμοσεν καθ’ ἑαυτοῦ ōmosen kath’ heautou, “he swore by himself.” He staked his own being, his own name and nature, on the fulfillment of his word.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:16

Hebrew: בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי נְאֻם־יְהוָה כִּי יַעַן אֲשֶׁר עָשִׂיתָ אֶת־הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה וְלֹא חָשַׂכְתָּ אֶת־בִּנְךָ אֶת־יְחִידֶךָ — bi nishbati neum-YHWH ki yaan asher asita et-haddavar hazzeh velo chasakhta et-binkha et-yechidekha, “By myself I have sworn, declares the Lord: because you have done this thing and have not withheld your son, your only son ….”

This is the only place in Genesis where the narrative records God’s oath in the act of swearing it; later passages look back to it (Gen 24:7; 26:3; 50:24). The phrase bi nishbati, “by myself I have sworn,” places the emphasis on bi, “by me.” The same formula appears in Isaiah 45:23 (בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי bi nishbati, “By myself I have sworn … to me every knee shall bow”) and in Jeremiah 22:5 and 49:13. Elsewhere God swears “by my holiness” (Ps 89:35) or by his own life: “As I live forever” (Deut 32:40). Later Scripture remembers this oath as the bedrock of Israel’s hope: God brought Israel to the land “that I swore to give to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob” (Exod 6:8, where the Hebrew literally says “that I lifted my hand” in oath).

The setting of this oath in Genesis 22 is significant. It came after the supreme test of Abraham’s faith, when he was ready to offer Isaac, the very son through whom the promise was to be fulfilled. Hebrews 11:17–19 will reflect on that moment: Abraham “considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead.” It was precisely when Abraham had shown that he would trust God even against the apparent destruction of the promise that God bound himself by an oath. The oath did not create the promise—it had been given decades earlier—but it confirmed it beyond all possibility of change.

Philo, the Jewish philosopher of Alexandria and an older contemporary of the apostles, also reflected on this verse in his Allegorical Interpretation. He observed that God swears by himself because nothing is greater than God, and that whatever God says is in fact an oath, since his words are always fulfilled. The author of Hebrews shares the first observation, but where Philo moves toward philosophical reflection on the nature of God’s speech, Hebrews moves toward pastoral application: God swore so that we might have strong encouragement (6:18).

6:14

saying, “Surely, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

The author now quotes the content of the oath from Genesis 22:17, in the form found in the Greek Old Testament, with one small but significant change.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:17

Hebrew: כִּי־בָרֵךְ אֲבָרֶכְךָ וְהַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה אֶת־זַרְעֲךָ כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם וְכַחוֹל אֲשֶׁר עַל־שְׂפַת הַיָּם — ki-varekh avarekhka veharbah arbeh et-zarakha kekhokhve hashamayim vekhachol asher al-sefat hayyam, “that I will surely bless you, and I will surely multiply your offspring as the stars of the heavens and as the sand that is on the seashore.”

The Hebrew uses a distinctive construction: an infinitive absolute followed by a finite form of the same verb, barekh avarekhka, literally “blessing I will bless you,” and harbah arbeh, “multiplying I will multiply.” This doubling is a common Hebrew way of adding emphasis and certainty—“I will most certainly bless you.” The Greek translators reproduced the Hebrew idiom closely, using a participle with a finite verb: εὐλογῶν εὐλογήσω σε καὶ πληθύνων πληθυνῶ eulogōn eulogēsō se kai plēthynōn plēthynō. Hebrews quotes these words exactly.

The Greek Old Testament introduces the oath with the strong particle of affirmation ἦ μήν ē mēn, “truly, surely,” which was the standard formula for an oath in Greek. The best text of Hebrews has the variant spelling εἰ μήν ei mēn, which is found in the Hellenistic Greek of the period with the same meaning; some later manuscripts of Hebrews have corrected it to the more familiar spelling. In either form it means “surely, most certainly,” and it marks the words as an oath.

The one change is at the end. Genesis says “I will multiply your offspring (zarakha, your seed)”; Hebrews says “I will multiply you.” The author is not misquoting; he has abbreviated the promise and focused it on Abraham himself, because his point is about Abraham as the one who received the promise and waited for it (6:15). The meaning is the same—Abraham would be multiplied in his descendants—but the personal focus suits the author’s purpose of holding Abraham up as an example to be imitated.

The two verbs—bless and multiply—sum up the promises to Abraham. “Bless” stands first. In Genesis 12:2–3 the word “bless” occurs five times: God will bless Abram, make him a blessing, bless those who bless him, and in him all the families of the earth will be blessed. In Genesis 22:18 the oath continues, “in your offspring all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you obeyed my voice.” The New Testament sees this blessing fulfilled in Christ, the true “offspring” of Abraham (Gal 3:16), through whom “the blessing of Abraham” comes to the Gentiles (Gal 3:14; compare Acts 3:25–26). The hearers of Hebrews, as “offspring of Abraham” whom Christ helps (2:16), are heirs of that blessing.

Notice the link with the parable of 6:7–8. The fruitful land “receives a share of blessing (εὐλογίας eulogias) from God”; now the author shows where that blessing comes from and how certain it is: God has sworn, “Blessing I will bless.” The double expression in the oath answers the doubled warning. Those who are tempted to drift are reminded that God’s promise is not grudging or uncertain but emphatic, doubled, and sworn.

6:15

And so, having patiently waited, Abraham obtained the promise.

This short verse sums up Abraham’s life and applies the lesson of 6:12. “And so” (καὶ οὕτως kai houtōs) means “and in this way”—that is, on the basis of God’s sworn promise. The participle μακροθυμήσας makrothymēsas, “having patiently waited,” is the verb form of the noun “patience” in 6:12. Abraham displayed the very quality the hearers need.

How long did Abraham wait? He was seventy-five years old when he left Haran in response to the promise (Gen 12:4), and he was a hundred when Isaac was born (Gen 21:5). For twenty-five years he lived as a sojourner in the land God had promised, without owning any of it except, at the end, a burial plot (Gen 23), and without the son through whom the promise would come. Those years included failures—his attempts to secure the promise by his own means, through Hagar (Gen 16)—but God was faithful, and Abraham’s faith, though tested, endured. Then, having received Isaac, he was asked to give him back (Gen 22), and he obeyed, trusting God to keep his word even through death (Heb 11:17–19).

“He obtained the promise” (ἐπέτυχεν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας epetychen tēs epangelias). The verb ἐπιτυγχάνω epitynchanō means “attain, obtain, reach.” In what sense did Abraham obtain the promise? Hebrews 11:13 says that the patriarchs “died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them and greeted them from afar,” and 11:39 says that all the heroes of faith “did not receive what was promised.” The answer lies in the different levels of fulfillment. Abraham did obtain the promise in its first installment: he received Isaac, the son of promise, and received him back again on Mount Moriah (11:19, “figuratively speaking, he did receive him back”). He also received the sworn guarantee of the promise’s ultimate fulfillment. But the full inheritance—the city with foundations, the heavenly country (11:10, 16)—awaited the coming of Christ and the consummation, when the faithful of all ages will be “made perfect” together (11:40). Abraham obtained enough to show that God keeps his word, and enough to die in hope of the rest.

The lesson for the hearers is direct. They too are waiting. The promised rest, the city to come, the return of Christ (9:28; 10:37) have not yet arrived, and the delay tempts them to grow weary. Abraham shows that faith and patience are rewarded, not because the waiting is easy, but because the God who promises cannot fail.

6:16

For people swear by someone greater than themselves, and for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute.

The author now explains, from ordinary human practice, why God’s oath matters. “People swear by someone greater” (κατὰ τοῦ μείζονος ὀμνύουσιν kata tou meizonos omnyousin)—that is, they call on a higher authority, usually God, to witness the truth of what they say and to punish them if they lie. Among Israelites the proper oath was by the name of the Lord (Deut 6:13; 10:20); among Greeks and Romans it was by the gods, the emperor’s genius, or other sacred things. The point is universal: an oath appeals upward, beyond the speaker, to a guarantor whose judgment no one can escape.

And “for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute.” Three words carry the thought. ἀντιλογία antilogia, literally “speaking against,” means contradiction, dispute, or opposition. Hebrews uses it again at 7:7 (“without any dispute, the lesser is blessed by the greater”) and 12:3 (Jesus endured such “hostility” from sinners). πέρας peras means “end, limit, boundary”: the oath is the point beyond which argument cannot go. And βεβαίωσις bebaiōsis, “confirmation,” is a legal term. In the Greek papyri from Egypt it is used for the legal guarantee by which a seller warranted a buyer’s title to property; Paul uses it of the “confirmation” of the gospel (Phil 1:7). Its related adjective βέβαιος bebaios, “firm, steadfast,” recurs through Hebrews (2:2; 3:14; 9:17) and will describe the anchor of the soul in 6:19.

Old Testament Text: The Oath That Settles a Dispute (Exod 22:11)

The law of Moses itself used the oath in exactly this way. If a man entrusted an animal to his neighbor and it died or was taken while no one was watching, the dispute was settled by an oath. Hebrew (Exod 22:10 in Hebrew numbering, 22:11 in English Bibles): שְׁבֻעַת יְהוָה תִּהְיֶה בֵּין שְׁנֵיהֶם אִם־לֹא שָׁלַח יָדוֹ בִּמְלֶאכֶת רֵעֵהוּ וְלָקַח בְּעָלָיו וְלֹא יְשַׁלֵּם — shevuat YHWH tihyeh ben shenehem im-lo shalach yado bimlekhet reehu velaqach bealav velo yeshallem, “an oath by the Lord shall be between the two of them, that he has not laid his hand on his neighbor’s property; and its owner shall accept it, and he shall not make restitution.” Once the oath was sworn, the case was closed. No further argument was possible, because the matter had been placed in the hands of God himself.

The author’s argument is from the lesser to the greater, a favorite pattern in Hebrews (2:2–3; 9:13–14; 10:28–29; 12:9, 25). If, among fallible human beings, an oath is accepted as the end of all dispute, how much more should the oath of God, who cannot lie, end every doubt in the hearts of his people? The hearers may have been arguing with themselves—wondering whether the Christian hope was worth the cost, whether God’s promises in Christ would really come to pass, whether they had been foolish to suffer for so uncertain a reward. The author answers: God has sworn. The dispute is closed.

Jesus taught his disciples not to swear oaths in daily speech but to let their “yes” be yes and their “no” be no (Matt 5:33–37; compare Jas 5:12), because oaths are needed only where truthfulness is in doubt. It is all the more striking, then, that God should swear. He does not need an oath to make his word true; his word is already truth itself. He swears because we need it. The oath is a condescension to our weakness, a gift to slow hearts.

6:17

In the same way God, wanting to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath,

The opening phrase ἐν ᾧ en hō, literally “in which,” links this verse to the human practice just described: “in this way,” or “accordingly,” God used the same means that people use to end dispute. The motive is God’s own will: περισσότερον βουλόμενος ὁ θεὸς ἐπιδεῖξαι perissoteron boulomenos ho theos epideixai, “God, wanting to show even more abundantly.” The comparative adverb perissoteron means “more abundantly, more convincingly, beyond what was necessary.” The promise alone would have been enough; God went further. This is the generosity of a Father who knows the frailty of his children and wants them not merely to be safe but to know they are safe.

The recipients of this demonstration are “the heirs of the promise” (τοῖς κληρονόμοις τῆς ἐπαγγελίας tois klēronomois tēs epangelias). This phrase reaches beyond Abraham. In 11:9 Isaac and Jacob are called “fellow heirs of the same promise,” and Paul teaches that “if you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s offspring, heirs according to promise” (Gal 3:29). The oath sworn on Mount Moriah was sworn for every believer who would inherit Abraham’s blessing, including the hearers of this sermon. That is why the author can say in the next verse that “we” have strong encouragement.

Word Study: ἀμετάθετος ametathetos and μεσιτεύω mesiteuō

Ametathetos, “unchangeable,” is formed from the negative prefix a- and the verb μετατίθημι metatithēmi, “to change, transfer, alter.” It describes something that cannot be moved from its place or altered. It was used of decisions and arrangements that could not be revoked. The noun with which it is joined, βουλή boulē, “purpose, counsel, plan,” refers to God’s settled will and design, as in Acts 2:23 (“the definite plan and foreknowledge of God”) and Eph 1:11 (“who works all things according to the counsel of his will”). The phrase “the unchangeableness of his purpose” stands in deliberate contrast to the instability of the hearers. They might waver; God’s purpose does not. The same truth appears in Psalm 33:11 (“the counsel of the Lord stands forever”) and Isaiah 46:10 (“my counsel shall stand”). Hebrews will later say that the Levitical priesthood and the law were subject to “change” (μετάθεσις metathesis, 7:12; 12:27, a noun from the same root); God’s purpose to bless his people in Christ is not.

Mesiteuō, “to mediate, act as guarantor,” is related to μεσίτης mesitēs, “mediator,” the title Hebrews gives to Jesus as “mediator of a better covenant” (8:6; 9:15; 12:24). A mesitēs was a go-between, and sometimes a guarantor who stood surety for an agreement. Here the verb means that God “interposed with an oath,” stepping in as his own guarantor. Since there was no one greater to stand between himself and Abraham, God himself stood in that place, so that he was at once the one who promised and the one who guaranteed the promise. Many commentators draw out this sense: God, as it were, brought himself in as the third party to the transaction.

The author may have another oath in mind as well. Psalm 110:4, which he is about to expound, begins, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind.” In 7:20–22 he will argue that Jesus became priest “not without an oath,” and that this makes him “the guarantor of a better covenant.” The oath to Abraham and the oath to the Messiah belong together. God bound himself by oath to bless Abraham’s offspring, and he bound himself by oath to establish a priest forever who would secure that blessing. Both oaths express the same unchangeable purpose.

6:18

so that through two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to take hold of the hope set before us.

This verse states the purpose of God’s oath, and it is one of the most comforting sentences in the letter. God swore “so that” (ἵνα hina) we might have strong encouragement.

“Two unchangeable things.” The phrase δύο πραγμάτων ἀμεταθέτων dyo pragmatōn ametathetōn most naturally refers to the two things just mentioned: God’s promise and God’s oath. Either alone would be utterly reliable, since God’s word is true. Together they form a double testimony, and the law required that a matter be established by two witnesses (Deut 19:15). Some interpreters have suggested that the two things are the oath to Abraham (Gen 22:16) and the oath to the Messiah in Psalm 110:4, which the author is about to introduce; but in the flow of 6:13–17 the promise and the oath are the pair that has been under discussion, and most commentators take them so.

“In which it is impossible for God to lie.” Here is the second “impossible” of the chapter: ἀδύνατον ψεύσασθαι θεόν adynaton pseusasthai theon. The first (6:4) closed the door against the apostate; this one closes the door against doubt. The two are not in conflict. The same God whose holiness makes it impossible to renew those who spurn his Son is the God whose truthfulness makes it impossible for him to break his word to those who flee to that Son. The impossibility is not a limitation on God’s power but an expression of his character. He cannot lie because he cannot deny himself (2 Tim 2:13). Paul speaks of “God, who cannot lie” (ὁ ἀψευδὴς θεός ho apseudēs theos, Titus 1:2), and the Old Testament affirms the same truth in Balaam’s oracle:

Old Testament Text: Numbers 23:19

Hebrew: לֹא אִישׁ אֵל וִיכַזֵּב וּבֶן־אָדָם וְיִתְנֶחָם הַהוּא אָמַר וְלֹא יַעֲשֶׂה וְדִבֶּר וְלֹא יְקִימֶנָּה — lo ish el vikhazzev uven-adam veyitnecham hahu amar velo yaaseh vedibber velo yeqimennah, “God is not a man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind. Has he said, and will he not do it? Or has he spoken, and will he not make it stand?”

The two verbs of the first line—“lie” (kazav) and “change his mind” (nicham)—correspond to the two ideas of Hebrews 6:17–18: God’s unchangeable purpose and his inability to lie. The second of these verbs appears again in Psalm 110:4, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind” (וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם velo yinnachem), the very oath Hebrews is about to expound. Samuel says the same: “the Glory of Israel will not lie or change his mind, for he is not a man, that he should change his mind” (1 Sam 15:29).

“Strong encouragement.” The phrase ἰσχυρὰν παράκλησιν ischyran paraklēsin joins a word for strength or might with paraklēsis, a word that means both “encouragement” and “exhortation.” Hebrews calls itself a “word of exhortation” (τοῦ λόγου τῆς παρακλήσεως tou logou tēs paraklēseōs, 13:22), and in 12:5 the same word describes Scripture’s address to us as sons. God’s oath is a strong word spoken to strengthen weak people. It does not merely inform; it emboldens.

“We who have fled for refuge.” The participle οἱ καταφυγόντες hoi kataphygontes, from καταφεύγω katapheugō, means “those who have fled for refuge, taken refuge.” The aorist looks back to the decisive moment of fleeing, the moment of coming to Christ. The image evokes the Old Testament cities of refuge, and the Greek translation of Deuteronomy uses this very verb for the manslayer who “flees” to one of those cities (Deut 4:42; 19:5).

Historical Background: Cities of Refuge and the Horns of the Altar

The law provided six cities of refuge, three on each side of the Jordan, to which a person who had killed another unintentionally could flee from the “avenger of blood,” the dead man’s nearest kinsman (Num 35:9–34; Deut 19:1–13; Josh 20). Hebrew: וְהִקְרִיתֶם לָכֶם עָרִים עָרֵי מִקְלָט תִּהְיֶינָה לָכֶם וְנָס שָׁמָּה רֹצֵחַ מַכֵּה־נֶפֶשׁ בִּשְׁגָגָה — vehiqritem lakhem arim are miqlat tihyenah lakhem venas shammah rotseach makkeh-nefesh bishgagah, “you shall select cities for yourselves; they shall be cities of refuge for you, and the manslayer who strikes a person unintentionally shall flee there” (Num 35:11). Inside the city he was safe; outside it, he was exposed to death. Remarkably, the manslayer was to remain in the city of refuge “until the death of the high priest who was anointed with the holy oil” (עַד־מוֹת הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדֹל ad-mot hakkohen haggadol, Num 35:25); only the high priest’s death set him free to return home.

Another form of refuge in Israel was to flee to the sanctuary and take hold of the horns of the altar, as Adonijah and Joab did (1 Kgs 1:50; 2:28). The refugee literally “took hold” of the place where God met his people.

Hebrews does not develop either picture in detail, and we should not press the details into an allegory. But the language of 6:18–20 fits both strikingly. Believers have fled for refuge; they “take hold” (κρατῆσαι kratēsai) of a hope that reaches into the sanctuary itself; and their safety rests on a high priest. Many readers have reflected that whereas the Old Testament refugee was released by the death of the high priest, the Christian refugee is secured by a high priest who died and now lives forever (7:16, 24–25), so that the refuge never ends.

“To take hold of the hope set before us.” The infinitive κρατῆσαι kratēsai means “to grasp, seize, hold fast.” The same verb appears in 4:14, “let us hold fast the confession.” The hope is “set before us” (τῆς προκειμένης ἐλπίδος tēs prokeimenēs elpidos). The verb πρόκειμαι prokeimai, “to lie before, be set before,” is used again in 12:1–2: “let us run with endurance the race set before us, looking to Jesus … who for the joy set before him endured the cross.” The hope is like a prize placed before the runner, or a refuge open before the fugitive. It is not something the believer creates; it is set before him by God. His part is to flee to it and grasp it.

Notice, finally, the movement of the verse. The hope is objective—“set before us” by God’s promise and oath. The encouragement is strong because its ground is God’s character, not our feelings. And yet the hope must be grasped; the refugee must flee. The author holds together God’s sure provision and the believer’s active laying hold, just as he held together “let us be carried” and “if God permits” in 6:1–3. The person who has fled to Christ, and keeps holding to him, may be strongly encouraged, for the God who swore cannot lie.

6:19

This hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast, and entering into the inner place behind the curtain,

The relative pronoun ἥν hēn, “which,” refers back to “the hope set before us” (6:18). This hope, the author says, “we have as an anchor of the soul” (ὡς ἄγκυραν … τῆς ψυχῆς hōs ankyran … tēs psychēs). The present tense “we have” (ἔχομεν echomen) is a statement of present possession: not “we hope to have,” but “we have.”

Historical Background: Anchors in the Ancient World

For sailors in the ancient Mediterranean, the anchor (ἄγκυρα ankyra) was the difference between safety and shipwreck. The earliest anchors were simply heavy stones; by the Roman period, ships carried anchors of wood with lead stocks or of iron, shaped much like the classic anchor of later times, with arms that bit into the seabed. A large merchant ship carried several. In the account of Paul’s voyage to Rome, the sailors, fearing that they would run aground on rocks in the dark, “let down four anchors from the stern and prayed for day to come” (Acts 27:29); later they cut them away to run the ship ashore (27:40). The scene illustrates exactly what an anchor did: it held a ship steady against wind and current, preventing it from drifting onto the rocks.

Greek and Roman writers often used the anchor as a picture of whatever holds a person steady amid the storms of life, and hope was a natural candidate for that role. Hebrews takes up this familiar image and fills it with new content. In the following centuries the anchor became one of the most common Christian symbols. Clement of Alexandria, around AD 200, advised believers that among suitable images for a signet ring were a dove, a fish, a ship running before the wind, a lyre, and a ship’s anchor (Paedagogus 3.11). Anchors appear frequently on Christian tombs in the Roman catacombs, sometimes shaped to suggest a cross, as a sign of the hope that holds fast beyond death. It is very likely that Hebrews 6:19 is the source of this symbol.

The image fits the hearers’ danger precisely. In 2:1 the author had warned them “lest we drift away” (μήποτε παραρυῶμεν mēpote pararyōmen), a word used of a ship slipping its moorings or being carried past the harbor by the current. Drifting is the characteristic danger of the sluggish (5:11; 6:12). The remedy for drifting is an anchor. And the anchor is not the believer’s own grip, which may be weak, but the hope God has set before him, fastened by God’s promise and oath.

The anchor is described with two adjectives: ἀσφαλῆ τε καὶ βεβαίαν asphalē te kai bebaian, “both sure and steadfast.” Asphalēs (from the negative prefix and a verb meaning “to trip, fall”) means secure, safe, “not liable to slip.” Bebaios means firm, steadfast, reliable—a word that echoes the “confirmation” (bebaiōsis) of the oath in 6:16. The anchor will not drag, and the ground in which it is fixed will not give way. Some interpreters take the adjectives as describing the hope and others the anchor; grammatically both are possible, and the meaning is the same, for the anchor is the hope.

Then the image takes a surprising turn. The anchor is also εἰσερχομένην εἰς τὸ ἐσώτερον τοῦ καταπετάσματος eiserchomenēn eis to esōteron tou katapetasmatos, “entering into the inner place behind the curtain.” An ordinary anchor goes down, out of sight, into the depths of the sea, and grips what the sailors cannot see. This anchor goes up, out of sight, into the heavenly sanctuary, and grips what believers cannot see—the very presence of God. The author has deliberately mixed his metaphors, moving from the harbor to the tabernacle, because no single picture can contain the reality. Our hope holds because it is fastened, not in the shifting circumstances of this world, but in heaven itself, where Christ is.

Old Testament Text: Behind the Curtain (Lev 16:2, 12)

Hebrew: דַּבֵּר אֶל־אַהֲרֹן אָחִיךָ וְאַל־יָבֹא בְכָל־עֵת אֶל־הַקֹּדֶשׁ מִבֵּית לַפָּרֹכֶת אֶל־פְּנֵי הַכַּפֹּרֶת אֲשֶׁר עַל־הָאָרֹן וְלֹא יָמוּת — dabber el-aharon achikha veal-yavo vekhol-et el-haqqodesh mibbet laparokhet el-pene hakkapporet asher al-haaron velo yamut, “Tell Aaron your brother not to come at any time into the holy place inside the curtain, before the mercy seat that is on the ark, so that he may not die” (Lev 16:2).

The phrase mibbet laparokhet, “inside the curtain,” recurs in Leviticus 16:12, where on the Day of Atonement the high priest takes a censer full of coals and two handfuls of incense and “brings it inside the curtain” (וְהֵבִיא מִבֵּית לַפָּרֹכֶת vehevi mibbet laparokhet), so that the cloud of incense covers the mercy seat “so that he does not die” (16:13). The Greek translation of Leviticus renders “inside the curtain” with the same expression Hebrews uses here, ἐσώτερον τοῦ καταπετάσματος esōteron tou katapetasmatos. The καταπέτασμα katapetasma, “curtain,” was the veil that separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (Exod 26:33). Behind it was the ark, the mercy seat, and the presence of God.

For an Israelite, “behind the curtain” was the most inaccessible place on earth. Only the high priest could enter, only on one day of the year, only with blood and incense, and only at the risk of his life. The Mishnah, recording later memories of the Second Temple ritual, says that the high priest did not prolong his prayer in the sanctuary, lest he alarm the people waiting outside (m. Yoma 5:1). Yet Hebrews says that the believer’s hope enters there now. This is the first explicit mention in the letter of the inner sanctuary, the theme that will dominate chapters 9 and 10, where Christ enters “once for all into the holy places” with his own blood (9:12), and believers are invited to enter “by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh” (10:19–20). The anchor image is the first glimpse of that open way.

6:20

where Jesus has entered as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

Why can our hope reach behind the curtain? Because Jesus is already there. ὅπου πρόδρομος ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν εἰσῆλθεν Ἰησοῦς hopou prodromos hyper hēmōn eisēlthen Iēsous, “where as forerunner on our behalf Jesus entered.” The aorist “entered” (εἰσῆλθεν eisēlthen) points to a completed event: Christ’s ascension and entry into the heavenly sanctuary after his sacrifice (compare 4:14, “a great high priest who has passed through the heavens”; 9:12, 24).

Word Study: πρόδρομος prodromos

Prodromos is formed from πρό pro, “before,” and the root of δρόμος dromos, “running, course.” It means “one who runs ahead,” a forerunner or advance-runner. In Greek military writing the prodromoi were scouts or advance cavalry who went ahead of the main army. The Greek Old Testament uses the word of the hornets that God sent ahead of Israel’s army (Wis 12:8) and of an early-ripening fig, the first fruit of the season, which promises the harvest to follow (Isa 28:4). In every use, a forerunner implies others who follow. He goes first, but he does not go alone.

Preachers have sometimes illustrated the word from ancient harbors, describing a small boat that carried a great ship’s anchor through a narrow harbor entrance and fixed it on shore, so that the ship could be drawn safely in. The picture is beautiful and fits the anchor of 6:19, but clear ancient evidence for prodromos as a technical term for such a boat is lacking, and it is wiser not to build on it. The author’s own picture is drawn not from the harbor but from the tabernacle.

And in the tabernacle, the word is revolutionary. The Levitical high priest was never a forerunner. He entered the Most Holy Place alone, and no one could follow him; the people waited outside, sometimes anxiously (compare Luke 1:10, 21). His entry, as Hebrews will say, signified that “the way into the holy places had not yet been disclosed” (9:8). But Jesus enters as prodromos, a forerunner—one whose entry opens the way for others to follow. He is the “pioneer” (ἀρχηγός archēgos) who is “bringing many sons to glory” (2:10), the one who has gone where his people will go, and whose presence there guarantees that they will arrive. The hearers, drifting and discouraged, are told that the one they follow has already reached the harbor, and that their anchor is fixed in him.

He entered ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν hyper hēmōn, “on our behalf.” This phrase gathers up the whole priestly work of Christ. He tasted death “on behalf of everyone” (2:9); he “always lives to intercede on their behalf” (7:25); he entered heaven itself “now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24). His entry is not a private triumph but a representative act. Where he is, there his people are counted to be, and there they will be (John 14:2–3).

The name Ἰησοῦς Iēsous, “Jesus,” comes last in its clause, with emphasis. Hebrews often uses the simple human name at climactic moments (2:9; 3:1; 4:14; 10:19; 12:2; 13:12), reminding the hearers that the one who has entered heaven is the same man who shared their flesh and blood, suffered, and was tempted as they are (2:14–18; 4:15).

Finally, the author completes his long detour and returns to the point he reached at 5:10: Jesus has entered, κατὰ τὴν τάξιν Μελχισέδεκ ἀρχιερεὺς γενόμενος εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα kata tēn taxin Melchisedek archiereus genomenos eis ton aiōna, “having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” The aorist participle genomenos, “having become,” connects his high priesthood with his completed work and exaltation (5:5–10). The word order differs from Psalm 110:4 (“you are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”): Hebrews puts “according to the order of Melchizedek” first and “forever” last. The rearrangement places the emphasis on the final words, eis ton aiōna, “forever.” That is exactly what the anchor image needs. An anchor is only as secure as what it is fixed to. Our hope is fixed to a high priest who will never die, never be replaced, never leave his post (7:16, 23–25). The word “forever” is the last word of the chapter in Greek, and it is the ground of the believer’s security.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 110:4

Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק — nishba YHWH velo yinnachem attah-khohen leolam al-divrati Malki-tsedeq, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: ‘You are a priest forever according to the manner of Melchizedek.’”

The psalm itself is an oath: nishba, “he has sworn.” This explains why the author, having just spoken of God’s oath to Abraham, now returns to this verse. The same God who swore to Abraham swore to the Messiah, and both oaths are irrevocable. The Hebrew al-divrati means “according to the manner (or order) of,” and leolam means “forever.” Melchizedek (Malki-tsedeq, “my king is righteousness” or “king of righteousness”) was the priest-king of Salem who blessed Abraham (Gen 14:18–20). The next chapter will explain why his “order” is superior to Aaron’s, and how Jesus fulfills it. With this verse the author has led his hearers back to the threshold of that teaching, now awake and anchored, ready for solid food.

For Reflection

  1. God did not need to swear an oath, yet he did so “to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose” (6:17). What does this reveal about God’s heart toward people who struggle to believe him?
  2. Abraham “having patiently waited, obtained the promise” (6:15). What promises of God are you waiting for, and how does Abraham’s long patience help you wait?
  3. The chapter contains two “impossibles”: it is impossible to renew the apostate (6:4–6), and it is impossible for God to lie (6:18). How do these two truths work together to keep a believer both humble and secure?
  4. The author describes believers as those “who have fled for refuge” (6:18). In what sense is the Christian life a continual fleeing to Christ? What tempts you to seek refuge elsewhere?
  5. Your hope is an anchor that enters “behind the curtain,” fastened where Jesus has gone as forerunner (6:19–20). In the storms you face now, what difference does it make that your security rests on where Christ is rather than on how you feel?
