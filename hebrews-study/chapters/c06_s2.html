The Certainty of God’s Promise (6:13–20)

Translation

13For when God made his promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself, 14saying,

“Surely, blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

15And so, having patiently waited, Abraham obtained the promise. 16For people swear by someone greater than themselves, and for them the oath, given as confirmation, is an end to every dispute. 17In the same way God, wanting to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath, 18so that through two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to take hold of the hope set before us. 19This hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast, and entering into the inner place behind the curtain, 20where Jesus has entered as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

Setting and Structure

The exhortation of 5:11–6:12 ended with a call to imitate “those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises” (6:12). The opening “for” of 6:13 shows that what follows explains and supports that call. The author offers the supreme example of faith and patience, Abraham, and then—more importantly—he lays bare the foundation on which Abraham’s faith and patience rested: the promise of God, confirmed by God’s own oath. The hearers, he insists, stand on that same foundation. Their hope is anchored where Abraham’s was, in the unchangeable purpose of God, and it reaches even further, into the heavenly sanctuary where Jesus has gone ahead of them.

The paragraph unfolds in three steps:

  1. God’s oath to Abraham (6:13–15). Having no one greater to swear by, God swore by himself (Gen 22:16–17). Abraham waited patiently and obtained the promise.
  2. What an oath does, and why God gave one (6:16–18). Among human beings an oath settles every dispute. God, wishing to show the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose, added his oath to his promise, so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we might have strong encouragement.
  3. The anchor and the forerunner (6:19–20). This hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, reaching behind the curtain, where Jesus has entered as forerunner, a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

The section is a hinge in the architecture of the whole sermon. Its final words, “a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” repeat Psalm 110:4, which the author had quoted at 5:6 and alluded to at 5:10 just before his long exhortation. He now returns to that point and is ready to expound it in chapter 7. The theme of the divine oath also bridges the two chapters. Psalm 110:4 itself begins, “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind,” and in 7:20–22 and 7:28 the author will argue that Jesus’ priesthood is superior precisely because it was established by an oath. The oath to Abraham in chapter 6 prepares the hearers to appreciate the oath to the Messiah in chapter 7.

There is also a deliberate counterpoint to the warning of 6:4–8. There, the author spoke of what is “impossible” for those who fall away (6:4); here he speaks of what is “impossible” for God (6:18). There, the barren land was “near to a curse”; here, God swears to “bless” (6:14). There, the apostates crucify the Son again; here, the Son has entered the sanctuary on our behalf. The warning showed the hearers the abyss; this paragraph shows them the rock. The author is not undoing his warning; he is completing it. The way to avoid falling away is to lay hold of the hope that God has set before us with such overwhelming assurance.

The historical setting gives the language of oaths and anchors special force. In the ancient world, oaths were the solemn guarantee of truthfulness in law courts, treaties, and contracts; they called a higher power to witness and to punish falsehood. Jewish writers wrestled with the question of why God should swear at all; Philo of Alexandria, puzzling over Genesis 22:16, reasoned that God swears by himself because there is nothing greater, and that the oath is given as a help to human weakness rather than because God’s word needs strengthening. Hebrews says something similar but grounds it more warmly in God’s pastoral will “to show even more fully to the heirs of the promise the unchangeableness of his purpose” (6:17). As for anchors, the hearers lived in a Mediterranean world whose commerce depended on ships, and many of them, if they lived in or near Rome, would have seen anchors and sailors in the harbors of Ostia or Puteoli. The image of an anchor holding a ship steady in a storm would have needed no explanation. Within a century or two, the anchor would be one of the most common Christian symbols, carved on tombs in the Roman catacombs as a sign of hope beyond death.
