4:3

For we who have believed are entering that rest, just as he has said, “As I swore in my wrath, ‘They shall not enter my rest,’” although his works were finished from the foundation of the world.

Here the exposition proper begins. Verse 2 said that hearing without faith brought Israel no benefit; verse 3 states the positive counterpart: “For we who have believed are entering that rest.” The contrast with 3:19 is deliberate. “They were not able to enter because of unbelief”; “we who have believed are entering.” Faith is the dividing line. The participle οἱ πιστεύσαντες hoi pisteusantes, “those who have believed,” is in the aorist tense, which views the act of believing as a decisive whole: those who have put their trust in God’s promise. It describes the people, not a single moment; Hebrews will insist that true faith endures (3:14; 10:38–39).

The main verb is εἰσερχόμεθα eiserchometha, “we are entering.” It is in the present tense, and it comes first in the sentence for emphasis. Interpreters have long debated what this present tense implies.

Interpretive Options: When do believers enter the rest?

  • A present reality. Believers enter God’s rest now, by faith in Christ. The present tense means “we are (already) entering.” Jesus’ invitation, “Come to me … and I will give you rest” (Matt 11:28), is often cited in support.
  • A future hope. The rest is the heavenly homeland entered at the end of the pilgrimage. The present tense is “futuristic,” expressing certainty: “we are as good as entering,” or “we are the ones who will enter.” Support comes from 4:1 (the promise is still open), 4:9 (the rest “remains”), and 4:11 (“let us strive to enter”), as well as from Hebrews’ picture of believers as pilgrims seeking a city to come (11:13–16; 13:14).
  • Already and not yet. Believers have truly begun to enter, yet the full possession of the rest lies ahead. The present tense describes a process under way: “we are in the act of entering.”

The third view does most justice to the whole of Hebrews. The sermon regularly speaks of the blessings of the age to come as both present and future. Believers “have tasted … the powers of the age to come” (6:5); they “have come” (perfect tense) to Mount Zion and the heavenly Jerusalem (12:22); they already “have” a hope that enters behind the curtain (6:19) and boldness to enter the sanctuary (10:19). Yet they still “seek the city that is to come” (13:14) and must endure in order to “receive what is promised” (10:36). Many commentators, including Lane and Bruce, rightly hold these two sides together. The rest is God’s, and it is entered by faith; faith lays hold of it now, as a pledge and foretaste, and faith perseveres until it is entered fully. To deny the present aspect would empty “we who have believed are entering” of its comfort; to deny the future aspect would empty “let us strive to enter” (4:11) of its urgency.

The preacher then supports his claim from the psalm: “just as he has said” (καθὼς εἴρηκεν kathōs eirēken). The verb is in the perfect tense—“he has said, and it stands said.” God’s oath in Psalm 95 is not a dead record; it remains in force. But how does an oath of exclusion prove that believers enter? The logic is this: God swore that the unbelieving would not enter “my rest.” The oath therefore assumes that God has a rest, that it is meant to be entered, and that unbelief is what bars the way. If unbelief excludes, faith is the way in. The negative oath implies the positive promise.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 95:11

Hebrew: אֲשֶׁר־נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי בְאַפִּי אִם־יְבֹאוּן אֶל־מְנוּחָתִי — asher-nishba‘ti ve’appi im-yevo’un el-menuchati, literally, “so that I swore in my anger, ‘If they shall enter into my rest …!’”

The Hebrew uses a standard oath formula. A person swearing an oath would say, “If I do such-and-such …,” leaving unspoken a self-curse such as “may God punish me.” The effect is a strong negative: “They shall certainly not enter.” The Greek of the Septuagint (Ps 94:11 in its numbering), which Hebrews quotes exactly, imitates the Hebrew literally: Εἰ εἰσελεύσονται ei eiseleusontai, “If they shall enter.” A Greek reader unfamiliar with the idiom would find it odd; a reader steeped in the Greek Old Testament would know it as the language of a solemn oath. The noun “my rest” is מְנוּחָתִי menuchati, from menuchah, “resting place, rest,” a word used for settled security in the land (Deut 12:9) and for God’s dwelling in Zion (Ps 132:14). The Septuagint renders it with κατάπαυσις katapausis, the word Hebrews builds its argument upon.

The background is the oath of Numbers 14:21–23, 28–30, where the Lord swore by his own life that the men who had seen his glory and signs and yet tested him ten times would not see the land. In Numbers the “rest” is the land of Canaan. Psalm 95, by calling it “my rest,” already points beyond the land to the rest that belongs to God himself.

The verse ends with a short but crucial clause: “although his works were finished from the foundation of the world.” The Greek is a genitive absolute, a construction that adds a circumstance alongside the main thought: καίτοι τῶν ἔργων ἀπὸ καταβολῆς κόσμου γενηθέντων kaitoi tōn ergōn apo katabolēs kosmou genēthentōn. The conjunction kaitoi, “and yet, although,” signals a point that must be held in tension with what precedes. God swore they would not enter—and yet his rest was not something still under construction. His works stood complete from the beginning. The word καταβολή katabolē means a “laying down,” as of a foundation, and “the foundation of the world” is a biblical way of speaking of creation (9:26; Matt 25:34; Eph 1:4).

Why does this matter? Because it shows that the rest of Psalm 95 is not merely Canaan, which Israel had not yet entered in the wilderness. It is God’s own rest, which has existed ever since he completed creation. Israel’s exclusion was not because the rest was not ready; it was ready from the start. The failure lay entirely on the human side, in unbelief. Jesus speaks in a similar way of the kingdom “prepared for you from the foundation of the world” (Matt 25:34). God’s gifts are not afterthoughts. The rest into which he calls his people was prepared before they were born, and it waits only to be entered by faith. The next verse supplies the scriptural proof for this clause.

4:4

For he has spoken somewhere concerning the seventh day in this way: “And God rested on the seventh day from all his works.”

To prove that God’s works were finished and his rest begun at creation, the preacher quotes Genesis 2:2. He introduces it with the words “he has spoken somewhere” (εἴρηκεν γάρ που eirēken gar pou). The little word που pou, “somewhere,” has puzzled some readers, since the author surely knew that the words come from the first chapters of Genesis. He used the same expression in 2:6 (“someone has testified somewhere”), introducing Psalm 8. The vagueness is not ignorance but emphasis. For the author, the human location of a text is less important than its divine speaker. The subject of “has spoken” is God, as in 4:3 and 4:7. Scripture is God’s speech, and what matters is that God said it. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish contemporary, can introduce well-known texts in the same way, so the expression would not have sounded careless to ancient ears.

The quotation itself is brief: “And God rested on the seventh day from all his works.” The verb is κατέπαυσεν katepausen, the verb cognate with katapausis, “rest.” This verbal link is the hinge of the argument. Psalm 95 speaks of “my rest” (katapausis); Genesis 2:2 says God “rested” (katepausen). Laying the two texts together, the preacher concludes that “my rest” in the psalm is the rest God entered on the seventh day.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 2:2

Hebrew: וַיְכַל אֱלֹהִים בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי מְלַאכְתּוֹ אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה וַיִּשְׁבֹּת בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי מִכָּל־מְלַאכְתּוֹ אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה — wayekhal elohim bayyom hashevi‘i melakhto asher ‘asah, wayyishbot bayyom hashevi‘i mikkol-melakhto asher ‘asah, “And on the seventh day God completed his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work that he had done.”

The Hebrew verb for “rested” is וַיִּשְׁבֹּת wayyishbot, from shavat, “to cease, stop, rest,” the root of the word “Sabbath.” It stresses cessation: God stopped his work of creating because it was finished. The Septuagint translates it with κατέπαυσεν katepausen, and Hebrews quotes essentially the Septuagint wording, adding the subject “God” to make the sense plain. (In the first half of the verse, the Septuagint reads that God finished his works “on the sixth day,” smoothing a difficulty in the Hebrew; Hebrews does not quote that clause.)

Is the link between Genesis 2:2 and Psalm 95:11 only a matter of the Greek translation? No. In Hebrew the two texts use different roots (shavat in Genesis, nuach/menuchah in the psalm), but the Hebrew Bible itself joins them. The Sabbath commandment in Exodus 20:11 explains God’s seventh-day rest with the verb nuach: וַיָּנַח בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי wayyanach bayyom hashevi‘i, “and he rested on the seventh day.” Exodus 31:17 uses both ideas: וּבַיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי שָׁבַת וַיִּנָּפַשׁ uvayyom hashevi‘i shavat wayyinnafash, “and on the seventh day he ceased and was refreshed.” So the connection Hebrews draws between God’s creation rest and the menuchah of Psalm 95 is grounded in the Hebrew Scriptures themselves, not merely in a convenient Greek word.

What is God’s rest? It is not weariness relieved. Isaiah declares that “the everlasting God, the Lord, the Creator of the ends of the earth, does not grow faint or weary” (Isa 40:28). Nor is it idleness. Jesus said, “My Father is working until now, and I am working” (John 5:17), and Hebrews itself says the Son “upholds all things by the word of his power” (1:3). Philo likewise insisted that God never ceases to act. God’s rest is the rest of completion and satisfaction: the work is finished, it is “very good” (Gen 1:31), and God now enjoys and reigns over what he has made. Scripture connects God’s rest with his dwelling and reign. Solomon’s prayer at the dedication of the temple and Psalm 132 call on God to arise to his “resting place” (2 Chr 6:41; Ps 132:8), and God says of Zion, “This is my resting place forever; here I will dwell” (Ps 132:14). Through Isaiah he asks, “Where is the place of my rest?” since heaven is his throne and earth his footstool (Isa 66:1). God’s rest, then, is his settled, joyful, sovereign presence in the midst of a finished creation.

One further feature of Genesis was noticed by early Christian readers. Each of the first six days ends with the refrain “and there was evening and there was morning,” but the seventh day does not. Augustine, at the close of his Confessions, observed that the seventh day has no evening, and he took it as a sign of a rest without end. Hebrews does not make that argument, and it should not be pressed as if Genesis were written to teach it. But it is in harmony with what Hebrews says: God’s seventh-day rest began at creation and has never ended. That is why it can still be offered “Today.”

4:5

And again in this passage: “They shall not enter my rest.”

Having quoted Genesis 2:2, the preacher immediately sets Psalm 95:11 beside it once more: “And again in this passage” (καὶ ἐν τούτῳ πάλιν kai en toutō palin, literally “and in this again”). “This” refers to the psalm already under discussion. The juxtaposition is the argument. In one text, God rests from his works on the seventh day. In the other, God speaks of “my rest” and swears that the rebels will not enter it. Put together, they show that the rest God offered and withheld is his own seventh-day rest.

The word “my” deserves to be weighed. God does not merely promise to give his people a rest of their own, though he does give them rest from their enemies. He speaks of inviting creatures into the rest that is his. This is one of the most astonishing ideas in Hebrews. The goal of redemption is not simply relief from trouble but fellowship with God in his own joy, sharing the satisfaction with which he looks on his finished work. Hebrews will later describe the destination of believers as the city of the living God, where myriads of angels are in festal gathering and the spirits of the righteous are made perfect (12:22–23). The rest of God is life in his presence.

The repetition of the oath also keeps the warning sharp. Every time the preacher says “my rest,” he repeats the words of exclusion: “They shall not enter.” The rest is real and open; but it is God’s rest, and he has sworn that unbelief will not enter it. The same text that holds out the promise also guards its entrance. The hearers must not take the open door lightly.

Taken together, verses 3–5 establish three things. First, the rest exists and has existed since creation (4:3b–4). Second, it is God’s own rest (4:5). Third, the wilderness generation did not enter it (4:5, recalling 3:18–19). From these premises the preacher will draw his conclusion in verses 6–9.

4:6

Since therefore it remains for some to enter it, and those who formerly had the good news proclaimed to them did not enter because of disobedience,

Verses 6–7 form one sentence. Verse 6 states two premises; verse 7 states what God did in response. The first premise is that “it remains for some to enter it” (ἀπολείπεται τινὰς εἰσελθεῖν εἰς αὐτήν apoleipetai tinas eiselthein eis autēn). The verb ἀπολείπω apoleipō in the passive means “to be left over, to remain.” Why must it remain for some to enter? Because God has a rest, and he did not prepare it for no one. God’s purpose in creation was that his creatures should share his rest. That purpose cannot be finally frustrated by human unbelief. As Paul asks, “Will their unbelief nullify the faithfulness of God? By no means!” (Rom 3:3–4). If the first hearers did not enter, others will.

The second premise repeats the sad fact: “those who formerly had the good news proclaimed to them did not enter because of disobedience.” The verb again is euangelizō, “to announce good news” (compare 4:2). The wilderness generation were the first to hear the promise of rest, and they did not go in. The cause is named ἀπείθεια apeitheia, “disobedience.”

Word Study: ἀπείθεια apeitheia and ἀπιστία apistia

In 3:19 the reason Israel did not enter was apistia, “unbelief.” In 4:6 and 4:11 it is apeitheia, “disobedience” (compare the verb in 3:18, “those who disobeyed”). The two words are closely related in Greek. Apeitheia comes from a root meaning “to be persuaded,” so it describes a refusal to be persuaded that shows itself in a refusal to obey. Hebrews uses the words almost interchangeably because, in Scripture, unbelief and disobedience are two sides of one coin. Unbelief is disobedience at the root; disobedience is unbelief in action.

The Old Testament account says the same. Moses reminded Israel, וַתַּמְרוּ אֶת־פִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם וְלֹא הֶאֱמַנְתֶּם לוֹ וְלֹא שְׁמַעְתֶּם בְּקֹלוֹ wattamru et-pi YHWH eloheikhem welo he’emantem lo welo shema‘tem beqolo, “you rebelled against the command [literally ‘mouth’] of the Lord your God, and you did not believe him, and you did not listen to his voice” (Deut 9:23). In one sentence Moses joins rebellion, unbelief, and refusal to hear God’s voice—exactly the elements of Psalm 95 (“Today, if you hear his voice”). Similarly in Numbers 14:11 the Lord asks, “How long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs that I have done among them?”

This pairing guards against two errors. It shows that faith is not mere intellectual agreement; genuine trust in God’s promise produces obedience to his word (compare 5:9, where Christ is the source of salvation “for all who obey him”). And it shows that obedience is not a rival to faith but its fruit. The wilderness generation did not fail because they lacked moral effort but because they did not trust the God who had promised.

The word “formerly” (πρότερον proteron) quietly implies a contrast: there were “former” hearers, and there are present ones. The preacher is setting the stage for the “Today” of verse 7. One generation’s failure did not cancel God’s offer; it only passed it to another generation.

The same verb “remains” (apoleipetai) will appear again in 4:9, “a Sabbath-rest remains,” and once more in 10:26, in one of Hebrews’ gravest warnings: for those who go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.” The echo is sobering. Something remains for the people of God: a rest. But for those who finally reject the only sacrifice, nothing remains but judgment. The promise and the warning are spoken in the same breath.
