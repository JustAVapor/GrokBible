9:23

It was necessary, then, for the copies of the things in the heavens to be purified with these, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these.

With “then” (οὖν oun) the preacher draws a conclusion from 9:18–22 and begins the final movement of the chapter. The verse is built on a balanced contrast (μέν … δέ men … de):

  • the copies of the things in the heavens — purified with these (animal blood and the rites just described);
  • the heavenly things themselves — purified with better sacrifices than these.

“It was necessary” (Ἀνάγκη anankē, “necessity”) picks up the “must” of 9:16. Within God’s ordering of things, purification by sacrifice was not optional.

“The copies of the things in the heavens” (τὰ ὑποδείγματα τῶν ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς ta hypodeigmata tōn en tois ouranois). A ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma is a copy, sketch, or outline. In 8:5 the preacher said the Levitical priests serve “a copy and shadow of the heavenly things,” quoting God’s instruction to Moses: וּרְאֵה וַעֲשֵׂה בְּתַבְנִיתָם אֲשֶׁר־אַתָּה מָרְאֶה בָּהָר ure’eh va’aseh betavnitam asher-attah mar’eh bahar, “See, and make them according to their pattern that you are being shown on the mountain” (Exod 25:40). The earthly tabernacle and its furnishings were copies of heavenly realities; they were purified “with these,” that is, with the blood of animals, water, scarlet wool, and hyssop.

“But the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these.” This is a startling statement. If the earthly copies needed purification, so did “the heavenly things themselves” (αὐτὰ τὰ ἐπουράνια auta ta epourania). Why would anything in heaven need to be purified? The plural “better sacrifices” (κρείττοσι θυσίαις kreittosi thysiais) is a generic plural, matching the plural “these” in the first half of the sentence; the preacher knows only one sacrifice of Christ (9:26, 28; 10:12, 14). “Better” (κρείττων kreittōn) is one of the great words of Hebrews: a better hope (7:19), a better covenant (7:22; 8:6), better promises (8:6), and now better sacrifices.

Interpretive Options: Why Do the Heavenly Things Need Purifying?

  • Heaven defiled by sin. Some suggest that sin, whether human or the rebellion of evil spirits, in some way defiled the heavenly realm itself, so that it needed cleansing (compare the accuser in heaven, Job 1:6; Rev 12:7–10). But Hebrews nowhere speaks of heaven itself as polluted, and God’s own dwelling is presented as the realm of perfect holiness. This view goes beyond what the text says.
  • Inauguration or consecration. Others take “purify” here in the sense of “consecrate” or “inaugurate”: as the earthly tabernacle was dedicated with blood when it began to be used (9:18–21), so the heavenly sanctuary was inaugurated for the worship of the new covenant people by Christ’s sacrifice. This fits the immediately preceding context of inauguration (9:18, “inaugurated”) and 10:20 (Jesus “inaugurated” a new and living way).
  • The removal of the barrier caused by human sin. Others point to Lev 16:16: the earthly sanctuary needed atonement “because of the uncleannesses of the sons of Israel,” since it dwelt “in the midst of their uncleannesses.” It was not the sanctuary’s own fault; it was defiled by its association with a sinful people. In the same way, the heavenly “things” are purified, not because heaven was sinful, but because sinful people are to be brought into God’s presence there. Christ’s sacrifice removes the guilt that would otherwise make their entry a defilement of God’s holy dwelling. The heavenly sanctuary is thus made fit to receive sinners as worshipers.
  • The people themselves as God’s dwelling. Some interpreters suggest that “the heavenly things” refers to the people of God, who are God’s “house” (3:6) and who have already come to the heavenly Jerusalem (12:22–23); what is cleansed is their conscience (9:14; 10:22).

The best explanation combines the second and third views, which both arise from the Old Testament pattern the preacher has been expounding. On the Day of Atonement the sanctuary was “atoned for” and “cleansed” (Lev 16:16, 19, 33) because of Israel’s sins, so that the holy God could go on dwelling among them; and at its founding the tabernacle was consecrated with blood (Lev 8:15). In the true sanctuary, Christ’s sacrifice both inaugurates the new covenant worship and deals with the sin that would otherwise bar sinful human beings from God’s presence. Heaven is not cleansed from its own impurity; it is opened to and made ready for a redeemed people. The fourth view rightly emphasizes that it is our sins that create the need (9:14; 10:22), but the language “the heavenly things themselves,” paralleled with “the copies,” points to the sanctuary rather than directly to the worshipers. In short, the preacher is saying that Christ’s death accomplished in reality, in the very presence of God, what the Levitical blood rites accomplished in symbol in the earthly copy.

The comfort here is profound. Sinners will not enter heaven by slipping in unnoticed, tolerated in a holy place they defile. They enter as those for whom the sanctuary itself has been prepared by a sacrifice that God has accepted. Jesus said, “In my Father’s house are many rooms…. I go to prepare a place for you” (John 14:2).

9:24

For Christ did not enter holy places made with hands, copies of the true ones, but into heaven itself, now to appear before the face of God on our behalf.

This verse explains (“for”) why “better sacrifices” were needed: Christ’s ministry takes place not in an earthly copy but in heaven itself.

“Holy places made with hands” (χειροποίητα … ἅγια cheiropoiēta … hagia) recalls 9:11 (“not made with hands”). “Copies of the true ones” (ἀντίτυπα τῶν ἀληθινῶν antitypa tōn alēthinōn): the word ἀντίτυπος antitypos means “corresponding to,” and here “a copy, a counterpart.” In later Christian vocabulary “antitype” came to mean the fulfillment of which an Old Testament “type” was the foreshadowing; but here, in Hebrews, the earthly sanctuary is the antitypos, the copy, and the heavenly is the original. “The true ones” (ἀληθινός alēthinos) means “real, genuine,” the ultimate reality as opposed to its representation, as in 8:2, “the true tabernacle which the Lord set up, not man.”

“But into heaven itself” (ἀλλ’ εἰς αὐτὸν τὸν οὐρανόν all’ eis auton ton ouranon). Here the “true sanctuary” is identified plainly: it is heaven itself, the dwelling place of God. Christ did not enter a symbol of God’s presence; he entered the presence of God.

“Now to appear before the face of God” (νῦν ἐμφανισθῆναι τῷ προσώπῳ τοῦ θεοῦ nyn emphanisthēnai tō prosōpō tou theou). The verb ἐμφανίζω emphanizō means “to make visible, to show plainly,” and in the passive, “to appear, to present oneself.” The phrase “before the face of God” is a Hebrew way of speaking of God’s immediate presence. In Israel, all the men were to “appear before the face of the Lord” at the three great festivals: שָׁלֹשׁ פְּעָמִים בַּשָּׁנָה יֵרָאֶה כָּל־זְכוּרְךָ אֶל־פְּנֵי הָאָדֹן יְהוָה shalosh pe’amim bashshanah yera’eh kol-zekhurkha el-pene ha’adon YHWH, “Three times in the year all your males shall appear before the face of the Lord, the Lord” (Exod 23:17). The psalmist longed for this: מָתַי אָבוֹא וְאֵרָאֶה פְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים matay avo ve’era’eh pene elohim, “When shall I come and appear before the face of God?” (Ps 42:3 in Hebrew; 42:2 in English). But even at the festivals, the worshiper came only to the courtyard. To see God’s face itself was impossible for sinful humanity: “You cannot see my face, for no human can see me and live” (Exod 33:20). Even the high priest entered the inner room shrouded in a cloud of incense, “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:13).

Christ, by contrast, appears openly and directly “before the face of God.” No cloud of incense shields him; no fear of death hangs over him. He enters as the Son, welcomed by the Father, seated at his right hand (1:3, 13). And he appears there “on our behalf” (ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν hyper hēmōn). He is our representative, the high priest who bears his people’s names before God, as Aaron bore the names of the twelve tribes on his shoulders and over his heart when he went into the Holy Place (Exod 28:12, 29). He “always lives to intercede for them” (7:25). Paul says the same: Christ Jesus “is at the right hand of God, who indeed intercedes for us” (Rom 8:34); John writes, “we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” (1 John 2:1).

The word “now” (νῦν nyn) marks this as Christ’s present ministry. The cross is past, the return is future, and between them stands the present reality of Christ before the face of God for us. This is what makes the Christian life possible. Believers can approach “the throne of grace” with boldness (4:16) because their high priest is already there, appearing for them. When a believer prays, he or she does not pray alone; Christ stands in the presence of the Father on that believer’s behalf.

9:25

Nor did he enter in order to offer himself repeatedly, as the high priest enters the holy places year by year with blood not his own,

The sentence continues the contrast of 9:24: Christ did not enter a hand-made sanctuary (9:24a), “nor” (οὐδ’ oud’) did he enter in order to repeat his offering (9:25). The preacher now focuses on the second great difference between Christ and the Levitical high priest: not only the place of his ministry but its finality.

“To offer himself repeatedly” (ἵνα πολλάκις προσφέρῃ ἑαυτόν hina pollakis prospherē heauton). πολλάκις pollakis means “often, many times.” The high priest’s ministry was characterized by repetition: the same entry, the same blood, the same ritual, “year by year” (κατ’ ἐνιαυτόν kat’ eniauton). Hebrews will later argue that this very repetition proved the ineffectiveness of the old sacrifices: “if they had been able to perfect the worshipers, would they not have ceased to be offered?” (10:1–2). Christ’s offering, by contrast, is never repeated.

“With blood not his own” (ἐν αἵματι ἀλλοτρίῳ en haimati allotriō). ἀλλότριος allotrios means “belonging to another, foreign.” The high priest brought the blood of animals, not his own. Here, as noted at 9:12, the preacher says the high priest entered “with” (en, “in, with”) blood, whereas he says Christ entered “through” (dia) his own blood. The contrast of “his own blood” (9:12) and “blood not his own” (9:25) frames the argument. The high priest offered a substitute other than himself; Christ offered himself.

This verse has important implications for Christian worship. Any understanding of the Lord’s Supper or of Christian ministry that suggests Christ is offered again and again as a sacrifice for sin runs directly against this verse and against the whole argument of Hebrews (see 7:27; 9:12, 26, 28; 10:10–14, 18). The Supper is a remembrance of, and a participation in the benefits of, the one sacrifice (1 Cor 10:16; 11:24–26); it is not a repetition of it. The preacher’s point is that repetition is the mark of the old, ineffective order; finality is the mark of Christ.

9:26

for then he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world. But as it is, he has been manifested once, at the consummation of the ages, to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself.

The preacher shows the absurdity of the alternative. “For then” (ἐπεί epei, “since, otherwise”) introduces an unreal consequence: if Christ’s sacrifice were like the high priest’s, needing to be repeated, then “he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world” (ἔδει αὐτὸν πολλάκις παθεῖν ἀπὸ καταβολῆς κόσμου edei auton pollakis pathein apo katabolēs kosmou). The logic is this: sin has existed since the beginning of human history; if Christ’s offering dealt only with the sins of a limited period, like the yearly Day of Atonement, then to deal with all sin he would have had to die again and again throughout history. That is unthinkable. Therefore his single sacrifice must be sufficient for all sin, of all times—backward to the foundation of the world (compare 9:15) and forward to the end.

Notice the verb: “to suffer” (παθεῖν pathein). For Christ to “offer himself” (9:25) meant for him to suffer and die. This confirms that Christ’s offering is not merely a heavenly presentation but centers on his death (see on 9:14). His offering and his suffering are one.

“But as it is” (νυνὶ δέ nyni de). This strengthened form of “now” is often logical (“but in fact”) rather than strictly temporal, though here both senses are present: in fact, and in this present age, Christ has done something once for all.

“He has been manifested once” (ἅπαξ … πεφανέρωται hapax … pephanerōtai). The verb φανερόω phaneroō, “to make manifest, reveal,” in the perfect passive means “he has been revealed (and stands revealed).” The same verb was used in 9:8: the way into the holy places had “not yet been manifested” (pephanerōsthai). Now it has been, because he has been manifested. The word refers to the whole of Christ’s appearing in history—his incarnation, life, and above all his death. Other New Testament writers use it the same way: Christ “was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was manifested at the end of the times for your sake” (1 Pet 1:20); “he was manifested to take away sins, and in him there is no sin” (1 John 3:5).

“At the consummation of the ages” (ἐπὶ συντελείᾳ τῶν αἰώνων epi synteleia tōn aiōnōn). συντέλεια synteleia means “completion, consummation, culmination.” Jesus used the phrase “the consummation of the age” of the end of history (Matt 13:39–40, 49; 24:3; 28:20). Hebrews opened by saying that God has spoken in his Son “at the end of these days” (1:2). The coming of Christ marks the climax toward which all previous ages moved. Paul says that we are those “upon whom the ends of the ages have come” (1 Cor 10:11). This means that the death of Christ is not one event among many in history; it is the decisive event, the turning point of the ages. The last days began with his coming, and believers now live in the age of fulfillment, even as they await its consummation at his return (9:28).

Word Study: ἀθέτησις athetēsis — “putting away, annulment”

“To put away sin” (εἰς ἀθέτησιν ἁμαρτίας eis athetēsin hamartias). ἀθέτησις athetēsis comes from ἀθετέω atheteō, “to set aside, nullify, reject.” In legal Greek, athetēsis was used for the annulment or cancellation of a legal obligation or decree. Hebrews used the word in 7:18: “there is a setting aside (athetēsis) of the former commandment because of its weakness and uselessness.” Here Christ’s sacrifice brings about the annulment of sin itself. Sin is put out of force, canceled, deprived of its claim. The singular “sin” (rather than “sins”) views sin in its totality—its guilt, its power, its condemnation. The yearly Day of Atonement could only bring “a reminder of sins year by year” (10:3); Christ’s sacrifice abolishes sin.

“By the sacrifice of himself” (διὰ τῆς θυσίας αὐτοῦ dia tēs thysias autou), literally “through his sacrifice.” The Greek pronoun could mean “his sacrifice” (the sacrifice he made) or “the sacrifice of him” (the sacrifice consisting of himself). Both are true, and the context of 9:14 and 9:25 (“offered himself”) favors the rendering “the sacrifice of himself.” He is priest and offering together.

The verse sums up the gospel in a single sentence: once, at the turning point of history, the Son of God appeared, and by sacrificing himself he dealt with sin decisively and forever. Nothing remains to be added. The believer who rests on this sacrifice rests on a finished work.

9:27

And just as it is appointed for human beings to die once, and after this comes judgment,

The preacher now draws an analogy from the common human experience of death. “Just as” (καθ’ ὅσον kath’ hoson, “inasmuch as, in the same measure as”) introduces the first half of a comparison completed in 9:28 by “so also.”

“It is appointed” (ἀπόκειται apokeitai). The verb ἀπόκειμαι apokeimai means “to be laid away, stored up, reserved.” It is used of a hope “laid up” for believers in heaven (Col 1:5) and of “the crown of righteousness laid up” for Paul (2 Tim 4:8). Here it means that death is reserved for, assigned to, every human being by divine decree. The background is God’s sentence on Adam: כִּי־עָפָר אַתָּה וְאֶל־עָפָר תָּשׁוּב ki-afar attah ve’el-afar tashuv, “for you are dust, and to dust you shall return” (Gen 3:19). Death is not merely a natural process; it is an appointment, God’s judgment on sin (Rom 5:12).

“For human beings to die once” (τοῖς ἀνθρώποις ἅπαξ ἀποθανεῖν tois anthrōpois hapax apothanein). The key word is again ἅπαξ hapax, “once.” Human beings die once. This rules out any notion of repeated lives, such as the reincarnation taught in some ancient philosophies and religions; each human life is lived once and ends once. (Exceptional cases recorded in Scripture—Enoch, who did not see death [11:5]; Elijah; those raised from the dead by Jesus and the apostles, who later died again; and believers alive at Christ’s return [1 Thess 4:15–17]—do not overturn the general rule the preacher states.)

“And after this comes judgment” (μετὰ δὲ τοῦτο κρίσις meta de touto krisis). Death is not the end. After it comes “judgment” (κρίσις krisis), God’s evaluation and verdict on each life. “Eternal judgment” is among the foundational teachings of 6:2; the preacher will warn of “a fearful expectation of judgment” for those who deliberately reject Christ (10:27), and he reminds his hearers that they have come to “God, the judge of all” (12:23). Jesus taught that after death there is no passing from one destiny to another (Luke 16:26). The verse does not specify the timing of the judgment (whether at death or at the final day), but the sequence is clear: one death, then judgment. There is no second chance after death.

Why does the preacher bring this in here? The verse serves his argument as an analogy. Human existence has a basic pattern: a single death, followed by a decisive outcome. Christ’s work follows a corresponding pattern: a single death, followed by a decisive outcome (9:28). But the analogy also contains a solemn reminder. Every hearer of this sermon will die once and face God’s judgment. The question is whether they will face it with or without the One who bore the sins of many. For the person who trusts Christ, the judgment that follows death has already been borne by him; for the person who turns away from him, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins” (10:26).

9:28

so also Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, apart from sin, to those who eagerly await him, for salvation.

The comparison is completed: “so also Christ” (οὕτως καὶ ὁ Χριστός houtōs kai ho Christos). As human beings die once and then face judgment, so Christ died once and will then appear for salvation. The parallel is exact in structure but glorious in contrast: for humanity, death leads to judgment; for Christ’s people, his death leads to salvation.

“Having been offered once” (ἅπαξ προσενεχθείς hapax prosenechtheis). The aorist passive participle, “having been offered,” is noteworthy because in 9:14 and 9:25 Christ “offered himself” (active). The passive may simply describe the event, but it may also hint at God’s role in the sacrifice. In Isaiah’s Servant Song, the Lord is the one who lays the people’s sin on the Servant: וַיהוָה הִפְגִּיעַ בּוֹ אֵת עֲוֺן כֻּלָּנוּ va-YHWH hifgia bo et avon kullanu, “and the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isa 53:6); and “it was the will of the Lord to crush him” (Isa 53:10). Both are true: Christ offered himself willingly, and the Father gave him up for us (Rom 8:32). Once again the word “once” (hapax) stands first, corresponding to “die once” in 9:27.

“To bear the sins of many” (εἰς τὸ πολλῶν ἀνενεγκεῖν ἁμαρτίας eis to pollōn anenenkein hamartias). This phrase comes from the climax of the fourth Servant Song.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 53:12

Hebrew: תַּחַת אֲשֶׁר הֶעֱרָה לַמָּוֶת נַפְשׁוֹ וְאֶת־פֹּשְׁעִים נִמְנָה וְהוּא חֵטְא־רַבִּים נָשָׂא וְלַפֹּשְׁעִים יַפְגִּיעַ — tachat asher he’erah lammavet nafsho ve’et-poshe’im nimnah vehu chet-rabbim nasa velapposhe’im yafgia, “because he poured out his life to death and was numbered with the transgressors; and he himself bore the sin of many, and for the transgressors he intercedes.”

The Hebrew verb נָשָׂא nasa, “to lift, carry, bear,” is used in the Levitical laws of bearing guilt (Lev 5:1; 16:22, where the scapegoat “shall bear on itself all their iniquities”). The Servant carries the sin of others as their substitute. The Greek Old Testament renders this line with the same verb Hebrews uses, ἀναφέρω anapherō, and the same words “sins of many.” Note also the last line: the Servant “intercedes” for transgressors, just as Christ “now” appears before God “on our behalf” (9:24; 7:25).

The verb ἀναφέρω anapherō has a double sense that suits the context perfectly. It means “to bear, carry” (as a burden), and it is also the regular sacrificial verb “to offer up” (literally “to carry up” onto the altar), used in 7:27 of the high priests who “offer up sacrifices” and of Christ who “offered up himself,” and in 13:15 of the sacrifice of praise. Christ both carried our sins and offered himself up as the sacrifice for them. Peter echoes the same Servant passage: “He himself bore (anēnenken) our sins in his body on the tree” (1 Pet 2:24).

“Many” (πολλῶν pollōn) reflects the Hebrew רַבִּים rabbim, which runs through Isa 52:13–53:12 (“many nations,” 52:15; “he will justify many,” 53:11; “the sin of many,” 53:12). In Hebrew idiom, “the many” emphasizes the great number of those who benefit from the one, in contrast to the single Servant who suffers. Jesus used it in the same sense: the Son of Man came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45), and his blood is “poured out for many” (Mark 14:24). The point is not to restrict the scope of Christ’s work to a small number, nor to say that every individual is automatically saved, but to celebrate the vast multitude whom the One saves.

“Will appear a second time” (ἐκ δευτέρου … ὀφθήσεται ek deuterou … ophthēsetai). This is the only verse in the New Testament that explicitly calls Christ’s return a “second” appearing, and it is the origin of the familiar phrase “the second coming.” The verb ὀφθήσεται ophthēsetai, “he will be seen, he will appear,” is the future passive of ὁράω horaō, “to see.” In the Greek Old Testament this verb is used of God’s glory “appearing” to his people. On the day of the tabernacle’s inauguration, after Aaron’s first sacrifices, Moses and Aaron came out and blessed the people, וַיֵּרָא כְבוֹד־יְהוָה אֶל־כָּל־הָעָם vayyera khevod-YHWH el-kol-ha’am, “and the glory of the Lord appeared to all the people” (Lev 9:23). And in Lev 16:2 God says, “in the cloud I will appear (אֵרָאֶה era’eh) over the mercy seat.” Christ will appear, visibly and gloriously.

“Apart from sin” (χωρὶς ἁμαρτίας chōris hamartias). The same phrase in 4:15 meant that Jesus was tempted “without sin,” that is, without sinning. Here it means something different: he will appear without any further reference to sin. His first coming was to deal with sin—to bear it and put it away. That work is finished. His second coming will not be to suffer, to bear sin, or to offer sacrifice again. The sin question has been settled. He will come in glory to complete the salvation of his people.

“To those who eagerly await him” (τοῖς αὐτὸν ἀπεκδεχομένοις tois auton apekdechomenois). The verb ἀπεκδέχομαι apekdechomai means “to wait eagerly, expectantly.” It is a characteristic New Testament word for the Christian attitude toward the future: creation “eagerly awaits” the revealing of the sons of God (Rom 8:19); “we eagerly await the redemption of our body” (Rom 8:23); “we eagerly await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ” (Phil 3:20); believers “eagerly await the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Cor 1:7). Christians are defined here not only as those who believe that Christ died, but as those who are waiting for him to return.

Historical Background: Waiting for the High Priest to Come Out

The closing words of the chapter complete the Day of Atonement picture with which it began. On that day the people of Israel gathered at the sanctuary, fasting and afflicting their souls (Lev 16:29–31), while the high priest went alone behind the curtain with the blood. No one could see what happened inside. The people waited, and their waiting was full of suspense: would the sacrifice be accepted? Would the high priest come out alive?

Scripture and later Jewish tradition preserve the drama of this moment. When Aaron first completed the sacrifices at the tabernacle’s inauguration, he “lifted up his hands toward the people and blessed them,” and then he and Moses “came out and blessed the people, and the glory of the Lord appeared to all the people” (Lev 9:22–23). Luke tells how the people waited outside for the priest Zechariah while he burned incense in the sanctuary, “and they were wondering at his delay” (Luke 1:21). The Mishnah says the high priest, after entering the Holy of Holies, offered only a short prayer in the outer sanctuary so as not to alarm the people waiting outside (m. Yoma 5:1), and that when he came out safely at the end of the day he held a celebration for his friends (m. Yoma 7:4). The book of Sirach, written in the second century BC, describes the splendor of the high priest Simon son of Onias as he came out from behind the curtain to bless the assembled worshipers, likening him to the morning star and the full moon (Sir 50:5–7, 20–21).

Hebrews applies this picture to Christ. Our High Priest has offered his sacrifice, his own blood, and has entered the true Holy of Holies, heaven itself, where he now appears before the face of God for us (9:24). His people wait outside, as it were, in this present age. But his sacrifice has been accepted—his session at God’s right hand proves it (1:3; 10:12)—and he will come out again. When he appears, it will not be to offer another sacrifice but to bring the full blessing of salvation to those who have been waiting for him.

“For salvation” (εἰς σωτηρίαν eis sōtērian). The chapter ends with the goal. “Salvation” in Hebrews is both present and future. Believers already experience its beginning (2:3; 6:9), but its fullness lies ahead: angels are sent to serve “those who are about to inherit salvation” (1:14); Christ became “the source of eternal salvation” (5:9); and here, at his second appearing, salvation will be brought to completion—the resurrection of the body, the entrance into the unshakable kingdom, the city that is to come, the full inheritance (9:15; 12:28; 13:14).

The final verse thus gathers up the whole chapter: one sacrifice, offered once; sins borne by the Servant; a high priest now in the presence of God for us; and a return that will bring salvation to his waiting people. For a congregation tempted to drift back to the endlessly repeated rituals of the old order, the preacher’s message is clear. Do not go back. Everything those rituals pointed to has been accomplished in Christ. Look forward, not backward. Wait for him with eager expectation, for he will come.

For Reflection

  1. Hebrews 9:14 says that Christ’s blood purifies the conscience “from dead works to serve the living God.” What is the connection between a cleansed conscience and the freedom to worship and serve? Where might a lingering sense of guilt be hindering your service?
  2. Christ offered himself “through the eternal Spirit” to God. How does seeing the cross as the united work of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit deepen your understanding of God’s love?
  3. “Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22). Why is it important to hold onto this truth in a culture that often prefers to speak of God’s love without mentioning sacrifice? How does Lev 17:11 (“I have given it to you”) show God’s grace in the sacrificial system?
  4. Christ is “now” appearing before the face of God on our behalf (9:24). How should this present ministry of Christ affect the way you pray, especially in times of weakness or failure?
  5. “It is appointed for human beings to die once, and after this comes judgment” (9:27). How does this sober truth, set side by side with Christ’s single sacrifice, shape the urgency of the gospel and the hope of the believer?
  6. Christians are described as “those who eagerly await him.” What would it look like for you to live more consciously as one who waits for Christ’s return, like the people waiting for the high priest to come out?
