12:12

Therefore straighten up the drooping hands and the weakened knees,

The conjunction Διό Dio, “therefore, for this reason,” draws the practical conclusion from the teaching on discipline. Because hardship is the loving training of a Father and leads to life, holiness, and peace, the readers must not collapse under it. The command ἀνορθώσατε anorthōsate, from ἀνορθόω anorthoō, means to straighten up again, to restore to an upright position. It is used in Luke 13:13 of the woman bent over for eighteen years, whom Jesus healed: “immediately she was made straight.” The aorist imperative calls for decisive action.

The picture is of an exhausted runner or fighter. “Drooping hands” (τὰς παρειμένας χεῖρας tas pareimenas cheiras, from παρίημι pariēmi, “to let fall, slacken”) are arms that hang limp; “weakened knees” (τὰ παραλελυμένα γόνατα ta paralelymena gonata, the participle of the verb from which “paralyzed” comes) are knees that are buckling. Both participles are in the perfect tense, describing a condition that has already set in. The preacher is not addressing a hypothetical danger. Some of the readers were already sagging. Earlier he had warned against becoming “sluggish” (5:11; 6:12); now he sees the signs of a runner about to drop.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 35:3

Hebrew: חַזְּקוּ יָדַיִם רָפוֹת וּבִרְכַּיִם כֹּשְׁלוֹת אַמֵּצוּ׃ — chazzequ yadayim rafot uvirkayim koshlot ammetsu, “Strengthen the drooping hands, and make firm the stumbling knees.”

Hebrews does not quote the verse word for word, but its language unmistakably echoes it, together with the Greek translation, which speaks of “slackened hands and weakened knees,” using the same participle for the knees that Hebrews uses. In Isaiah the words are addressed to those who are to encourage God’s exhausted people. The next verse continues: “Say to those with anxious hearts, ‘Be strong; do not fear! Behold, your God will come … he will come and save you’” (Isa 35:4). The chapter is one of the great prophecies of the return from exile and the coming salvation of God: the desert will bloom, the eyes of the blind will be opened, “then the lame will leap like a deer” (Isa 35:6), and a highway will run through the wilderness, called “the Way of Holiness” (דֶּרֶךְ הַקֹּדֶשׁ derekh haqqodesh, Isa 35:8), on which the redeemed of the Lord will return to Zion with joy. Every one of these themes appears in Hebrews 12: strengthened hands and knees (12:12), the healing of the lame (12:13), holiness (12:14), and arrival at Zion (12:22). The echo is not a stray phrase but a window onto the whole prophetic picture. The readers are the pilgrims on that highway, and the God who comes to save them is the reason they can straighten up.

Notice that the command is addressed to the community as a whole (the verb is plural). It means both “strengthen your own drooping hands” and “strengthen the drooping hands among you.” In Isaiah the command was to strengthen others; in Hebrews the context of mutual oversight (12:15; compare 3:13; 10:24–25) suggests that the readers are to help one another to stand. Encouragement in the church is not only a matter of words; it is the patient work of lifting up the weary.

12:13

and make straight paths for your feet, so that what is lame may not be put out of joint but rather be healed.

The second command draws on Proverbs 4:26. In the Hebrew, the wise father tells his son, פַּלֵּס מַעְגַּל רַגְלֶךָ וְכָל־דְּרָכֶיךָ יִכֹּנוּ palles ma‘gal raglekha vekhol-derakhekha yikkonu, “Make level the track of your foot, and all your ways will be firm.” The Greek translation reads, “Make straight paths for your feet,” using the same words that Hebrews uses here: τροχιὰς ὀρθάς trochias orthas, “straight tracks.” A τροχιά trochia was the track left by a wheel, a rut, or more generally a path or course—an apt word for a running track. It is significant that this verse comes from the same chapters of Proverbs as the quotation on discipline (Prov 3:11–12): the preacher is still drawing on the instruction of the wise father to his son.

The verb ποιεῖτε poieite, “make,” is a present imperative: keep making straight paths, as an ongoing practice. “Straight” paths are both level (free of obstacles) and direct (not wandering off course). In biblical language, a straight path is also a morally upright way of life (Prov 3:6; 11:5). The readers are to remove from their common life whatever would trip the weak: compromise, quarreling, and the drift back toward what they had left behind.

“So that what is lame may not be put out of joint but rather be healed.” The purpose clause shows that the concern is especially for the weakest members of the community. “What is lame” (τὸ χωλόν to chōlon, neuter, “the lame thing, the lame limb”) may refer to a lame member of the body or, figuratively, to the lame members of the church. The verb ἐκτραπῇ ektrapē (from ἐκτρέπομαι ektrepomai) is debated. Its usual sense in the New Testament is “to turn aside, wander away” (1 Tim 1:6; 5:15; 2 Tim 4:4), which would yield “so that the lame may not be turned off the road.” But Greek medical writers used the word for a joint that is dislocated, and the contrast with “be healed” (ἰαθῇ iathē) suggests the medical sense: “so that the lame limb may not be put out of joint.” An injured ankle, forced onto rough ground, becomes worse; on level ground, it can heal. The two meanings are close in effect. Either way, the weak member is in danger of being lost, and the community is responsible to make the way smooth enough for him to recover.

Here again Isaiah 35 stands in the background: on the highway of God’s salvation “the lame will leap like a deer” (Isa 35:6). The church is not a race for the strong alone. The preacher’s concern for the lame anticipates the concern for “anyone” who falls short in 12:15, and it reflects the heart of the Shepherd, who seeks the lost, brings back the strayed, binds up the injured, and strengthens the weak (Ezek 34:16).

12:14

Pursue peace with all, and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord,

The verb διώκετε diōkete means to pursue, chase, run after, and it can also mean to persecute. It keeps the running image alive. The readers have been pursued by their persecutors; now they must themselves pursue peace and holiness with the same energy. The present imperative calls for continuing effort.

“Peace with all.” The phrase recalls Psalm 34:14 (Hebrew 34:15): “Turn from evil and do good; seek peace and pursue it” (בַּקֵּשׁ שָׁלוֹם וְרָדְפֵהוּ baqqesh shalom verodfehu), a verse quoted in 1 Pet 3:11. “With all” (μετὰ πάντων meta pantōn) may mean all people, including outsiders and even persecutors (compare Rom 12:18, “if possible, so far as it depends on you, be at peace with all people”), or all the members of the community. In context, with its concern for the weak and for a root of bitterness that could defile many, the focus is probably on peace within the church, but the expression is broad enough to include everyone. A community under pressure is easily tempted to turn on itself, and a community in conflict with itself is ill-equipped to endure persecution from outside.

“And the holiness.” The second object of “pursue” is τὸν ἁγιασμόν ton hagiasmon, “the holiness” or “sanctification.” This noun, unlike ἁγιότης hagiotēs in 12:10, emphasizes the process or state of being made holy. The article (“the holiness”) and the following relative clause identify it as the specific holiness that is necessary for seeing the Lord. This is the holiness God’s discipline aims to produce (12:10).

“Without which no one will see the Lord.” The phrase is solemn and absolute: “no one” (οὐδείς oudeis). To “see the Lord” is to enter his presence, the ultimate hope of the believer (Job 19:26–27; Ps 17:15; Matt 5:8, “Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God”; 1 John 3:2; Rev 22:4). “The Lord” here probably refers to God, as in the quotation of 12:5–6, though Hebrews applies the title “Lord” to Jesus as well (2:3; 7:14; 13:20), and to see the one is to see the other. Throughout Scripture, the holy God cannot be approached by the unholy. Hebrews has taught that sinners may approach him only through the blood of Jesus, who has sanctified his people (10:10, 19–22).

Interpretive Options: Holiness Received or Holiness Pursued?

  • Holiness as a gift received. Hebrews teaches that believers “have been sanctified” by Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (10:10) and that Jesus “sanctifies” his people through his own blood (13:12). This holiness is a status given, not earned.
  • Holiness as a goal pursued. Yet here the readers are commanded to “pursue” holiness, and 10:14 describes them as “those who are being sanctified.” Holiness is also a life to be lived.

Scripture holds both together, and so must we. The holiness without which no one will see the Lord is not a moral achievement that earns access to God; that would contradict the whole argument of Hebrews, which insists that access to God is opened only by the blood of Jesus (10:19–20) and that the law “made nothing perfect” (7:19). But neither is it a merely legal status that leaves the life unchanged. Those who have been set apart by Christ’s blood will pursue the holiness that fits their new standing, and the absence of any such pursuit would show that they have not truly shared in Christ (3:14). Calvin and Owen both stress that holiness is the necessary fruit and evidence of saving grace, not its ground. The Father’s discipline (12:10) and the believer’s pursuit (12:14) work together toward the same goal: a people fit to see God.
