Benediction (13:20–21)

Translation

20Now may the God of peace, who brought up from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep by the blood of the eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus, 21equip you with every good thing to do his will, doing in us what is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever. Amen.

Setting and Structure

After asking the readers to pray for him (13:18–19), the author prays for them. The benediction is a single long sentence in Greek. Its structure is simple, though its content is rich.

  • The one addressed: “the God of peace” (13:20a).
  • What God has done: “who brought up from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep by the blood of the eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus” (13:20b).
  • The request: “equip you with every good thing to do his will” (13:21a).
  • How God answers: “doing in us what is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ” (13:21b).
  • Doxology: “to whom be the glory forever. Amen” (13:21c).

The prayer follows a pattern found throughout the Bible. Request rests on what God has already done. Because God has raised the shepherd through the blood of the covenant, he can be trusted to complete his work in the sheep. The structure also mirrors the logic of the new covenant. God has acted in Christ (past), God is now working in his people (present), and glory belongs to him forever (future).

In a few lines the benediction gathers up the great themes of the sermon. These include the covenant (chapters 8–10), the blood (9:12–14; 10:19, 29; 12:24), the eternal character of Christ’s work (5:9; 9:12, 14, 15), doing God’s will (10:7, 9, 36), and what is “pleasing” to God (11:5–6; 12:28; 13:16). It also adds something not stated explicitly anywhere else in Hebrews. The sermon has spoken constantly of Christ’s exaltation to God’s right hand (1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2). It has implied his resurrection when it spoke of his “indestructible life” (7:16) and of the one who “always lives to make intercession” (7:25). It has spoken of “resurrection of the dead” as a basic teaching (6:2) and of resurrection in the stories of faith (11:19, 35). But only here does it say in so many words that God raised Jesus from the dead.

Benedictions of this kind are common at the close of New Testament letters. The “God of peace” appears in several of Paul’s (Rom 15:33; 16:20; Phil 4:9; 1 Thess 5:23; 2 Thess 3:16). The form of a prayer-wish in the third person (“may the God of peace … equip you”) is also typical (compare 1 Thess 5:23, “may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely”). But the content here is distinctive. No other benediction in the New Testament speaks of “the great shepherd of the sheep” or of “the blood of the eternal covenant.” The author has shaped a familiar form to summarize his own message.

The prayer also casts the work of Christ in a new image. Throughout the sermon Jesus has been the high priest. Here he is the shepherd. The two images are connected. In the Old Testament, both priests and kings were called to care for God’s people. Moses, who led the people through the sea and the wilderness, was a shepherd before he was a leader (Exod 3:1). The God who “brought up” Moses, “the shepherd of his flock,” from the sea (Isa 63:11) has now brought up the great shepherd from the dead. The sermon that began by contrasting Jesus with angels and with Moses (1:4–14; 3:1–6) ends by naming him the shepherd greater than Moses.

Verse by Verse

13:20

Now may the God of peace, who brought up from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep by the blood of the eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus,

Ὁ δὲ θεὸς τῆς εἰρήνης Ho de theos tēs eirēnēs, “Now the God of peace.” After a sermon that has spoken of the “consuming fire” (12:29), the fearful judgment (10:27, 31), and the trembling at Sinai (12:21), the final prayer addresses God as the God of peace. This is not a contradiction. The same God who is holy and just has made peace with his people through the blood of his Son. In biblical thought, peace (Hebrew shalom; Greek εἰρήνη eirēnē) is far more than the absence of conflict. It is wholeness, well-being, and right relationship with God and with one another. Melchizedek was “king of Salem, which means king of peace” (7:2), a figure of Christ. The readers were urged to “pursue peace with all people, and holiness” (12:14). Paul writes that “having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ” (Rom 5:1), and that God reconciled all things to himself, “making peace by the blood of his cross” (Col 1:20). The God of peace is the God who has removed the enmity of sin through the blood of the covenant.

The first description of God is ὁ ἀναγαγὼν ἐκ νεκρῶν ho anagagōn ek nekrōn, “who brought up from the dead.” The verb ἀνάγω anagō means “bring up, lead up.” It is not the usual word for resurrection (normally egeirō, “raise,” as in 11:19, or anistēmi). Paul uses it once in a similar sense: “who will descend into the abyss? that is, to bring Christ up from the dead” (Rom 10:7). The choice of “bring up” suggests ascent: from the realm of the dead, upward. In Hebrews, resurrection and exaltation belong together. The God who brought Jesus up from the dead also brought him up into heaven, to his own right hand (1:3; 4:14; 9:24). The aorist participle describes a completed act. This is the only place in Hebrews where Jesus’ resurrection is explicitly named. It is the presupposition of everything the sermon says about his living, eternal priesthood. A dead priest could not intercede. Jesus “always lives to make intercession” (7:25) because God brought him up from the dead.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 63:11

The language of this verse closely recalls Isaiah 63, where the prophet remembers the exodus and longs for God to act again. Hebrew: וַיִּזְכֹּר יְמֵי־עוֹלָם מֹשֶׁה עַמּוֹ אַיֵּה הַמַּעֲלֵם מִיָּם אֵת רֹעֵי צֹאנוֹ אַיֵּה הַשָּׂם בְּקִרְבּוֹ אֶת־רוּחַ קָדְשׁוֹ — vayyizkor yeme-‘olam Mosheh ‘ammo: ayyeh hamma‘alem miyyam et ro‘e tso’no? ayyeh hassam beqirbo et-ruach qodsho?, “Then he remembered the days of old, Moses, his people: Where is he who brought them up from the sea with the shepherds of his flock? Where is he who put in their midst his Holy Spirit?” (Isa 63:11).

The verb הַמַּעֲלֵם hamma‘alem, “who brought them up,” comes from ‘alah, “go up,” in the causative form, “bring up.” It is the standard verb for God’s bringing Israel up out of Egypt. In the Masoretic Hebrew, “shepherds” is plural (רֹעֵי ro‘e). It probably refers to Moses and Aaron, as in Psalm 77: נָחִיתָ כַצֹּאן עַמֶּךָ בְּיַד־מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן nachita khatso’n ‘ammekha beyad-Mosheh ve’Aharon, “You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron” (Ps 77:20; Hebrew 77:21). The Greek translation of Isaiah has the singular, “the shepherd of the sheep” (τὸν ποιμένα τῶν προβάτων ton poimena tōn probatōn). It makes the shepherd himself the one whom God brought up out of the sea, using a different verb with the same meaning, “bring up.”

Hebrews 13:20 uses exactly the Greek phrase “the shepherd of the sheep” and the idea of God “bringing up.” But it replaces the sea with “the dead,” and adds “great.” The correspondence is close enough that many commentators believe the author had Isaiah 63:11 in mind. If so, the meaning is rich. At the exodus, God brought Moses, the shepherd of the flock, up out of the waters, and through him led his people to freedom. In the Bible, the deep waters are often a picture of death (Ps 18:4–5, 16; 69:1–2, 14–15; Jonah 2:2–6). Now God has brought up a greater shepherd from death itself, leading his people in a new and greater exodus. Hebrews has already contrasted Moses, faithful as a servant in God’s house, with Christ, faithful as a Son over it (3:1–6). Here the contrast is completed. Moses was “the shepherd of the sheep.” Jesus is “the great shepherd of the sheep.”

The Greek word order is emphatic: τὸν ποιμένα τῶν προβάτων τὸν μέγαν ton poimena tōn probatōn ton megan, “the shepherd of the sheep, the great one.” The repeated article gives “great” special weight. This matches the way Hebrews describes Jesus as a “great high priest” (4:14) and “a great priest over the house of God” (10:21). He is great because of who he is, the Son of God, and because of what he has done.

The image of the shepherd runs through Scripture. The Lord himself is the shepherd of his people: יְהוָה רֹעִי לֹא אֶחְסָר YHWH ro‘i lo echsar, “The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not lack” (Ps 23:1). Before his death Moses asked God to appoint a leader for Israel, so that “the congregation of the Lord may not be like sheep that have no shepherd” (Num 27:17). God answered with Joshua, whose name in Greek is the same as Jesus’ (Iēsous; compare Heb 4:8). Through Ezekiel God condemned the false shepherds of Israel who fed themselves instead of the flock. He promised, “I myself will search for my sheep and seek them out,” and, “I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall feed them” (Ezek 34:11, 23). The New Testament announces the fulfillment in Jesus. He is “the good shepherd,” who “lays down his life for the sheep” (John 10:11). He has authority to lay his life down and to take it up again (John 10:17–18). Peter calls him “the shepherd and overseer of your souls” (1 Pet 2:25) and “the chief Shepherd” who will appear in glory (1 Pet 5:4). Jesus, applying Zechariah, spoke of himself as the shepherd who would be struck so that the sheep would scatter (Matt 26:31; Zech 13:7). After his resurrection he would go before them (Matt 26:32). This connects naturally with 13:17. The leaders who “keep watch over your souls” are under-shepherds. The great shepherd is Jesus.

The next phrase is ἐν αἵματι διαθήκης αἰωνίου en haimati diathēkēs aiōniou, “by the blood of the eternal covenant.” The preposition ἐν en here means “by, by virtue of, in connection with.”

Interpretive Options: How Does “By the Blood” Connect?

  • With “brought up from the dead.” God raised Jesus by virtue of the blood of the covenant. The resurrection was God’s acceptance of Christ’s sacrifice and his vindication of the one who shed his blood. Because the blood had made a full atonement, death could not hold him.
  • With “the great shepherd.” Jesus became the great shepherd by means of his blood. Like the good shepherd of John 10, he laid down his life for the sheep, and so he has the right to lead them.

The Greek allows either, and the two ideas are closely connected. The first is more natural, since the phrase follows “brought up from the dead … the shepherd” and the main action of the clause is God’s raising. Zechariah 9:11 (below) also supports it, since there God frees prisoners from the pit “by the blood of your covenant.” The resurrection is not separate from the cross. It is God’s seal on the covenant sacrifice. The blood of Christ, which he carried as it were into the heavenly sanctuary (9:12), was accepted, and the one who shed it was brought up from the dead to live forever. Either way, the blood is the basis of everything: of the shepherd’s office, of his resurrection, and of the covenant he mediates.

Old Testament Text: Zechariah 9:11

Hebrew: גַּם־אַתְּ בְּדַם־בְּרִיתֵךְ שִׁלַּחְתִּי אֲסִירַיִךְ מִבּוֹר אֵין מַיִם בּוֹ — gam-at bedam-beritekh shillachti asirayikh mibbor en mayim bo, “As for you also, because of the blood of your covenant, I have set your prisoners free from the pit in which there is no water” (Zech 9:11).

This verse follows directly after Zechariah’s prophecy of Zion’s king coming “humble and riding on a donkey” (Zech 9:9), the prophecy Jesus fulfilled at his entry into Jerusalem (Matt 21:5; John 12:15). God promises that “by the blood of your covenant,” bedam-beritekh, he will release his people’s captives from a waterless pit. In the Old Testament בּוֹר bor, “pit,” is often a picture of the grave and the realm of death: “I am counted among those who go down to the pit” (Ps 88:4; compare Ps 28:1; 30:3). The Greek translation uses the same phrase as Hebrews, “by the blood of covenant” (ἐν αἵματι διαθήκης en haimati diathēkēs). Whether or not the author was consciously echoing Zechariah, the parallel shows the biblical logic. By the blood of the covenant God brings his people up from the pit of death. Hebrews shows that he did this first for the shepherd, and through him for the sheep.

The covenant blood also recalls Sinai. When the first covenant was made, Moses sprinkled the people with blood and said, הִנֵּה דַם־הַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר כָּרַת יְהוָה עִמָּכֶם hinneh dam-habberit asher karat YHWH ‘immakhem, “Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you” (Exod 24:8). Hebrews quoted these words in 9:20. Jesus used them at the Last Supper: “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Mark 14:24; compare Luke 22:20, “the new covenant in my blood”). Hebrews has warned against treating “the blood of the covenant, by which he was sanctified, as common” (10:29). Here, at the end, it celebrates that blood as the foundation of God’s peace.

The covenant is called αἰώνιος aiōnios, “eternal.” This is the only place Hebrews uses “eternal” of the covenant itself, though it has used the word for “eternal salvation” (5:9), “eternal judgment” (6:2), “eternal redemption” (9:12), “eternal Spirit” (9:14), and “eternal inheritance” (9:15). Earlier the author has called it the “new covenant” (8:8, 13; 9:15; 12:24) and the “better covenant” (7:22; 8:6). The prophets had promised an everlasting covenant: וְכָרַתִּי לָהֶם בְּרִית עוֹלָם אֲשֶׁר לֹא־אָשׁוּב מֵאַחֲרֵיהֶם לְהֵיטִיבִי אוֹתָם וְאֶת־יִרְאָתִי אֶתֵּן בִּלְבָבָם לְבִלְתִּי סוּר מֵעָלָי ve-kharatti lahem berit ‘olam asher lo-ashuv me’achrehem leheitivi otam, ve’et-yir’ati etten bilvavam levilti sur me‘alai, “I will make with them an everlasting covenant, that I will not turn away from doing them good; and I will put the fear of me in their hearts, so that they will not turn away from me” (Jer 32:40; compare Isa 55:3; 61:8; Ezek 37:26). The old covenant was temporary and is “ready to vanish away” (8:13). The new covenant is eternal because it rests on an eternal sacrifice, offered “through the eternal Spirit” (9:14), and is mediated by a priest who lives forever. Jeremiah’s promise also prepares for the next verse. God himself will put his fear in their hearts. He will work in his people what pleases him.

The verse ends with the name, held back to the last: τὸν κύριον ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦν ton kyrion hēmōn Iēsoun, “our Lord Jesus.” The full phrase “our Lord” appears only one other time in Hebrews (7:14, “our Lord was descended from Judah”). After the titles of shepherd and covenant mediator comes the personal, human name, as at so many key points in the sermon (2:9; 3:1; 6:20; 10:19; 12:2, 24). He is “our” Lord, the Lord of this congregation. The great shepherd is their shepherd.

13:21

equip you with every good thing to do his will, doing in us what is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever. Amen.

Now comes the request. καταρτίσαι ὑμᾶς ἐν παντὶ ἀγαθῷ katartisai hymas en panti agathō, “may he equip you with every good thing.” καταρτίσαι katartisai is an aorist optative. The optative is the Greek mood used for wishes and prayers. It is rare in the New Testament, and when it is used in this way it means “may it be so.” Paul uses the same form in similar prayers: “May the God of peace himself sanctify you completely” (1 Thess 5:23); “May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace” (Rom 15:13).

Word Study: καταρτίζω katartizō, “equip, make complete”

The verb means “put in order, make complete, equip, restore.” It was used of fishermen “mending” their nets (Mark 1:19), of “restoring” a believer caught in sin (Gal 6:1), and of the equipping of the saints for ministry (Eph 4:12, the related noun). Peter promises that “the God of all grace … will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you” (1 Pet 5:10). In Hebrews the verb has appeared twice before, both times of God’s creative action. By faith we understand that “the ages were framed by the word of God” (11:3). In Psalm 40, as Hebrews quotes it, the Son says to the Father, “a body you have prepared for me” (10:5). The God who framed the universe and prepared a body for his Son for the work of redemption is asked to equip his people for their work. What God made, God completes.

Textual Note: “Every Good Thing” and “Every Good Work”

The Greek text used in this study reads ἐν παντὶ ἀγαθῷ en panti agathō, “in every good thing.” Many later manuscripts add the word ἔργῳ ergō, “work,” producing “in every good work.” This is the reading behind many older English versions. The addition is probably a scribal expansion influenced by the familiar phrase “every good work” elsewhere (2 Cor 9:8; Col 1:10; 2 Thess 2:17; 2 Tim 3:17). The difference in meaning is slight. “Every good thing” is a little broader. It includes every gift and grace God gives, as well as the good works that come from them.

The purpose is εἰς τὸ ποιῆσαι τὸ θέλημα αὐτοῦ eis to poiēsai to thelēma autou, “to do his will.” This phrase ties the benediction to the heart of Hebrews. Christ came into the world saying, “Behold, I have come … to do your will, O God” (10:7, 9). “By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10). The readers need endurance “so that, having done the will of God, you may receive what is promised” (10:36). Christ did God’s will in his sacrifice. Now the readers are to do God’s will in their lives. Their obedience is not a way to earn what Christ has already accomplished. It is the fruit of the covenant he established.

The next clause shows how this happens: ποιῶν ἐν ἡμῖν τὸ εὐάρεστον ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ poiōn en hēmin to euareston enōpion autou dia Iēsou Christou, “doing in us what is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ.” There is a wordplay in the Greek. The readers are to do (poiēsai) God’s will, while God is doing (poiōn) in them what pleases him. Human action and divine action are not rivals. God works, and therefore his people work. Paul expresses the same truth: “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure” (Phil 2:12–13).

This is the new covenant promise in action. Through Jeremiah God said, “I will put my laws into their minds, and write them on their hearts” (Jer 31:33, quoted in Heb 8:10). The Hebrew reads נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם וְעַל־לִבָּם אֶכְתֲּבֶנָּה natatti et-torati beqirbam ve‘al-libbam ekhtavennah, “I will put my law within them, and on their heart I will write it.” Under the old covenant, God’s will stood outside the people as a command they failed to keep (8:9). Under the new covenant God works his will within them. The benediction prays for exactly this: God doing in his people what pleases him.

τὸ εὐάρεστον ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ to euareston enōpion autou, “what is pleasing in his sight,” is the final use of the “pleasing” theme. Enoch “pleased God,” and “without faith it is impossible to please him” (11:5–6). The readers are to worship God “in a way pleasing to him” (12:28). With sacrifices of praise and sharing “God is well pleased” (13:16). Now the author prays that God himself will produce in them what pleases him. Even the sacrifices that please God are, in the end, God’s gift. All of it comes διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ dia Iēsou Christou, “through Jesus Christ,” just as the sacrifice of praise is offered “through him” (13:15). He is the mediator of both: God’s working in us, and our offering to God.

Textual Note: “In Us” or “In You”?

The text followed here reads ἐν ἡμῖν en hēmin, “in us.” Some manuscripts read ἐν ὑμῖν en hymin, “in you.” In Greek the two words differ by only one letter and were pronounced almost the same in later centuries, so they are often confused in manuscripts. “In you” matches the preceding “equip you,” which makes it the smoother reading. For that reason a scribe would be more likely to change “us” to “you” than the reverse. “In us” is also well attested and is preferred by most textual critics. If “in us” is original, the author includes himself in the prayer. He prays for God to equip them, and then he acknowledges that God must work in all of us, preacher and hearers together. That is in keeping with the author’s habit of saying “let us” rather than only “you must.”

The prayer ends with a doxology: ᾧ ἡ δόξα εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας· ἀμήν hō hē doxa eis tous aiōnas; amēn, “to whom be the glory forever. Amen.” The phrase “forever” (eis tous aiōnas, literally “unto the ages”) is exactly the phrase used in 13:8: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever.”

Textual Note: “Forever” or “Forever and Ever”?

Many manuscripts read εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων eis tous aiōnas tōn aiōnōn, “unto the ages of the ages,” that is, “forever and ever.” This fuller form is common in New Testament doxologies (Gal 1:5; Phil 4:20; 1 Tim 1:17; 2 Tim 4:18; 1 Pet 4:11; 5:11). Other manuscripts have the shorter “unto the ages.” The editors of the text used here judged the shorter reading original. Scribes familiar with the fuller liturgical form would naturally have expanded the shorter one, but would have had no reason to shorten the longer. Either way, the meaning is the same: glory to him without end.

Interpretive Options: To Whom Is the Glory Given?

  • To Jesus Christ. The relative pronoun “to whom” (ᾧ hō) most naturally refers to the nearest preceding person, “Jesus Christ.” There are clear New Testament doxologies addressed to Christ: “To him be the glory both now and to the day of eternity” (2 Pet 3:18); “The Lord will rescue me … to him be the glory forever and ever” (2 Tim 4:18, where “the Lord” is Christ); “To him who loves us and freed us from our sins by his blood … to him be the glory and the dominion forever and ever” (Rev 1:5–6). The phrase “forever” would then echo 13:8, “Jesus Christ … forever.”
  • To God the Father. The subject of the whole sentence is “the God of peace.” He is the one who raised Jesus, who equips, and who works in us. Many New Testament doxologies are addressed to God the Father, and “through Jesus Christ” often appears in them, followed by a relative clause that is likely to refer to God: “to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever” (Rom 16:27; compare 1 Pet 4:11, where the same question arises).

The question cannot be settled with certainty from grammar. The nearness of “Jesus Christ” favors a reference to him. The structure of the whole prayer, in which God is the main actor, favors a reference to the Father. Many commentators lean toward the Father as the main subject. But it is important that either reading is fully biblical. Hebrews has shown that the Son is “the radiance of God’s glory” (1:3), worshiped by the angels (1:6), addressed as God (1:8), and “counted worthy of more glory than Moses” (3:3). In Revelation, glory is given “to him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb, forever and ever” (Rev 5:13). Whether the author directed this doxology to the Father or to the Son, Scripture teaches that the glory belongs to both, and through the Son to the Father.

ἀμήν amēn, “Amen,” comes from the Hebrew אָמֵן amen, from the root meaning “firm, reliable, true.” To say “Amen” is to declare, “This is true; let it be so.” In the synagogue and in the early church the congregation said “Amen” in response to prayer and praise (Neh 8:6; 1 Cor 14:16). The author expects the readers, hearing his sermon read aloud in their assembly, to answer the benediction with their own “Amen.”

The benediction is a fitting summary of the whole sermon. The readers have been warned, rebuked, encouraged, and instructed. They have been called to hold fast, to draw near, to run with endurance, and to go outside the camp. Now the author commits all of it to God. The endurance they need, the faith they must keep, the obedience God requires—all of this God himself will supply. It rests on what he has already done in raising the great shepherd by the blood of the eternal covenant. The God who brought up the shepherd from the dead will not fail to bring the sheep after him.

For Reflection

  1. After a sermon full of solemn warnings, the author prays to “the God of peace.” How does the blood of the eternal covenant make it possible for the holy God, who is “a consuming fire” (12:29), to be the God of peace toward his people?
  2. Hebrews names the resurrection of Jesus explicitly only here, though its whole argument depends on a living high priest. How does the resurrection undergird everything Hebrews says about Christ’s priesthood and our hope?
  3. Jesus is “the great shepherd of the sheep,” greater than Moses (Isa 63:11; Heb 3:1–6). What does it mean for you personally to be under his care? How does this shape the way you view the human leaders of the church (13:17)?
  4. The author prays that God will “equip” his people to do his will while God himself is “doing in us what is pleasing in his sight” (13:21; compare Phil 2:12–13). How does this truth free you both from complacency and from anxious self-effort?
  5. The benediction is a prayer for others. Try praying 13:20–21 for someone you know by name. What changes when you pray for others on the basis of what God has done in Christ rather than on the basis of their merit or progress?
