One Sacrifice for All Time (10:1–18)

Translation

1For since the law has a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image of the realities, it can never, by the same sacrifices that they offer perpetually year by year, make perfect those who draw near. 2Otherwise, would they not have ceased to be offered, because the worshipers, once cleansed, would no longer have any consciousness of sins? 3But in these sacrifices there is a reminder of sins year by year. 4For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.

5Therefore, when he comes into the world, he says,

“Sacrifice and offering you did not desire,
but a body you prepared for me;
6in burnt offerings and sin offerings
you took no pleasure.
7Then I said, ‘Behold, I have come—
in the scroll of the book it is written concerning me—
to do your will, O God.’”

8When he says above, “Sacrifices and offerings and burnt offerings and sin offerings you did not desire, nor did you take pleasure in them” (these are offered according to the law), 9then he has said, “Behold, I have come to do your will.” He takes away the first in order to establish the second. 10By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.

11And every priest stands day by day ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never strip away sins. 12But this one, having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, sat down at the right hand of God, 13from then on waiting until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet. 14For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.

15And the Holy Spirit also bears witness to us; for after saying,

16“This is the covenant that I will make with them
after those days, says the Lord:
putting my laws on their hearts,
I will also write them on their minds,”

17he then adds,

“And their sins and their lawless deeds
I will by no means remember any more.”

18Now where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer an offering for sin.

Setting and Structure

With this section the author finishes the great central exposition of his sermon. He announced its theme at 8:1–2: “we have such a high priest, who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens, a minister of the sanctuary and of the true tabernacle.” He then showed that Jesus mediates a better covenant (8:6–13), that the earthly tabernacle and its rites were a temporary “parable for the present time” (9:1–10), and that Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary “once for all” by his own blood, securing eternal redemption, cleansing the conscience, inaugurating the new covenant, and putting away sin by the sacrifice of himself (9:11–28). Now, in 10:1–18, he gathers all of this into a closing summary that is also a climax. Little here is entirely new, but everything is said with a new finality. The section ends with the words, “there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:18). After that, the author has nothing more to explain, only an appeal to make (10:19).

The argument runs in four movements, each with its own Old Testament foundation.

  • The law’s inability (10:1–4). The law had only a “shadow” of the good things to come. Its sacrifices, repeated every year, could not perfect the worshipers. If they had, they would have stopped. Instead they served as a yearly reminder of sins, because animal blood cannot take sins away. This picks up 9:9–10 and 9:25–26 and prepares for the contrast to come.
  • The Son’s obedient offering (10:5–10). Psalm 40:6–8 is heard as the voice of Christ entering the world. God did not desire the sacrifices of the law; he prepared a body for his Son, and the Son came to do God’s will. The author explains the quotation (10:8–9) and draws the conclusion: by that will “we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10).
  • Standing priests and a seated Priest (10:11–14). Psalm 110:1, the text that has shaped the whole sermon since 1:3 and 1:13, returns. Every priest stands, offering the same sacrifices daily. Christ, having offered one sacrifice, sat down, and now waits for his enemies to be subdued. By one offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.
  • The Spirit’s witness (10:15–18). Jeremiah 31:33–34, first quoted in full at 8:8–12, is cited again in shortened form as the testimony of the Holy Spirit. The new covenant promises an inward law and a final forgetting of sins. Where sins are forgiven, no further offering is needed.

The section is held together by deliberate contrasts that a listener would hear at once when the sermon was read aloud. The old order offers “the same sacrifices” (10:1, 11), “year by year” (10:1, 3) and “day by day” (10:11), “repeatedly” (10:11). Christ offers “one sacrifice” (10:12), “a single offering” (10:14), “once for all” (10:10). The old sacrifices “can never” perfect (10:1) or remove sins (10:4, 11). Christ’s offering “has perfected” (10:14). The phrase “for all time” or “perpetually” (εἰς τὸ διηνεκές eis to diēnekes) occurs three times (10:1, 12, 14), and with it the author makes his point almost by wordplay. The priests offer their sacrifices perpetually and get nowhere, while Christ’s one sacrifice is effective for all time. The law brings a “reminder” of sins (10:3); in the new covenant God will “remember” them no more (10:17). The section also forms a frame with the beginning of the central exposition. Jeremiah 31 is quoted at the start (8:8–12) and again at the end (10:16–17), and Christ’s session at God’s right hand appears at the start (8:1) and again here (10:12). Commentators such as Lane and Cockerill have drawn attention to this framing, which marks 8:1–10:18 as one carefully built unit.

Three Old Testament witnesses therefore stand at the climax of the argument: a psalm of the Son’s obedience (Ps 40), a psalm of the Son’s enthronement (Ps 110), and a prophecy of the new covenant (Jer 31). The author does not treat these as proof texts pulled out of their settings. He listens to them as the voice of God speaking in the Scriptures (“he says,” 10:5; “the Holy Spirit also bears witness,” 10:15), and he hears in them the logic of God’s own plan. God never intended animal sacrifice to be the final word. He always desired a fully obedient person, a priest-king who would finish his work and sit down, and a covenant in which sin is truly forgiven and the law is written on the heart.

The historical setting matters too. The author speaks of the Levitical sacrifices as still being offered: “they offer” (10:1), “are offered” (10:8), and “every priest stands day by day” (10:11). In Jerusalem the daily burnt offering, the Tamid, was presented every morning and evening, and every autumn the high priest entered the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement. For a congregation of Jewish believers under pressure, the temple stood as a visible, ancient, and socially respectable means of dealing with sin. They had no altar, priesthood, or smoke of sacrifice of their own to point to (cf. 13:9–14). The author’s purpose is to show that turning back to that system would mean leaving the reality for the shadow, and leaving the finished work of the seated Priest for the unfinished and unfinishable labor of priests who never sit down.

Verse by Verse

10:1

For since the law has a shadow of the good things to come, not the very image of the realities, it can never, by the same sacrifices that they offer perpetually year by year, make perfect those who draw near.

The opening “for” (γὰρ gar) ties this verse to the end of chapter 9. There the author showed that Christ appeared “once” at the consummation of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself (9:26), and that he was offered “once” to bear the sins of many (9:28). Why did God’s plan require such a once-for-all sacrifice? Because the law, with all its sacrifices, could never accomplish what was needed. Chapter 10 begins by explaining why.

The sentence begins in Greek with the word “shadow” (Σκιὰν skian), placed first for emphasis. The law “has” (ἔχων echōn) a shadow. The participle gives the reason the law cannot do what follows: because it possesses only a shadow, it cannot perfect. The author has already called the earthly sanctuary “a copy and shadow of the heavenly things” (8:5). There the point was mainly spatial: earth versus heaven, copy versus original. Here the shadow is “of the good things to come” (τῶν μελλόντων ἀγαθῶν tōn mellontōn agathōn). The perspective is historical: the law looked ahead. The same phrase appears at 9:11, where Christ came as “high priest of the good things that have come,” and for the author those future goods have now arrived in Christ. To the law they were still to come; to the church they are present realities, though their full enjoyment remains future (cf. 13:14).

Paul uses the same imagery: food laws, festivals, new moons and Sabbaths “are a shadow of the things to come, but the body belongs to Christ” (Col 2:17). A shadow is real, and it truly tells you something about the object that casts it, such as its outline, its size, and that it is coming toward you. But you cannot live in a shadow, eat a shadow, or be cleansed by one. The law was no mistake. It was God’s own gift, and its shadows were accurate. But its very nature meant it pointed beyond itself.

Word Study: εἰκών eikōn, “image”

The law has a shadow, “not the very image of the realities” (οὐκ αὐτὴν τὴν εἰκόνα τῶν πραγμάτων ouk autēn tēn eikona tōn pragmatōn). In everyday English an “image” can be a mere copy, but that is not the sense here. The author sets “image” against “shadow” as the fuller against the fainter. An eikōn is a form that actually shares in and shows the reality it represents. Elsewhere in the New Testament Christ is “the image of God” (2 Cor 4:4; Col 1:15), and believers are being transformed into the same image (2 Cor 3:18; Rom 8:29). The intensive “the very” (αὐτὴν autēn) sharpens the contrast: the law did not possess the actual form of the realities themselves. The word “realities” (πραγμάτων pragmatōn, from πρᾶγμα pragma, “thing, matter, reality”) refers to the substance of salvation: true cleansing, real access to God, the new covenant, and the heavenly sanctuary.

John Chrysostom illustrated the verse with an image from painting. An artist first sketches an outline, and while it remains only an outline it is a kind of shadow. When the colors are laid on, it becomes an image. The law was the outline; the gospel is the finished portrait. The illustration fits well, because the outline and the portrait are by the same artist and of the same subject. The old covenant is not opposed to the new. It is the new in sketch form.

Some readers have heard Platonic philosophy in this language, and the vocabulary of shadow and image was certainly familiar in the Greek-speaking world. Philo of Alexandria used similar terms. But the author of Hebrews fills them with a biblical, historical meaning. The “good things” are “to come”: they arrive in history, in the incarnation, death, and exaltation of the Son, “at the consummation of the ages” (9:26). His contrast is not between a timeless world of ideas and the material world. It is between promise and fulfillment, between the age of preparation and the age of the Son.

The second half of the verse states the consequence. By “the same sacrifices” (ταῖς αὐταῖς θυσίαις tais autais thysiais) offered “year by year” (κατ’ ἐνιαυτὸν kat’ eniauton), the law “can never … make perfect those who draw near.” The phrase “year by year” points to the Day of Atonement (Lev 16), the annual ceremony that was the high point of the whole sacrificial system and that has dominated the comparison since 9:7 (“once a year,” cf. 9:25). If even the greatest sacrifice of the year had to be repeated, the problem was not with the lesser offerings alone. The whole system was unable to finish its work.

The verb “offer” is present tense (προσφέρουσιν prospherousin, “they offer”), and it is joined to “perpetually” (εἰς τὸ διηνεκὲς eis to diēnekes). This phrase means “continually, without interruption,” and it will reappear twice in this section (10:12, 14), where it means “for all time.” The repetition is pointed. The old priests offer “perpetually” and never arrive. Christ offers one sacrifice whose effect lasts “for all time.” The Greek word order allows “perpetually” to be read either with “they offer” or with “can never … make perfect,” but the sense is the same either way: endless repetition, no completion.

“Make perfect” (τελειῶσαι teleiōsai, from τελειόω teleioō) is one of Hebrews’ key words. Applied to worshipers, it does not mean moral flawlessness. It means bringing them to the goal for which worship exists: a conscience truly cleansed and unhindered access to God (9:9, 14). The law “made nothing perfect” (7:19), and its gifts and sacrifices “cannot perfect the worshiper with respect to the conscience” (9:9). The people the law could not perfect are “those who draw near” (τοὺς προσερχομένους tous proserchomenous). This is the language of worship, of approaching God at his sanctuary. In Hebrews it describes Christians who “draw near” to the throne of grace (4:16) and to God through Christ (7:25), and it is the very verb of the great exhortation in 10:22: “let us draw near.” The old system could bring worshipers only so far. Christ brings them all the way in.

Textual Note

Two variants in this verse are worth knowing. (1) The earliest manuscript of Hebrews, the papyrus P46 (around AD 200), reads “a shadow of the good things to come and the image of the realities,” replacing “not the very” with “and.” Nearly all other witnesses read “not the very image,” and the context, which is built on contrast, strongly supports it; the P46 reading is generally regarded as a scribal slip. (2) Some manuscripts read the plural “they can never” (referring to the sacrifices or priests) instead of the singular “it [the law] can never.” Metzger discusses the evidence. Either way, the meaning is unaffected: the law and its sacrifices were alike unable to perfect.

10:2

Otherwise, would they not have ceased to be offered, because the worshipers, once cleansed, would no longer have any consciousness of sins?

The author now argues from the plain facts of practice. “Otherwise” (ἐπεὶ epei, literally “since [if it were otherwise]”) introduces a question that expects the answer “Yes.” The construction (οὐκ ἂν ἐπαύσαντο ouk an epausanto, a past tense verb with the particle an) describes something contrary to fact: “they would have ceased, but they did not.” If the sacrifices had actually done what was needed, they would have stopped. A medicine that cures is not taken forever, and a debt that is paid is not paid again every year. The repetition of the sacrifices is the evidence of their inadequacy.

The reasoning turns on the conscience. If the worshipers had been “cleansed once” (ἅπαξ κεκαθαρισμένους hapax kekatharismenous), they would “no longer have any consciousness of sins” (μηδεμίαν ἔχειν ἔτι συνείδησιν ἁμαρτιῶν mēdemian echein eti syneidēsin hamartiōn). Two features of the Greek deserve attention. First, the participle “cleansed” is in the perfect tense. Greek uses the perfect for a completed action whose result continues, so the phrase pictures a cleansing done once that leaves the worshiper in a lasting state of cleanness. Second, the word “once” (ἅπαξ hapax) is the same word the author has just used of Christ’s own sacrifice (9:26, 28). The author is quietly saying that what the law could not do, Christ has done.

Word Study: συνείδησις syneidēsis, “conscience, consciousness”

The word combines “with” and “knowing,” and refers to the inner knowledge a person has of himself, especially a moral self-awareness that accuses or excuses (Rom 2:15). It is normally translated “conscience,” and Hebrews uses it this way at 9:9, 9:14, 10:22, and 13:18. Here, with the genitive “of sins,” it means “consciousness of sins,” the awareness of standing guilty before God. The author does not mean that a cleansed person forgets that he has sinned or becomes careless about sin. He means that the worshiper would no longer carry an unresolved burden of guilt that bars him from God. The new covenant does not produce people who are unaware of their sins. It produces people whose sins have been dealt with, so that they can draw near to God “with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having had our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience” (10:22).

Why should the worshipers be described as “those who serve” (τοὺς λατρεύοντας tous latreuontas)? The verb λατρεύω latreuō means to serve God in worship, and in Hebrews it describes those who take part in the sanctuary’s rites (8:5; 9:9; 13:10). The goal of Christ’s blood is to cleanse the conscience “to serve the living God” (9:14). Worship and a clean conscience belong together, and the tragedy of the old system was that it kept its worshipers serving at a distance.

Historical Background: The Present Tense and the Date of Hebrews

This verse is one of the clearest signs that Hebrews was written while the Jerusalem temple still stood. The author asks whether the sacrifices “would not have ceased” if they had been effective, and he plainly assumes that they have not ceased. Throughout this section the sacrifices are described in the present tense: “they offer” (10:1), “are offered” (10:8), “every priest stands” (10:11). In AD 70, the daily sacrifice in Jerusalem came to an end during the Roman siege. Josephus records the day on which the continual offering failed (J.W. 6.94), and the temple was burned soon after.

Two cautions keep the argument honest. First, writers after AD 70 sometimes described temple rites in the present tense. Josephus does so, and so does 1 Clement (41:2), probably written in the 90s. The present tense alone, therefore, does not prove an early date. Second, the author’s argument concerns the tabernacle described in the Scriptures, not the Herodian temple as such. Even so, the cumulative evidence is strong. Here the author argues that effective sacrifices would have stopped. Had the sacrifices in fact stopped by an act of God’s judgment on Jerusalem, it is hard to believe he would not have mentioned it, since it would have powerfully confirmed his whole case (cf. 8:13, “what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away”). The best explanation is that the sacrifices were still being offered when he wrote, most likely in the 60s.

Textual Note

A few manuscripts omit the negative “not,” turning the question into a statement: “for then they would have ceased.” This makes sense, but the question form is far better attested and suits the author’s rhetorical style, inviting the hearers to draw the obvious conclusion themselves.

10:3

But in these sacrifices there is a reminder of sins year by year.

The sacrifices did not cease. Instead (ἀλλ’ all’, “but on the contrary”), they did something the worshiper may not have expected: they kept bringing sins back to mind. “In them” (ἐν αὐταῖς en autais), that is, in the very act of offering them, there is “a reminder of sins” (ἀνάμνησις ἁμαρτιῶν anamnēsis hamartiōn) every year.

Word Study: ἀνάμνησις anamnēsis, “reminder, remembrance”

An anamnēsis is an act of calling something to mind. The word occurs only four times in the New Testament: here, and three times in the accounts of the Lord’s Supper, where Jesus says, “Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19; 1 Cor 11:24–25). The contrast is striking and surely instructive. The yearly sacrifices of the old covenant brought sins to remembrance. The meal of the new covenant brings Christ to remembrance, along with his body given and his blood poured out. The old rite said every year, “Your sins are still here.” The new one says, “Your sins have been borne.”

The Old Testament itself provides a background for this idea. In the ritual for a wife suspected of unfaithfulness, the offering is called in Hebrew מִנְחַת זִכָּרוֹן מַזְכֶּרֶת עָוֺן minchat zikkaron mazkeret avon, “a grain offering of remembrance, bringing iniquity to remembrance” (Num 5:15). A sacrifice could serve, in other words, to bring guilt into God’s view as well as the worshiper’s. The author sees the Day of Atonement working in a similar way. When the high priest confessed “all the iniquities of the people of Israel” over the head of the goat (Lev 16:21), the nation was reminded each year of the full weight of its guilt. Philo of Alexandria could observe that sacrifices offered by wicked people do not bring release from sins but only a reminding of them (On the Life of Moses 2.107). The author of Hebrews goes further: even the sacrifices of the faithful could only remind, never remove.

It is important to see what the author is and is not claiming. He is not saying that the Day of Atonement was meaningless or that Israel’s faithful worshipers received nothing from God through it. Leviticus says the ceremony made atonement “once a year” (Lev 16:34), and those who offered in faith were truly forgiven by God, who had appointed the rite (Lev 4:20, 26, 31). What the author denies is that the animal blood itself was the ground of that forgiveness, or that the rite could ever bring the matter to an end. The ceremony was God’s provision for a period of waiting. Its forgiveness rested, as Paul puts it, on God’s “passing over of sins previously committed” (Rom 3:25) in anticipation of the sacrifice of Christ, and Hebrews says that his death redeems from transgressions committed under the first covenant (9:15). The yearly reminder kept Israel looking for something more.

Pastorally, the verse speaks to anyone who tries to deal with guilt by repetition, whether by religious rituals, penance, or an endless round of self-reproach. Repetition that never arrives at certainty only reinforces the awareness of guilt. The good news of the following verses is that God himself has supplied the one sacrifice that ends the reminding.

10:4

For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.

Here is the root reason, stated in one blunt sentence. The word “impossible” (ἀδύνατον adynaton) comes first for emphasis. It is one of four “impossibles” in Hebrews: it is impossible to renew to repentance those who fall away (6:4–6), impossible for God to lie (6:18), impossible to please God without faith (11:6), and here, impossible for animal blood to take away sins. Each marks a fixed boundary of reality.

“Bulls and goats” (ταύρων καὶ τράγων taurōn kai tragōn) are the animals of the Day of Atonement. The high priest offered a bull for himself and his house and a goat for the people, and he brought the blood of both into the Most Holy Place (Lev 16:11–16; cf. Heb 9:12–13, 19). The author is speaking of the highest and holiest sacrifice of Israel’s year, not some careless or lesser offering.

The verb “take away” (ἀφαιρεῖν aphairein, from ἀφαιρέω aphaireō) means to remove or take off. Paul uses it when he quotes the new-covenant promise of Isaiah: “this is my covenant with them, when I take away their sins” (Rom 11:27). Taking away sin is God’s own work, and the means he chooses must be adequate to the task. Why could animal blood not remove sin? The Scriptures supply the answers the author assumes. Sin is a personal offense against a personal God, committed by human beings who bear his image. An animal is not a person, does not choose to offer itself, and cannot stand in a human being’s place in any final way. The prophets had long recognized this. “I do not delight in the blood of bulls, or of lambs, or of male goats,” said the Lord (Isa 1:11). “Will I eat the flesh of bulls or drink the blood of goats?” he asked (Ps 50:13; Hebrew הַאוֹכַל בְּשַׂר אַבִּירִים וְדַם עַתּוּדִים אֶשְׁתֶּה ha’okhal besar abbirim wedam attudim eshteh). And David, after his great sin, prayed, “You do not delight in sacrifice, or I would give it; in burnt offering you take no pleasure. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit” (Ps 51:16–17; Hebrew 51:18–19). What the law required pointed to a deeper need: a human, willing, obedient, and spotless offering. That is exactly what the next verses provide.

This verse has sometimes been read as though the author despised the Levitical system. He did not. God commanded those sacrifices, and they served God’s purpose as types and teachers (9:9, “a parable”). Calvin rightly remarks, in substance, that the sacrifices had their efficacy not in themselves but as pointing to Christ. Their power lay in what they signified. Once the substance has come, to cling to the sign as though it could save is to misunderstand both the sign and the substance. The author’s “impossible” is a summons to look away from bulls and goats to the Lamb of God.
