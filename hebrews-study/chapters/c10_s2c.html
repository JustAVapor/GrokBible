The Warning: No Sacrifice Remains for Those Who Spurn the Son (10:26–31)

10:26

For if we go on sinning willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins,

The little word “for” (γάρ gar) ties this warning tightly to what has gone before. The author has just warned against forsaking the assembly (10:25). Now he shows where such forsaking can lead. The movement from neglect to apostasy is not sudden; it is the end of a road whose first steps look small.

The first word of the Greek sentence is Ἑκουσίως hekousiōs, “willfully,” “deliberately,” “voluntarily.” Placed first, it carries the emphasis. Its only other New Testament use is positive: elders are to shepherd the flock “not under compulsion but willingly” (1 Pet 5:2). The word describes action that comes from one’s own settled choice, as opposed to action done under pressure, in ignorance, or by weakness.

The verb is a present participle in a genitive absolute construction: ἁμαρτανόντων ἡμῶν hamartanontōn hēmōn, literally “we sinning,” best rendered “if we go on sinning.” The present tense in Greek presents the action as ongoing. The author is not describing a single act of sin, however serious, but a continuing, chosen course. And he says “we” — he includes himself. This is not a sermon aimed at outsiders; it is a warning the preacher addresses to the whole congregation, himself among them.

Old Testament Background: Sins of Error and Sins “with a High Hand” (Num 15:27–31)

The Law of Moses drew a sharp line between two kinds of sin. For a person who sinned “in error” or “unintentionally” — Hebrew בִשְׁגָגָה bishgagah — sacrifice was provided: “If one person sins in error, that person shall bring a she-goat in its first year as a sin offering, and the priest shall make atonement for the person who errs, when he sins in error before the Lord, to make atonement for him, and he will be forgiven” (Num 15:27–28; compare Lev 4:2). The Greek Old Testament regularly translates this idea with the adverb akousiōs, “involuntarily” — the exact opposite of Hebrews’ hekousiōs, “willfully.”

But then the law continues: “But the person who acts with a high hand (בְּיָד רָמָה beyad ramah), whether native-born or sojourner — it is the Lord he blasphemes, and that person shall be cut off from the midst of his people. For he has despised the word of the Lord and broken his commandment; that person shall surely be cut off; his iniquity is upon him” (Num 15:30–31). The image of the “high hand” is of a fist raised in defiance. For such sin the law provided no sacrifice. The very next passage illustrates it with a man who deliberately gathered wood on the Sabbath and was put to death (Num 15:32–36).

Jewish tradition kept this distinction alive. The Community Rule from Qumran prescribed that a member who transgressed the law of Moses deliberately (“with a high hand”) was to be expelled permanently, while one who sinned inadvertently was disciplined and could be restored (1QS 8–9). The Mishnah warns that the person who says, “I will sin, and the Day of Atonement will atone,” finds that the Day of Atonement does not atone for him (m. Yoma 8:9). Hebrews draws on this whole background. Its readers would immediately recognize “willful” sin as the defiant, high-handed sin for which the law knew no sacrifice.

It is crucial, however, to see what kind of “willful sin” the author means. He cannot mean every deliberate sin a believer commits. If that were so, no Christian could have hope, for every believer at times sins knowingly. Scripture is full of believers who sinned deliberately and were restored: David’s adultery and murder (2 Sam 11–12; Ps 51), Peter’s threefold denial of the Lord (Mark 14:66–72; John 21:15–19). John writes to Christians, “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9), and, “if anyone sins, we have an advocate before the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous one” (1 John 2:1). Hebrews itself presents Jesus as a high priest who deals gently with the ignorant and wayward (5:2) and invites believers to approach the throne of grace “to receive mercy” (4:16).

The context defines the sin. The author has just warned against abandoning the assembly (10:25), and in 10:29 he will describe the person in view with three devastating phrases: he has trampled underfoot the Son of God, regarded the covenant blood as common, and insulted the Spirit of grace. In 3:12 the danger was “an evil heart of unbelief, in falling away from the living God”; in 6:6 it was “falling away” and “crucifying again the Son of God.” The “willful sin” of 10:26 is therefore not a moral lapse but apostasy: the deliberate, persistent, knowing repudiation of Christ and his sacrifice by one who has heard and understood the gospel. It is the high-handed rejection of the very remedy for sin. Calvin made the same point in his comment on this verse: the apostle is speaking not of this or that particular sin but of a general defection, a forsaking of the gospel altogether.

The condition is “after receiving the knowledge of the truth” (μετὰ τὸ λαβεῖν τὴν ἐπίγνωσιν τῆς ἀληθείας meta to labein tēn epignōsin tēs alētheias). The noun epignōsis often denotes full or genuine knowledge, an acknowledgment, and the phrase “knowledge of the truth” was something of a standard early Christian expression for coming to understand and accept the gospel (1 Tim 2:4; 2 Tim 2:25; 3:7; Titus 1:1). The person in view is not ignorant. He has heard, understood, and once acknowledged the gospel. This parallels the description in 6:4–5: “once enlightened,” having “tasted the heavenly gift.” His sin is not the sin of ignorance, for which the old law provided sacrifice and for which the new covenant offers abundant mercy (compare 1 Tim 1:13, where Paul says he received mercy “because I acted ignorantly in unbelief”). It is rejection with open eyes.

The consequence: “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins” (οὐκέτι περὶ ἁμαρτιῶν ἀπολείπεται θυσία ouketi peri hamartiōn apoleipetai thysia). The phrase picks up 10:18 almost word for word: “where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer an offering for sin.” In 10:18 that sentence was glorious good news — no further offering is needed because Christ’s one offering has accomplished full forgiveness. Here the same truth turns into a solemn warning. Since Christ’s sacrifice is the one and only sacrifice that takes away sin (10:10–14), the person who turns his back on it has nowhere else to go. The Levitical sacrifices cannot help him; they never could take away sins (10:4, 11), and they have now been set aside (10:9). There is no second Christ and no second cross. To reject the only remedy is to be left without remedy.

This is not a statement that God refuses to forgive a repentant sinner. It is a statement about where forgiveness is found. As long as a person persists in repudiating Christ, there is no sacrifice for him anywhere. Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, insisted that it does not abolish repentance but rules out a second sacrifice. The verse’s force is exactly this: outside of Christ there is no atonement, and the one who deliberately leaves Christ has left the only place where sin is forgiven.

10:27

but a certain fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that is about to consume the adversaries.

If no sacrifice remains, what does remain? Only judgment. The author describes it with two phrases.

First, “a certain fearful expectation of judgment” (φοβερὰ δέ τις ἐκδοχὴ κρίσεως phobera de tis ekdochē kriseōs). The word “fearful” (phobera) stands first for emphasis and will be repeated at the end of the paragraph (10:31), framing the whole warning. The noun ekdochē, found only here in the New Testament, means “expectation” or “waiting for.” The indefinite pronoun tis, “a certain,” adds a note of foreboding — “a kind of dreadful expectation,” something almost too terrible to describe precisely. The irony is sharp. Believers “eagerly await” Christ, who will appear “for salvation” (9:28, using a related verb). The apostate also waits — but what he awaits is judgment.

Second, “a fury of fire” (πυρὸς ζῆλος pyros zēlos), literally “a zeal of fire” or “a jealousy of fire.” The fire is personified as if it had a passion of its own, eager to consume. The word zēlos is the Greek Old Testament’s regular rendering of the Hebrew qinʾah, “zeal, jealousy,” which describes the intense, exclusive love of God for his covenant people and his burning anger against those who betray him. Moses declared, “For the Lord your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God” (Deut 4:24; Hebrew אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה הוּא אֵל קַנָּא esh okhlah hu, el qanna), a verse Hebrews will echo in 12:29: “for our God is a consuming fire.” God’s jealousy is not petty envy; it is the holy refusal of a covenant Lord to share his people’s loyalty with another.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 26:11

The wording of 10:27 closely echoes Isaiah 26:11 in its Greek form. The Hebrew reads: “Lord, your hand is raised high, yet they do not see. Let them see your zeal for the people and be put to shame; yes, let the fire meant for your adversaries devour them” (Hebrew קִנְאַת־עָם qinʾat-ʿam, “zeal for the people,” and אַף־אֵשׁ צָרֶיךָ תֹאכְלֵם af-esh tsarekha tokhlem, “yes, the fire of your adversaries will consume them”). The Greek translation of Isaiah uses the very words Hebrews combines: “zeal” (zēlos), “fire” (pyr), “consume” (a future of esthiō, “eat”), and “the adversaries” (τοὺς ὑπεναντίους tous hypenantious). The author does not formally quote the verse, but he borrows its language so that hearers who knew Isaiah would recognize the source.

The context in Isaiah is instructive. Isaiah 26 is a song of the redeemed, celebrating the strong city of God (26:1–4) and confessing hope in resurrection: “Your dead shall live; their corpses shall rise” (26:19). Then comes the call to God’s people to hide “for a little moment until the wrath has passed by” (26:20), the very verse Hebrews will quote in 10:37. In Isaiah the fire falls on God’s enemies; God’s people are sheltered. Hebrews’ chilling point is that a person who once stood among God’s people can, by repudiating Christ, place himself among the “adversaries.” The covenant does not protect someone who has turned against the covenant’s Lord.

The fire is “about to” consume (ἐσθίειν μέλλοντος esthiein mellontos). The verb mellō expresses what is impending. Throughout Hebrews it describes the realities of the coming age — “the world to come” (2:5), “the age to come” (6:5), “the good things to come” (9:11; 10:1), “the city that is to come” (13:14). Judgment, too, belongs to that coming reality, and it is near (10:25, 37). The present tense of “consume” pictures the fire’s continuing work.

Jesus himself spoke in similar terms. He warned of “the unquenchable fire” (Mark 9:43) and of the “eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels” (Matt 25:41). Paul spoke of the Lord Jesus “revealed from heaven with the angels of his power, in flaming fire, giving vengeance to those who do not know God and to those who do not obey the gospel of our Lord Jesus” (2 Thess 1:7–8). The language of Hebrews is not exaggeration; it is the consistent testimony of Scripture.

10:28

Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without mercy on the testimony of two or three witnesses.

The author now builds an argument “from the lesser to the greater” — what the rabbis called qal wachomer, “light and heavy.” He used the same form in 2:2–3 (“if the word spoken through angels proved firm … how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?”) and will use it again in 12:25 (“if they did not escape when they refused him who warned them on earth, much less shall we escape if we reject him who warns from heaven”). The lesser case comes first: the penalty for rejecting Moses’ law.

“Anyone who has set aside” (ἀθετήσας τις athetēsas tis). The verb atheteō means to set aside, reject, nullify, treat as invalid. The related noun athetēsis described the “setting aside” of the former commandment because of its weakness (7:18) and the “putting away” of sin by Christ’s sacrifice (9:26). Here it describes not a mere breach of some law but a rejection of the law as a whole — a refusal of the covenant itself. The aorist participle presents the act as decisive.

The law’s penalty was death, “without mercy” (χωρὶς οἰκτιρμῶν chōris oiktirmōn), “on the testimony of two or three witnesses” (ἐπὶ δυσὶν ἢ τρισὶν μάρτυσιν epi dysin ē trisin martysin). The language comes from Deuteronomy, and the specific passage the author has in mind is significant.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 17:2–7

Deuteronomy 17:2–7 deals with a man or woman within Israel “who does evil in the eyes of the Lord your God by transgressing his covenant, and goes and serves other gods and bows down to them” (17:2–3). If the charge is investigated and proved, the guilty person is to be stoned to death. Then comes the rule of evidence: “At the mouth of two witnesses or three witnesses the one who is to die shall be put to death; he shall not be put to death at the mouth of a single witness” (Deut 17:6; Hebrew עַל־פִּי שְׁנַיִם עֵדִים אוֹ שְׁלֹשָׁה עֵדִים יוּמַת הַמֵּת ʿal-pi shenayim ʿedim o sheloshah ʿedim yumat hammet, literally “at the mouth of two witnesses or three witnesses shall the dying one be put to death”). The same principle applies to all cases in Deut 19:15.

The choice of this passage is no accident. The capital crime in Deuteronomy 17 is not just any transgression; it is covenant apostasy — turning from the Lord to serve other gods. The parallel with Hebrews is exact: the old-covenant apostate died; what then of the new-covenant apostate? The phrase “without mercy” also reflects Deuteronomy’s repeated insistence that the community must not spare one who leads Israel into idolatry: “your eye shall not look on him with pity, nor shall you spare him” (Deut 13:8; Hebrew 13:9 וְלֹא־תָחוֹס עֵינְךָ עָלָיו welo-tachos ʿeinkha ʿalav).

The requirement of two or three witnesses shows that the penalty was not arbitrary. The guilt had to be established beyond doubt. The person executed under Deuteronomy 17 had been proved guilty of deliberately abandoning the covenant. Likewise, the person in view in Hebrews 10 is not someone whose faith has merely faltered, but one whose repudiation of Christ is clear and settled.

The present tense “dies” (ἀποθνῄσκει apothnēskei) states the law’s rule as a general principle: this is what happens under Moses. The author does not dispute the justice of that law; he assumes it. The argument depends on the readers’ agreeing that the old covenant’s penalty was right.

10:29

How much worse punishment, do you suppose, will he be judged to deserve who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, and has regarded as common the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and has insulted the Spirit of grace?

Now the “greater” side of the argument. “How much worse” (πόσῳ … χείρονος posō … cheironos) — the same “how much more” form the author used of Christ’s blood in 9:14. The question “do you suppose?” (δοκεῖτε dokeite) invites the hearers to work out the obvious answer for themselves.

The word for “punishment,” τιμωρίας timōrias, appears only here in the New Testament. It denotes penalty that vindicates the honor of one who has been wronged. The verb “will he be judged to deserve” (ἀξιωθήσεται axiōthēsetai, future passive of axioō, “to count worthy”) shows that the punishment is fitting, not arbitrary; the passive points to God as judge.

Why is the punishment “worse” than death under Moses? Because the offense is greater. The author describes it in three parallel clauses, each an aorist participle, each answering to one of the three great realities of the new covenant: the Son, the blood, and the Spirit. Together they show what “sinning willfully” (10:26) really is.

1. “Who has trampled underfoot the Son of God” (ὁ τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ καταπατήσας ho ton huion tou theou katapatēsas). The verb katapateō means to tread down, trample, treat with utter contempt. Jesus used it of salt that has lost its taste, “thrown out and trampled under foot” (Matt 5:13), and of pearls trampled by pigs (Matt 7:6). The title “Son of God” recalls all that chapter 1 has said of him (1:2–3, 6, 8). The irony is terrible: God is putting Christ’s enemies under his feet (1:13; 10:13), and the apostate puts Christ under his. Compare 6:6, “crucifying again for themselves the Son of God and holding him up to contempt.”

2. “And has regarded as common the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified” (καὶ τὸ αἷμα τῆς διαθήκης κοινὸν ἡγησάμενος ἐν ᾧ ἡγιάσθη kai to haima tēs diathēkēs koinon hēgēsamenos en hō hēgiasthē). The adjective koinos means “common” in the sense of ordinary, profane, not holy — the opposite of “sanctified.” Peter used it in his vision: “I have never eaten anything common or unclean” (Acts 10:14). To regard Christ’s blood as “common” is to treat it as the ordinary blood of an ordinary man, perhaps a criminal justly executed, with no power to cleanse. “The blood of the covenant” recalls Moses’ words at Sinai, “Behold the blood of the covenant” (Exod 24:8; Hebrew הִנֵּה דַם־הַבְּרִית hinneh dam-habberit), quoted in 9:20, and Jesus’ words at the Last Supper, “this is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many for forgiveness of sins” (Matt 26:28). The blood God regards as precious (1 Pet 1:19; Heb 13:20), the apostate regards as nothing special.

3. “And has insulted the Spirit of grace” (καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς χάριτος ἐνυβρίσας kai to pneuma tēs charitos enybrisas). The verb enybrizō, another New Testament hapax, intensifies the word hybrizō, “to treat with insolence, outrage.” In an honor-and-shame culture an insult was a public assault on someone’s dignity — and here the one insulted is the Holy Spirit himself. The title “Spirit of grace” likely echoes Zechariah 12:10: “I will pour out on the house of David and on the inhabitant of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and of pleas for mercy, and they will look to me, the one whom they pierced” (Hebrew רוּחַ חֵן וְתַחֲנוּנִים ruach chen wetachanunim). The Spirit is the one who brings God’s grace to sinners and leads them to look in repentance on the one they pierced. To insult him is to spurn the very one who offers grace. One thinks of Jesus’ solemn warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (Matt 12:31–32). Such people once “became partakers of the Holy Spirit” (6:4); their sin is committed not in ignorance of his work but against it.

These three clauses define the sin of 10:26: not a particular moral lapse but the conscious, contemptuous rejection of the Son, his sacrifice, and his Spirit — the whole of the new covenant’s saving provision.

Interpretive Options: “By Which He Was Sanctified”

The phrase “the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified” (ἐν ᾧ ἡγιάσθη en hō hēgiasthē) raises a serious question: was the apostate a genuinely regenerate believer? Elsewhere in Hebrews “sanctify” (hagiazō) describes what Christ’s sacrifice does for his people (2:11; 10:10, 14; 13:12). Interpreters have answered in several ways.

  • View 1: Genuine believers can finally fall away and be lost. Many Arminian and Wesleyan interpreters take the phrase at face value: the person was truly sanctified by Christ’s blood, then apostatized, and is now lost. Its strength is a straightforward reading of “sanctified.” Its weakness is that it sets this verse against other statements in Hebrews and the rest of Scripture: those whom Christ’s offering sanctifies are “perfected for all time” (10:14); Jesus “is able to save to the uttermost” (7:25); and of his sheep Christ said, “they will never perish, and no one will seize them out of my hand” (John 10:28; compare Rom 8:29–39; Phil 1:6).
  • View 2: The subject of “he was sanctified” is Christ himself. John Owen and a few others proposed that the one “sanctified” by the covenant blood is the Son of God, who was consecrated as priest by his own blood (compare John 17:19, “for their sake I sanctify myself”). This removes the difficulty but strains the grammar: the natural subject is the person being described, and Hebrews consistently uses this language of believers (2:11; 10:10, 14; 13:12).
  • View 3: The person was outwardly sanctified as a member of the covenant community but was never truly regenerate. On this view the apostate had been set apart in the visible sense: he had been baptized (10:22), had professed the confession (10:23), had shared in the community’s worship, perhaps including the Lord’s Supper with its “blood of the covenant,” and had experienced the Spirit’s work among God’s people (6:4–5). The precedent is Israel, consecrated as God’s people at Sinai (Exod 19:10, 14; 24:8), yet largely fallen in the wilderness through unbelief (Heb 3:7–4:11; 1 Cor 10:1–12). John says of those who left the church, “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have remained with us” (1 John 2:19); compare those who receive the word “with joy” and yet “in a time of testing fall away” (Luke 8:13). The apostate is described in terms of the privileges he really enjoyed and the profession he really made — which is what makes his turning against the blood so heinous.
  • View 4: The warning is addressed to genuine believers as the very means by which God preserves them. Schreiner and others argue that the warnings are addressed to the congregation as believers, and that they describe a real consequence — final judgment — that would follow apostasy. God uses such warnings, heeded in faith, as the means by which he keeps his own from falling away. The warnings are not unreal; they do their work by being taken seriously, while God’s promises assure that his own will heed them and persevere.

How should we weigh these? The text itself gives two decisive clues. First, Hebrews repeatedly makes perseverance the evidence of genuine participation in Christ: “we have become partakers of Christ, if indeed we hold fast the beginning of our confidence firm until the end” (3:14); “whose house we are, if we hold fast” (3:6). Note the perfect tense in 3:14 — “we have become” — and the condition: holding fast to the end shows that one has become a partaker. Apostasy, by the same logic, reveals that one never truly had. Second, the section ends with the author’s confident declaration: “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39). He does not say that true believers may shrink back and perish, but that the true people of God belong to faith and not to destruction. Taken with 6:9 and 10:14, this strongly suggests that the apostate of 10:29 was sanctified in the sense of View 3 — set apart within the covenant community and its privileges — without the inward, heart-renewing work of the new covenant (8:10–12; 10:16–17), which always issues in perseverance.

At the same time, View 4 rightly insists that the warning is addressed to the whole congregation, including those who are genuine believers, and that it is meant to be heard and feared. The author does not say, “This could never happen to you.” He says, in effect, “Look where this road leads; do not take one step further on it.” God’s preservation of his people does not bypass their perseverance; it works through it, and one of his chief instruments is precisely this kind of warning (compare 1 Cor 9:27; 10:12; Phil 2:12–13). Views 3 and 4 are therefore complementary rather than competing: the warning is a real instrument by which God keeps his true people, and the fate it describes is the real fate of those who, having stood within the visible covenant community, finally repudiate Christ.

None of this blunts the warning. Whatever one concludes about the apostate’s former state, the outcome is the same: “a fearful expectation of judgment.” No one may console himself with theological arguments — “I am secure, so I may drift as I please” — while trampling on the Son of God. The only safe place is the place the author has just described: drawing near, holding fast, and keeping company with the people of God.

10:30

For we know him who said, “Vengeance is mine; I will repay,” and again, “The Lord will judge his people.”

The author grounds his warning in the character of God as revealed in Scripture. “For we know him who said” (οἴδαμεν γὰρ τὸν εἰπόντα oidamen gar ton eiponta). He does not say, “We know the text that says,” but, “We know the one who said.” Scripture is the voice of a living person whose character we know. As often in Hebrews, an Old Testament text is introduced as God’s own speech (1:5–13; 3:7; 8:8; 10:5). The readers know him: they know his holiness, his faithfulness, and his justice. They cannot pretend that he will treat apostasy lightly.

Both quotations come from the Song of Moses (Deut 32), a text the author has already quoted in 1:6 (Deut 32:43). The Song is significant. God gave it to Moses as a witness against Israel, foretelling that after entering the land the people would grow fat, forsake God, and turn to other gods (Deut 31:16–21). The Song itself describes that apostasy: “Jeshurun grew fat and kicked … he abandoned the God who made him and treated with contempt the Rock of his salvation” (Deut 32:15); “they provoked me to jealousy with a no-god” (32:21); and it warns, “a fire has been kindled in my anger, and it burns to the lowest Sheol” (32:22). A Song written to warn God’s own people against apostasy is precisely the right text for the author’s purpose.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 32:35–36

Deut 32:35. Hebrew: לִי נָקָם וְשִׁלֵּם li naqam weshillem, “Mine is vengeance and recompense,” continuing, “for the time when their foot slips; for the day of their calamity is near, and the things prepared for them hasten.” The Greek translation of Deuteronomy, as it has come down to us, reads differently: “In the day of vengeance I will repay.” Hebrews’ wording, Ἐμοὶ ἐκδίκησις, ἐγὼ ἀνταποδώσω Emoi ekdikēsis, egō antapodōsō, “Vengeance is mine; I will repay,” is closer to the Hebrew, with its emphatic “to me” standing first. Remarkably, Paul quotes the line in exactly the same words in Rom 12:19. This suggests that both writers knew a form of the verse current in Jewish and early Christian use — a rendering closer to the Hebrew than the standard Greek text. The emphasis falls on “mine”: vengeance belongs to God alone.

Deut 32:36. Hebrew: כִּי־יָדִין יְהוָה עַמּוֹ ki-yadin YHWH ʿammo, “For the Lord will judge his people,” continuing, “and have compassion on his servants, when he sees that their power is gone.” The identical line appears in Ps 135:14. Here Hebrews agrees word for word with the Greek Old Testament: Κρινεῖ κύριος τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ Krinei kyrios ton laon autou. “Lord” here stands for the divine name, YHWH.

The Hebrew verb din means “to judge” in the full biblical sense of exercising rule and executing justice. In the immediate context of Deut 32:36 the emphasis is on vindication: God will act on behalf of his people and have compassion on them when they are helpless, and he will take vengeance on their enemies (32:41–43). How then can Hebrews use the line as a warning? Because in the Song as a whole the God who vindicates his people is also the God who judges apostasy within his people (32:15–25). His judgment is a two-edged reality: rescue for those who are truly his, and condemnation for those who have betrayed him. The apostate, having joined the “adversaries” (10:27), will find the Lord’s judgment falling not in his favor but against him. And for the faithful, who are suffering and tempted to lose heart (10:32–36), the same truth is a comfort: God will vindicate them.

The first quotation is noteworthy for the way Paul uses it. In Rom 12:19 it grounds the command that believers must never take revenge: leave room for God’s wrath, for vengeance is his. Hebrews uses the same truth from the other side: since vengeance belongs to God, no one who sins against him can escape it. Both uses rest on the same conviction. Justice is not abandoned; it is in God’s hands.

The second quotation is startling because of its object: “his people.” The warning is not about pagans who never knew God, but about those who belong, or claim to belong, to his covenant people. Peter makes the same point: “the time has come for judgment to begin from the house of God” (1 Pet 4:17). Privilege brings accountability. Amos had told Israel, “Only you have I known of all the clans of the earth; therefore I will visit upon you all your iniquities” (Amos 3:2).

10:31

It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.

The warning ends with a sentence of only seven Greek words, stark and unforgettable: φοβερὸν τὸ ἐμπεσεῖν εἰς χεῖρας θεοῦ ζῶντος phoberon to empesein eis cheiras theou zōntos. The adjective “fearful” stands first, as in 10:27, closing the paragraph with the word that opened it.

“To fall into the hands” (ἐμπεσεῖν εἰς χεῖρας empesein eis cheiras) is a biblical idiom for coming under someone’s power, often in judgment or captivity. The “hands” of God in Scripture are hands of both creative power and judgment (Ps 95:5; Isa 66:2; Ps 32:4).

The phrase recalls a famous Old Testament scene. After David sinned by numbering the people, God gave him a choice of three punishments. David answered, “It is very hard for me. Let us fall, please, into the hand of the Lord, for his mercies are many; but into human hands let me not fall” (2 Sam 24:14; Hebrew נִפְּלָה־נָּא בְיַד־יְהוָה nippelah-nna veyad-YHWH, “let us fall, please, into the hand of the Lord”). The wise man Jesus ben Sira echoes the thought: “Let us fall into the hands of the Lord and not into human hands, for as great as his majesty is, so great is his mercy” (Sir 2:18). For the repentant sinner, falling into God’s hands is the best thing that can happen, because his mercies are great. But for the one who has spurned the only sacrifice for sin, the same hands become fearful. There is no contradiction. The difference lies not in God but in the person’s relation to the mercy God offers. David fell into God’s hands as a penitent who built an altar and offered sacrifice (2 Sam 24:18–25). The apostate falls into them having rejected the only altar and the only sacrifice there is.

The title “the living God” (θεοῦ ζῶντος theou zōntos) is a favorite in Hebrews (3:12; 9:14; 10:31; 12:22). It stresses that God is not an idea, a tradition, or a dead idol, but an active, personal, powerful being who sees, speaks, and acts. In 3:12 the danger was “falling away from the living God”; here the end of that path is falling into the hands of the living God. One cannot escape him by turning away from him. In 4:12–13 the author reminded his readers that “no creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and laid bare to the eyes of him to whom we must give account.”

Jesus himself taught the same holy fear: “Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but are not able to kill the soul; fear rather the one who is able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna” (Matt 10:28). Hebrews will return to this fear at the climax of its final warning: “let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire” (12:28–29). This fear is not opposed to the boldness of 10:19. The same believer who enters God’s presence with boldness through Christ trembles at the thought of meeting God apart from Christ. Rightly heard, the warning does not drive believers to despair; it drives them away from presumption and back to Christ, in whom alone the living God is met as Father rather than as Judge.
