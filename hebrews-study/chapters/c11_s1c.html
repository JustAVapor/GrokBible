Abraham and the Patriarchs (11:8–22)

11:8

By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed by going out to a place that he was going to receive as an inheritance; and he went out, not knowing where he was going.

With Abraham the catalogue reaches its center of gravity. More space is given to him (11:8–12, 17–19) than to anyone else except Moses, and the reflection of verses 13–16 grows out of his story. This is fitting. Abraham is the father of the faithful (Rom 4:11–12, 16), the man to whom God first gave “the promise” that runs through the whole of Hebrews (6:13–15), and the one whose “seed” the Son came to help (2:16). For Jewish hearers he was the great ancestor; for Christian hearers he was the model of believing God against all appearances.

The verse turns on two verbs of hearing and going. “When he was called” translates a present participle, καλούμενος kaloumenos, literally “being called.” The tense suggests that Abraham’s obedience followed the call at once, while the call was still, as it were, sounding in his ears. The main verb is ὑπήκουσεν hypēkousen, “obeyed,” from ὑπακούω hypakouō, a compound of “under” and “hear”: to listen in submission, to hear and act. In both Greek and Hebrew, obeying is a form of hearing; the Hebrew verb שָׁמַע shamaʿ means both. There is a natural link with the description of Jesus, who “learned obedience” and became the source of salvation “to all who obey him” (5:8–9). Faith, in Hebrews, is never passive assent. It is hearing that gets up and goes.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 12:1, 4

Hebrew (12:1): וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־אַבְרָם לֶךְ־לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ וּמִמּוֹלַדְתְּךָ וּמִבֵּית אָבִיךָ אֶל־הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר אַרְאֶךָּ — vayyomer YHWH el-avram: lekh-lekha me’artsekha umimmoladtekha umibbeit avikha el-ha’arets asher ar’ekka, “And the Lord said to Abram, ‘Go, for your own sake, from your land and from your kindred and from your father’s house, to the land that I will show you.’”

Hebrew (12:4a): וַיֵּלֶךְ אַבְרָם כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר אֵלָיו יְהוָה — vayyelekh avram ka’asher dibber elav YHWH, “So Abram went, as the Lord had spoken to him.”

The command lists three circles of belonging, each closer than the last: country, clan, and father’s household. In the ancient Near East these were a person’s protection, livelihood, inheritance, and identity. To leave them was to become vulnerable, a man without rights in someone else’s land. The destination is left unnamed: “the land that I will show you.” The verb is future; the land had not yet been shown. Genesis then records the response with the utmost simplicity: “So Abram went.” Joshua later reminded Israel that Abraham’s family “served other gods” beyond the Euphrates (Josh 24:2), so the call also meant leaving the religion of his fathers. Stephen, in Acts 7:2–4, adds that God first appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, before he settled in Haran (compare Gen 11:31).

Abraham obeyed “by going out” (ἐξελθεῖν exelthein), and the verse ends by repeating the verb: “and he went out” (ἐξῆλθεν exēlthen). “Going out” is a theme to watch. Joseph will speak of the “exodus,” the going out of Israel (11:22); the patriarchs will be described as having “gone out” of their former land (11:15); and the climactic exhortation of the letter will be, “So let us go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach; for here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come” (13:13–14). Abraham’s going out is the pattern for the believer’s life.

He went out “to a place that he was going to receive as an inheritance.” The verb ἤμελλεν ēmellen, “he was going to,” points to the future, and the inheritance (κληρονομία klēronomia) is the same family of words used of Noah in the previous verse and of believers who “inherit the promises” through faith and patience (6:12). Abraham never actually took possession of the land in his lifetime (Acts 7:5); he received it only as a promise for his descendants. And he went “not knowing where he was going” (μὴ ἐπιστάμενος ποῦ ἔρχεται mē epistamenos pou erchetai). The participle epistamenos means “understanding, knowing with certainty.” What Abraham lacked was not trust but information. He knew who had called him, but not where the road would lead.

That is the heart of faith as Hebrews describes it: the willingness to obey God’s word without seeing the end of the road. It is the opposite of the attitude of the wilderness generation, who had seen God’s works yet would not go forward when he told them to (3:7–19). The hearers stood at a similar point. Following Christ had already cost them their property and their standing (10:32–34), and they could not see where the path would lead next. The preacher’s word to them, through Abraham, is that it is enough to know the One who calls.

11:9

By faith he lived as a sojourner in the land of promise as in a foreign land, dwelling in tents with Isaac and Jacob, the fellow heirs of the same promise;

Abraham’s faith did not end with his arrival. It had to continue in a long life of unsettledness. The verb “lived as a sojourner” (παρῴκησεν parōkēsen, from παροικέω paroikeō) means to live alongside others as a resident alien, someone who lives in a place without belonging to it or having the rights of a citizen. Its noun, πάροικος paroikos, is the common Greek Old Testament translation for the Hebrew גֵּר ger, “sojourner, resident alien,” and the verb renders the Hebrew גּוּר gur, “to sojourn.” Genesis describes Canaan precisely this way. God promised Abraham, וְנָתַתִּי לְךָ וּלְזַרְעֲךָ אַחֲרֶיךָ אֵת אֶרֶץ מְגֻרֶיךָ venatatti lekha ulezarʿakha acharekha et erets megurekha, “I will give to you and to your offspring after you the land of your sojournings” (Gen 17:8). The land was promised, yet the patriarchs lived in it as foreigners.

Hebrews makes the paradox plain. Abraham lived “in the land of promise” (εἰς γῆν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας eis gēn tēs epangelias), the land that God had pledged to him, and yet he lived there “as in a foreign land” (ὡς ἀλλοτρίαν hōs allotrian, literally “as belonging to another”). The only piece of it he ever owned was a grave, the cave of Machpelah, which he bought from the Hittites to bury Sarah (Gen 23). Stephen summed it up: God “gave him no inheritance in it, not even a foot’s length, but promised to give it to him as a possession and to his offspring after him” (Acts 7:5).

He lived “in tents” (ἐν σκηναῖς en skēnais). Genesis repeatedly mentions Abraham pitching his tent (Gen 12:8; 13:3, 18; 18:1). The tent is the dwelling of the nomad and the pilgrim: portable, temporary, without foundations. Interestingly, the noun skēnē is the same word Hebrews uses for the tabernacle (8:2, 5; 9:2–3), the portable sanctuary of Israel’s wilderness journey. The tent is the symbol of a people on the way, not yet arrived.

He lived this way “with Isaac and Jacob, the fellow heirs of the same promise.” This is literally true in a way that may surprise modern readers. Abraham was a hundred years old when Isaac was born (Gen 21:5); Isaac was sixty when Jacob was born (Gen 25:26); and Abraham lived to be a hundred and seventy-five (Gen 25:7). So Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob shared the tent life together for about fifteen years, three generations of heirs living as foreigners in their own promised land. The promise was renewed to each of them: to Isaac (“Sojourn in this land … for to you and to your offspring I will give all these lands,” Gen 26:3) and to Jacob (Gen 28:13). The word “fellow heirs” (συγκληρονόμων synklēronomōn) is used in the New Testament of believers who share Christ’s inheritance (Rom 8:17; Eph 3:6; 1 Pet 3:7). The phrase “the same promise” reaches forward to the hearers themselves, whom Hebrews calls “the heirs of the promise” (6:17). They and the patriarchs are fellow heirs of a single hope.

For believers in the Roman world, the image was not abstract. Many early Christians knew what it was to be treated as outsiders in their own cities, and the letter to Diognetus, written in the second century, famously described Christians as living in their own homelands as though they were resident aliens, sharing in everything as citizens yet enduring everything as foreigners. Hebrews shows that this is no new thing. It is the ancestral pattern of the people of God.

11:10

for he was waiting for the city that has the foundations, whose designer and builder is God.

Why would a man who had been promised a land be content to live in tents in it? Verse 10 gives the reason (“for,” γάρ gar): Abraham was looking beyond the land to something greater. The verb “he was waiting for” (ἐξεδέχετο exedecheto, from ἐκδέχομαι ekdechomai) means to wait expectantly, to look out for. It is in the imperfect tense, which describes ongoing action: he kept on waiting, all his life. Hebrews used the same verb of Christ, who sits at God’s right hand “waiting from that time until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet” (10:13). Christ and Abraham both wait in faith for what God has promised.

The object of Abraham’s waiting is “the city that has the foundations” (τὴν τοὺς θεμελίους ἔχουσαν πόλιν tēn tous themelious echousan polin). Both nouns have the definite article: not “a city with foundations” but “the city that has the foundations,” as though there were only one city whose foundations truly deserved the name. The contrast with tents is deliberate. A tent is held down by pegs; it has no foundations at all. Abraham lived in tents because he was waiting for a city that could never be moved. Later, Hebrews names it: “the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22), and “the city that is to come” (13:14). The book of Revelation shows the same city descending from God, with “twelve foundations” bearing the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb (Rev 21:2, 14). Paul speaks of “the Jerusalem above” who is “our mother” (Gal 4:26). The psalmist had already sung of Zion, “His foundation is on the holy mountains” (Ps 87:1), and Isaiah had promised the afflicted city that God would lay her foundations with sapphires (Isa 54:11).

Did Abraham himself know of a heavenly city? Genesis does not say so in words. But Hebrews reads his behavior as evidence of his hope. A man who never settled, who confessed himself a stranger (Gen 23:4), and who refused to let his son return to the land he had left (Gen 24:6–8), showed by his life that the earthly land was not the full horizon of his expectation. Jesus said that Abraham “rejoiced to see my day; he saw it and was glad” (John 8:56). Paul calls Abraham “heir of the world” (Rom 4:13). The promise of Canaan was real, but it was a pledge and pattern of a greater inheritance, which faith grasped even when its outline was still dim.

Word Study: τεχνίτης καὶ δημιουργός technitēs kai dēmiourgos

The city’s maker is described with two nouns. τεχνίτης technitēs (compare English “technical”) means a craftsman, artisan, or designer, one who brings skill to a work (Acts 19:24; Rev 18:22). The Wisdom of Solomon complains that idolaters failed to recognize “the craftsman” (technitēs) while paying attention to his works (Wis 13:1). δημιουργός dēmiourgos, which occurs only here in the New Testament, means a builder, maker, or constructor. In Greek philosophy the word had a famous history: in Plato’s Timaeus the “Demiurge” is the craftsman who fashions the visible world after eternal patterns, and later Gnostic teachers would use the term for an inferior creator distinct from the highest God. Hebrews will have none of that. The city’s designer and its builder are one and the same, and he is God himself. Nothing about it is secondhand or human-made; it belongs with the “true tent that the Lord pitched, not man” (8:2) and the sanctuary “not made with hands” (9:11, 24). Together the two words say that God both planned the city and built it; it is entirely his work.

The contrast with the cities of the hearers’ world is sharp. Roman writers spoke of Rome as a city founded to last forever, and every ancient city boasted of its founders and its walls. But every earthly city, including Jerusalem with its temple, would prove impermanent; Hebrews says plainly, “here we have no lasting city” (13:14). The only city with unshakable foundations is the one God builds. To wait for it, as Abraham did, is not to despise the present world but to refuse to make it one’s final home.
