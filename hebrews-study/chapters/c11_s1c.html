Abraham and the Patriarchs (11:8–22)

11:8

By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed by going out to a place that he was going to receive as an inheritance; and he went out, not knowing where he was going.

With Abraham the catalogue reaches its center of gravity. More space is given to him (11:8–12, 17–19) than to anyone else except Moses, and the reflection of verses 13–16 grows out of his story. This is fitting. Abraham is the father of the faithful (Rom 4:11–12, 16), the man to whom God first gave “the promise” that runs through the whole of Hebrews (6:13–15), and the one whose “seed” the Son came to help (2:16). For Jewish hearers he was the great ancestor; for Christian hearers he was the model of believing God against all appearances.

The verse turns on two verbs of hearing and going. “When he was called” translates a present participle, καλούμενος kaloumenos, literally “being called.” The tense suggests that Abraham’s obedience followed the call at once, while the call was still, as it were, sounding in his ears. The main verb is ὑπήκουσεν hypēkousen, “obeyed,” from ὑπακούω hypakouō, a compound of “under” and “hear”: to listen in submission, to hear and act. In both Greek and Hebrew, obeying is a form of hearing; the Hebrew verb שָׁמַע shamaʿ means both. There is a natural link with the description of Jesus, who “learned obedience” and became the source of salvation “to all who obey him” (5:8–9). Faith, in Hebrews, is never passive assent. It is hearing that gets up and goes.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 12:1, 4

Hebrew (12:1): וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־אַבְרָם לֶךְ־לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ וּמִמּוֹלַדְתְּךָ וּמִבֵּית אָבִיךָ אֶל־הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר אַרְאֶךָּ — vayyomer YHWH el-avram: lekh-lekha me’artsekha umimmoladtekha umibbeit avikha el-ha’arets asher ar’ekka, “And the Lord said to Abram, ‘Go, for your own sake, from your land and from your kindred and from your father’s house, to the land that I will show you.’”

Hebrew (12:4a): וַיֵּלֶךְ אַבְרָם כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר אֵלָיו יְהוָה — vayyelekh avram ka’asher dibber elav YHWH, “So Abram went, as the Lord had spoken to him.”

The command lists three circles of belonging, each closer than the last: country, clan, and father’s household. In the ancient Near East these were a person’s protection, livelihood, inheritance, and identity. To leave them was to become vulnerable, a man without rights in someone else’s land. The destination is left unnamed: “the land that I will show you.” The verb is future; the land had not yet been shown. Genesis then records the response with the utmost simplicity: “So Abram went.” Joshua later reminded Israel that Abraham’s family “served other gods” beyond the Euphrates (Josh 24:2), so the call also meant leaving the religion of his fathers. Stephen, in Acts 7:2–4, adds that God first appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, before he settled in Haran (compare Gen 11:31).

Abraham obeyed “by going out” (ἐξελθεῖν exelthein), and the verse ends by repeating the verb: “and he went out” (ἐξῆλθεν exēlthen). “Going out” is a theme to watch. Joseph will speak of the “exodus,” the going out of Israel (11:22); the patriarchs will be described as having “gone out” of their former land (11:15); and the climactic exhortation of the letter will be, “So let us go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach; for here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come” (13:13–14). Abraham’s going out is the pattern for the believer’s life.

He went out “to a place that he was going to receive as an inheritance.” The verb ἤμελλεν ēmellen, “he was going to,” points to the future, and the inheritance (κληρονομία klēronomia) is the same family of words used of Noah in the previous verse and of believers who “inherit the promises” through faith and patience (6:12). Abraham never actually took possession of the land in his lifetime (Acts 7:5); he received it only as a promise for his descendants. And he went “not knowing where he was going” (μὴ ἐπιστάμενος ποῦ ἔρχεται mē epistamenos pou erchetai). The participle epistamenos means “understanding, knowing with certainty.” What Abraham lacked was not trust but information. He knew who had called him, but not where the road would lead.

That is the heart of faith as Hebrews describes it: the willingness to obey God’s word without seeing the end of the road. It is the opposite of the attitude of the wilderness generation, who had seen God’s works yet would not go forward when he told them to (3:7–19). The hearers stood at a similar point. Following Christ had already cost them their property and their standing (10:32–34), and they could not see where the path would lead next. The preacher’s word to them, through Abraham, is that it is enough to know the One who calls.

11:9

By faith he lived as a sojourner in the land of promise as in a foreign land, dwelling in tents with Isaac and Jacob, the fellow heirs of the same promise;

Abraham’s faith did not end with his arrival. It had to continue in a long life of unsettledness. The verb “lived as a sojourner” (παρῴκησεν parōkēsen, from παροικέω paroikeō) means to live alongside others as a resident alien, someone who lives in a place without belonging to it or having the rights of a citizen. Its noun, πάροικος paroikos, is the common Greek Old Testament translation for the Hebrew גֵּר ger, “sojourner, resident alien,” and the verb renders the Hebrew גּוּר gur, “to sojourn.” Genesis describes Canaan precisely this way. God promised Abraham, וְנָתַתִּי לְךָ וּלְזַרְעֲךָ אַחֲרֶיךָ אֵת אֶרֶץ מְגֻרֶיךָ venatatti lekha ulezarʿakha acharekha et erets megurekha, “I will give to you and to your offspring after you the land of your sojournings” (Gen 17:8). The land was promised, yet the patriarchs lived in it as foreigners.

Hebrews makes the paradox plain. Abraham lived “in the land of promise” (εἰς γῆν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας eis gēn tēs epangelias), the land that God had pledged to him, and yet he lived there “as in a foreign land” (ὡς ἀλλοτρίαν hōs allotrian, literally “as belonging to another”). The only piece of it he ever owned was a grave, the cave of Machpelah, which he bought from the Hittites to bury Sarah (Gen 23). Stephen summed it up: God “gave him no inheritance in it, not even a foot’s length, but promised to give it to him as a possession and to his offspring after him” (Acts 7:5).

He lived “in tents” (ἐν σκηναῖς en skēnais). Genesis repeatedly mentions Abraham pitching his tent (Gen 12:8; 13:3, 18; 18:1). The tent is the dwelling of the nomad and the pilgrim: portable, temporary, without foundations. Interestingly, the noun skēnē is the same word Hebrews uses for the tabernacle (8:2, 5; 9:2–3), the portable sanctuary of Israel’s wilderness journey. The tent is the symbol of a people on the way, not yet arrived.

He lived this way “with Isaac and Jacob, the fellow heirs of the same promise.” This is literally true in a way that may surprise modern readers. Abraham was a hundred years old when Isaac was born (Gen 21:5); Isaac was sixty when Jacob was born (Gen 25:26); and Abraham lived to be a hundred and seventy-five (Gen 25:7). So Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob shared the tent life together for about fifteen years, three generations of heirs living as foreigners in their own promised land. The promise was renewed to each of them: to Isaac (“Sojourn in this land … for to you and to your offspring I will give all these lands,” Gen 26:3) and to Jacob (Gen 28:13). The word “fellow heirs” (συγκληρονόμων synklēronomōn) is used in the New Testament of believers who share Christ’s inheritance (Rom 8:17; Eph 3:6; 1 Pet 3:7). The phrase “the same promise” reaches forward to the hearers themselves, whom Hebrews calls “the heirs of the promise” (6:17). They and the patriarchs are fellow heirs of a single hope.

For believers in the Roman world, the image was not abstract. Many early Christians knew what it was to be treated as outsiders in their own cities, and the letter to Diognetus, written in the second century, famously described Christians as living in their own homelands as though they were resident aliens, sharing in everything as citizens yet enduring everything as foreigners. Hebrews shows that this is no new thing. It is the ancestral pattern of the people of God.

11:10

for he was waiting for the city that has the foundations, whose designer and builder is God.

Why would a man who had been promised a land be content to live in tents in it? Verse 10 gives the reason (“for,” γάρ gar): Abraham was looking beyond the land to something greater. The verb “he was waiting for” (ἐξεδέχετο exedecheto, from ἐκδέχομαι ekdechomai) means to wait expectantly, to look out for. It is in the imperfect tense, which describes ongoing action: he kept on waiting, all his life. Hebrews used the same verb of Christ, who sits at God’s right hand “waiting from that time until his enemies are made a footstool for his feet” (10:13). Christ and Abraham both wait in faith for what God has promised.

The object of Abraham’s waiting is “the city that has the foundations” (τὴν τοὺς θεμελίους ἔχουσαν πόλιν tēn tous themelious echousan polin). Both nouns have the definite article: not “a city with foundations” but “the city that has the foundations,” as though there were only one city whose foundations truly deserved the name. The contrast with tents is deliberate. A tent is held down by pegs; it has no foundations at all. Abraham lived in tents because he was waiting for a city that could never be moved. Later, Hebrews names it: “the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22), and “the city that is to come” (13:14). The book of Revelation shows the same city descending from God, with “twelve foundations” bearing the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb (Rev 21:2, 14). Paul speaks of “the Jerusalem above” who is “our mother” (Gal 4:26). The psalmist had already sung of Zion, “His foundation is on the holy mountains” (Ps 87:1), and Isaiah had promised the afflicted city that God would lay her foundations with sapphires (Isa 54:11).

Did Abraham himself know of a heavenly city? Genesis does not say so in words. But Hebrews reads his behavior as evidence of his hope. A man who never settled, who confessed himself a stranger (Gen 23:4), and who refused to let his son return to the land he had left (Gen 24:6–8), showed by his life that the earthly land was not the full horizon of his expectation. Jesus said that Abraham “rejoiced to see my day; he saw it and was glad” (John 8:56). Paul calls Abraham “heir of the world” (Rom 4:13). The promise of Canaan was real, but it was a pledge and pattern of a greater inheritance, which faith grasped even when its outline was still dim.

Word Study: τεχνίτης καὶ δημιουργός technitēs kai dēmiourgos

The city’s maker is described with two nouns. τεχνίτης technitēs (compare English “technical”) means a craftsman, artisan, or designer, one who brings skill to a work (Acts 19:24; Rev 18:22). The Wisdom of Solomon complains that idolaters failed to recognize “the craftsman” (technitēs) while paying attention to his works (Wis 13:1). δημιουργός dēmiourgos, which occurs only here in the New Testament, means a builder, maker, or constructor. In Greek philosophy the word had a famous history: in Plato’s Timaeus the “Demiurge” is the craftsman who fashions the visible world after eternal patterns, and later Gnostic teachers would use the term for an inferior creator distinct from the highest God. Hebrews will have none of that. The city’s designer and its builder are one and the same, and he is God himself. Nothing about it is secondhand or human-made; it belongs with the “true tent that the Lord pitched, not man” (8:2) and the sanctuary “not made with hands” (9:11, 24). Together the two words say that God both planned the city and built it; it is entirely his work.

The contrast with the cities of the hearers’ world is sharp. Roman writers spoke of Rome as a city founded to last forever, and every ancient city boasted of its founders and its walls. But every earthly city, including Jerusalem with its temple, would prove impermanent; Hebrews says plainly, “here we have no lasting city” (13:14). The only city with unshakable foundations is the one God builds. To wait for it, as Abraham did, is not to despise the present world but to refuse to make it one’s final home.

11:11

By faith—together with Sarah herself—he received power to beget offspring, even beyond the season of life, since he considered faithful the one who had promised.

The next example moves from Abraham’s journey to the promise of a son, and it presents one of the most debated sentences in Hebrews. The story itself is clear enough. God had promised Abraham offspring as numerous as the stars (Gen 15:5), but years passed, and Abraham and Sarah grew old. When God repeated the promise, Abraham laughed and said in his heart, הַלְּבֶן מֵאָה־שָׁנָה יִוָּלֵד וְאִם־שָׂרָה הֲבַת־תִּשְׁעִים שָׁנָה תֵּלֵד hallevan me’ah-shanah yivvaled, ve’im-sarah havat-tishʿim shanah teled, “Shall a child be born to a man a hundred years old? And shall Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?” (Gen 17:17). Genesis adds that Sarah was past the age of childbearing (חָדַל לִהְיוֹת לְשָׂרָה אֹרַח כַּנָּשִׁים chadal lihyot lesarah orach kannashim, “it had ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women,” Gen 18:11), and that when she overheard the promise, she too laughed (18:12). The Lord answered with a question that is the theme of the whole episode: הֲיִפָּלֵא מֵיְהוָה דָּבָר hayippale meYHWH davar, “Is anything too wonderful for the Lord?” (18:14). A year later, “the Lord visited Sarah as he had said … and Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age” (21:1–2).

The question is: whose faith does Hebrews 11:11 commend, Abraham’s or Sarah’s? The answer depends on a small matter of spelling and a larger matter of idiom.

Textual Note: “Sarah herself”

The earliest manuscripts of the New Testament were written in capital letters, without spaces between words and without accents or other marks. The words for “Sarah herself” would have appeared simply as ΑΥΤΗΣΑΡΡΑ. These letters can be read in two ways. With the later marks added one way, they are nominative, αὐτὴ Σάρρα autē Sarra, “Sarah herself,” making Sarah the subject of the sentence. Added the other way, they are dative, αὐτῇ Σάρρᾳ autē Sarra (with a small “iota subscript” under the final vowels), “with Sarah herself,” making Sarah a companion in the action of someone else. The edition used for this study prints the dative. In addition, a number of manuscripts, some of them early, add the word στεῖρα steira, “barren” (“Sarah herself, who was barren”), and many later manuscripts add the verb “she gave birth” after “beyond the season of life.” That longer form lies behind the King James Version: “Through faith also Sara herself received strength to conceive seed, and was delivered of a child when she was past age.” The additions look like scribal attempts to make Sarah the subject clearer. Modern editions and translations are divided: some keep Sarah as subject, others make Abraham the subject.

Word Study: δύναμιν εἰς καταβολὴν σπέρματος dynamin eis katabolēn spermatos

Literally, “power for the laying down of seed.” The noun καταβολή katabolē means “a throwing down, a laying down,” and so also “a foundation” (Hebrews uses it in the phrase “the foundation of the world,” 4:3; 9:26). Combined with σπέρμα sperma, “seed,” it was a standard Greek expression for the father’s part in procreation, the depositing of seed that begins a new life. Greek writers used different language for the mother’s part, the receiving and nurturing of seed. That is why the phrase is the crux of the problem: if Sarah is the subject, the sentence says that she received power to do what, in ordinary Greek usage, only a father does.

Interpretive Options: Sarah or Abraham?

  • Sarah is the subject: “By faith Sarah herself, though barren, received power to conceive, even beyond the age for it.” This is the reading of the Latin Vulgate, the King James Version, many older interpreters, and some modern translations. In its favor: it is the most natural way to read the nominative text; it gives a woman a place in the catalogue alongside Rahab (11:31) and the women of 11:35; and Scripture elsewhere honors Sarah as a model for believing women (1 Pet 3:5–6). Her laughter in Genesis 18:12 need not rule her out, since many in this chapter had faith that was imperfect. Defenders explain the difficult phrase in one of two ways: either it is used loosely for “conceiving” (receiving seed), or katabolē is taken in its other sense, “foundation,” giving the meaning “power to found a posterity.”
  • Abraham is the subject: “By faith—together with Sarah herself—he received power to beget offspring.” On this reading, Abraham, who has been the subject since verse 8 (“he lived as a sojourner … he was waiting”), remains the subject, and Sarah is mentioned in a dative phrase of association: she was included in the miracle with him. In its favor: (1) the expression “laying down of seed” naturally describes the father’s role; (2) verse 12 continues, “therefore from one man, and him as good as dead,” where “one” and “as good as dead” are masculine and refer to Abraham, which flows smoothly if Abraham is already the subject; (3) the final clause, “since he considered faithful the one who had promised,” closely matches Paul’s description of Abraham in Romans 4:19–21: “he did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was as good as dead (the same verb as Heb 11:12) … and the deadness of Sarah’s womb … being fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised.” A number of modern commentators, among them F. F. Bruce in his revised commentary and William Lane, adopt this reading.

Weighing the evidence, the reading with Abraham as subject is the more probable. It respects the ordinary meaning of the Greek idiom, it fits the flow from verse 10 to verse 12, it matches the dative form printed in the text used for this study, and it agrees with the apostolic interpretation of the same event in Romans 4. Genesis itself centers the promise of seed on Abraham’s faith (“he believed the Lord, and he counted it to him as righteousness,” Gen 15:6). Yet the verse does not exclude Sarah. On any reading she is named, and the phrase “Sarah herself” honors her: the miracle was hers as much as his, for it was her womb, “dead” as surely as his body, that God brought to life (Rom 4:19). Hebrews joins husband and wife in the one act of God’s power. This conclusion is held with appropriate humility; the alternative is ancient and respectable, and nothing in the meaning of the passage as a whole depends on the choice. Either way, the point stands: God gave life where there was none, because his promise was trusted.

The phrase “even beyond the season of life” (καὶ παρὰ καιρὸν ἡλικίας kai para kairon hēlikias) means “past the proper time of life,” beyond the age when having children was possible. The preposition para, “beyond, contrary to,” stresses that the birth went against nature. That was the point of the long delay. God waited until the human possibility was gone, so that the child would be unmistakably the gift of his power and the fruit of his promise. Isaac’s very name, יִצְחָק yitschaq, “he laughs,” remembered the laughter of disbelief that God turned into laughter of joy (Gen 21:6).

The ground of faith is stated in the closing clause: “since he considered faithful the one who had promised” (ἐπεὶ πιστὸν ἡγήσατο τὸν ἐπαγγειλάμενον epei piston hēgēsato ton epangeilamenon). The verb ἡγέομαι hēgeomai means “to regard, consider, judge”; it describes a settled evaluation. The words echo, almost exactly, the preacher’s earlier exhortation: “Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful” (πιστὸς γὰρ ὁ ἐπαγγειλάμενος pistos gar ho epangeilamenos, 10:23). Faith rests not on its own strength but on the faithfulness of the God who promised. Abraham looked at his own body and Sarah’s, saw only death, and then looked at the One who had spoken, and judged him trustworthy. That judgment is what faith is.

11:12

Therefore from one man, and him as good as dead, there were born descendants as many as the stars of heaven in multitude and as the innumerable sand that is by the shore of the sea.

The result of Abraham’s faith is stated with a contrast as strong as language can make it: on one side, “one man,” and he “as good as dead”; on the other, a people as countless as the stars and the sand. The word “therefore” (διό dio) ties the result to the faith of verse 11: because he trusted the God who promised, the promise came to pass.

“From one man” (ἀφ’ ἑνός aph’ henos) recalls Isaiah’s word to the exiles, who felt few and weak: הַבִּיטוּ אֶל־אַבְרָהָם אֲבִיכֶם וְאֶל־שָׂרָה תְּחוֹלֶלְכֶם כִּי־אֶחָד קְרָאתִיו וַאֲבָרְכֵהוּ וְאַרְבֵּהוּ habbitu el-avraham avikhem ve’el-sarah techolelkhem, ki-echad qera’tiv va’avarekhehu ve’arbehu, “Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you; for he was but one when I called him, and I blessed him and multiplied him” (Isa 51:2). God’s way is to begin with one, and from that one to make a multitude. Hebrews has already shown that the Son and those he sanctifies are all “from one” (2:11, the same Greek phrase).

“And him as good as dead” renders καὶ ταῦτα νενεκρωμένου kai tauta nenekrōmenou. The phrase kai tauta, literally “and these things,” is a Greek idiom meaning “and that too,” “and what is more.” The participle nenekrōmenou comes from νεκρόω nekroō, “to make dead,” and is in the perfect tense: Abraham was in a state of deadness as far as fathering children was concerned. Paul uses the same verb of Abraham’s body (Rom 4:19). The language is intentionally strong. From a source that was, humanly speaking, dead, God brought forth life. It is the same pattern that will appear in verse 19, when Abraham believes that God can raise the dead, and that will reach its fulfillment in the resurrection of Jesus (13:20).

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:17 (with 15:5; 32:12; Deut 1:10)

Hebrew: כִּי־בָרֵךְ אֲבָרֶכְךָ וְהַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה אֶת־זַרְעֲךָ כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם וְכַחוֹל אֲשֶׁר עַל־שְׂפַת הַיָּם — ki-varekh avarekhekha, veharbah arbeh et-zarʿakha kekhokhvei hashamayim vekhachol asher ʿal-sefat hayyam, “I will surely bless you, and I will surely multiply your offspring as the stars of heaven and as the sand that is on the shore of the sea.”

The double verbs (“blessing I will bless … multiplying I will multiply”) are the Hebrew way of expressing certainty and intensity. The expression “the shore of the sea” is literally “the lip of the sea” (שְׂפַת הַיָּם sefat hayyam), and Hebrews’ Greek keeps the image: τὸ χεῖλος τῆς θαλάσσης to cheilos tēs thalassēs, “the lip of the sea.” The stars were first promised in Genesis 15:5, when God brought Abraham outside and told him to count them if he could. Jacob later recalled the promise of offspring “like the sand of the sea, which cannot be counted for multitude” (אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יִסָּפֵר מֵרֹב asher lo-yissafer merov, Gen 32:13 Hebrew; 32:12 English), which explains Hebrews’ word “innumerable” (ἀναρίθμητος anarithmētos). And Moses could tell Israel on the plains of Moab, וְהִנְּכֶם הַיּוֹם כְּכוֹכְבֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם לָרֹב vehinnekhem hayyom kekhokhvei hashamayim larov, “and behold, you are today as the stars of heaven in multitude” (Deut 1:10). Hebrews’ phrase “in multitude” (τῷ πλήθει tō plēthei) matches the Hebrew larov.

The physical descendants of Abraham did become a great nation. But the New Testament sees the promise reaching further still. Paul writes that “those who are of faith are the sons of Abraham” and “if you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s offspring, heirs according to promise” (Gal 3:7, 29). Hebrews says that the Son came to help “the seed of Abraham” (2:16). The hearers of this sermon, Jewish and Gentile believers in Jesus, were themselves part of the innumerable multitude that sprang from one man as good as dead. Every believer is living proof that God keeps his word.

11:13

In faith these all died, not having received the promises, but having seen them from afar and greeted them, and having confessed that they were strangers and exiles on the earth.

Here the preacher pauses for the second time to reflect on what his examples mean (compare 11:6). The rhythm of “by faith … by faith” is interrupted. Instead of πίστει pistei, “by faith,” the sentence begins κατὰ πίστιν kata pistin, “according to faith,” “in the way of faith.” The change is significant. The patriarchs did not die “by faith” as though death were an achievement of faith; they died in faith, still holding to it, their faith intact to the end. They died as they had lived, believing.

“These all” (οὗτοι πάντες houtoi pantes) most naturally refers to the patriarchs just named, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, and Jacob, whose life of sojourning has been described (11:8–12). It cannot include Enoch, who did not see death (11:5). It may glance back at Abel and Noah as well, but the language of strangers, homeland, and city belongs to the patriarchal story.

They died “not having received the promises” (μὴ λαβόντες τὰς ἐπαγγελίας mē labontes tas epangelias). This may seem to contradict 6:15, where Abraham, “having patiently waited, obtained the promise.” But there is no contradiction. In 6:15 the promise is the birth of Isaac and the oath confirming the blessing (Gen 22:16–17), which Abraham did see. Here the “promises” in the plural are the full content of what God pledged: the land as a permanent possession, the countless offspring, the blessing of all nations through Abraham’s seed (Gen 12:3), and above all the city of God. These they did not receive in their lifetimes. They saw only the first installment, a single son and a burial plot. Hebrews will say the same, at the end of the chapter, of everyone in the list: “all these … did not receive what was promised” (11:39). The full inheritance awaits the coming of Christ and the consummation that he will bring.

Yet they were not without the promises altogether. They had “seen them from afar and greeted them” (πόρρωθεν αὐτὰς ἰδόντες καὶ ἀσπασάμενοι porrōthen autas idontes kai aspasamenoi). The verb ἀσπάζομαι aspazomai is the ordinary word for greeting someone, the word used in the closing greetings of letters (13:24, “greet all your leaders”). The picture is of travelers who catch sight of their destination on the far horizon and wave to it, or of sailors who sight the coast of home and salute it, though they cannot yet land. Faith sees what is far off and welcomes it as though it were near. Jesus said, “Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day; he saw it and was glad” (John 8:56).

Old Testament Text: Genesis 23:4

Hebrew: גֵּר־וְתוֹשָׁב אָנֹכִי עִמָּכֶם תְּנוּ לִי אֲחֻזַּת־קֶבֶר עִמָּכֶם וְאֶקְבְּרָה מֵתִי מִלְּפָנָי — ger-vetoshav anokhi ʿimmakhem; tenu li achuzzat-qever ʿimmakhem, ve’eqberah meti millefanai, “A resident alien and a sojourner am I among you; give me property for a grave among you, so that I may bury my dead out of my sight.”

Abraham spoke these words to the Hittites of Hebron when Sarah died. The two Hebrew terms describe the legal and social status of an outsider. A גֵּר ger was a foreigner living among a people not his own, without the land rights of a native; a תּוֹשָׁב toshav was a settler or temporary resident, someone who lived in a place but did not belong to it. Together the phrase means “I am a foreigner living here without a stake in the land.” The irony is deep: the man to whom God had promised all the land had to ask permission to buy a grave in it. The patriarchs repeated the confession. Jacob told Pharaoh that the days of his life were “the days of the years of my sojournings” (מְגוּרַי megurai, Gen 47:9). Israel’s later worship turned it into prayer: David confessed, כִּי־גֵרִים אֲנַחְנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ וְתוֹשָׁבִים כְּכָל־אֲבֹתֵינוּ ki-gerim anachnu lefanekha vetoshavim kekhol-avotenu, “For we are resident aliens before you, and sojourners, as all our fathers were” (1 Chr 29:15; compare Ps 39:13 Hebrew; 39:12 English).

Hebrews renders the confession with two Greek nouns: ξένοι xenoi, “strangers, foreigners,” and παρεπίδημοι parepidēmoi, “exiles, temporary residents,” people staying for a while in a place that is not their home. Peter uses the second word when he addresses believers as “exiles of the dispersion” and urges them “as sojourners and exiles” to abstain from fleshly desires (1 Pet 1:1; 2:11). In the Greco-Roman city, the resident foreigner was a recognized category: he could live and trade there, but he could not vote, often could not own land, and was easily suspected. That was the patriarchs’ status, and Hebrews widens it. Abraham called himself an alien in Canaan; Hebrews says they were strangers “on the earth” (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς epi tēs gēs). The Hebrew word אֶרֶץ erets and the Greek word gē can both mean either “land” or “earth,” and the preacher draws out the larger sense: their alienation was not merely from one territory but from the present world as a whole.

The participle “having confessed” (ὁμολογήσαντες homologēsantes) is a significant word in Hebrews. Its noun, “confession” (ὁμολογία homologia), describes the Christian profession of faith that the hearers must hold fast (3:1; 4:14; 10:23). The patriarchs made a public declaration about who they were and where they belonged, and they did not take it back. The hearers, publicly shamed for their confession of Christ (10:33), are invited to see that their position is not a disgrace but the ancient vocation of God’s people.

11:14

For those who say such things make it plain that they are seeking a homeland.

The preacher now reasons from the patriarchs’ confession to its meaning. Anyone who calls himself a stranger is implicitly saying that he belongs somewhere else. “Those who say such things” (οἱ … τοιαῦτα λέγοντες hoi … toiauta legontes) “make it plain” (ἐμφανίζουσιν emphanizousin, “show clearly, declare openly”) that they are “seeking a homeland.”

The word for homeland, πατρίς patris, is related to the word for “father” (patēr); it means “fatherland,” one’s native country, the place where one truly belongs. In the ancient world, one’s patris was a central part of identity. People were known by their city (Saul “of Tarsus,” Jesus “of Nazareth”), and to be cut off from one’s homeland was among the bitterest of misfortunes. The patriarchs had left their earthly patris in Mesopotamia and had not yet received a new one. Their confession was that they were still looking.

The verb “they are seeking” (ἐπιζητοῦσιν epizētousin) is a strengthened form of “seek,” meaning to search for earnestly, to strive after. It is the same verb Hebrews uses in the climactic statement of 13:14: “For here we have no lasting city, but we seek (ἐπιζητοῦμεν epizētoumen) the city that is to come.” The link is deliberate. What the patriarchs sought, the hearers seek; they share the same pilgrim identity. It also echoes 11:6, where God rewards those who “seek him out.” The search for the homeland and the search for God are, in the end, one search, for the homeland is the place where God dwells with his people.

The verse quietly asks a probing question of every reader: what do your words and your life “make plain” about where you think your home is? The patriarchs’ confession was credible because their lives matched it. They lived in tents. They were in no hurry to secure a permanent stake in the world as it is. Their speech and their conduct testified together that their hearts were set on another country.

11:15

If they had been mindful of that land from which they had gone out, they would have had opportunity to return.

The preacher anticipates a possible misunderstanding. Perhaps the homeland the patriarchs were seeking was simply the country they had left, Mesopotamia, Ur and Haran? No, he says, for if that had been their longing, nothing stopped them from going back. The sentence is a condition contrary to fact: the main clause, εἶχον ἂν καιρὸν ἀνακάμψαι eichon an kairon anakampsai, “they would have had opportunity to return,” uses the imperfect tense with the particle an, the Greek way of saying “this would have been so, but it was not.”

The verb in the if-clause is μνημονεύουσιν mnēmoneuousin, “they were remembering, were mindful of,” which means more than recalling a fact; it means to keep something in mind, to dwell on it with longing. The patriarchs did not live with their hearts turned back toward the land “from which they had gone out” (ἐξέβησαν exebēsan). The same verb “remember” returns in verse 22, where Joseph, at his death, “made mention” (literally “remembered”) of the exodus. The patriarchs did not remember the land behind them; Joseph remembered the promise before him. Faith’s memory looks forward.

The opportunity to return was real. The road to Mesopotamia remained open; Abraham’s family lived there. Abraham sent his servant back to his kindred to find a wife for Isaac (Gen 24), and Jacob himself lived twenty years in Haran (Gen 31:38, 41). But Abraham was emphatic that Isaac must never go back to live there: הִשָּׁמֶר לְךָ פֶּן־תָּשִׁיב אֶת־בְּנִי שָׁמָּה hishshamer lekha pen-tashiv et-beni shammah, “Take care that you do not take my son back there” (Gen 24:6; compare 24:8). And Jacob, after his years in Haran, returned to Canaan at God’s command (Gen 31:3, 13). The family had every opportunity to go back to the comfortable, settled civilization of their origins, and they refused.

The verb “to return” (ἀνακάμπτω anakamptō, “to bend back, turn back”) brings the application close. The hearers too had an opportunity to return. They could go back to the life they had known before they confessed Christ, back to the security of the synagogue’s recognized status, back to the respectability their neighbors would grant if they quietly dropped their confession. This is precisely the danger the whole letter addresses: drifting away (2:1), falling away (3:12; 6:6), shrinking back (10:38–39). The patriarchs show that the door back is always open, and that faith declines to walk through it. As Jesus said, “No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God” (Luke 9:62).

11:16

But as it is, they long for a better one, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God; for he has prepared for them a city.

“But as it is” (νῦν δέ nyn de) introduces the reality over against the supposition of verse 15. They did not long for the land behind them; “they long for a better one.” The verb ὀρέγονται oregontai means “to stretch out for, to reach toward, to aspire to,” the posture of a person straining forward to grasp something (compare 1 Tim 3:1). It is present tense: this longing was the continuing direction of their lives.

What they longed for was “better” (κρείττονος kreittonos). This is one of the signature words of Hebrews, which speaks of a better name (1:4), a better hope (7:19), a better covenant with better promises (7:22; 8:6), better sacrifices (9:23), a better possession (10:34), a better resurrection (11:35), something better for us (11:40), and blood that speaks better than Abel’s (12:24). Everything God gives through Christ is “better” than what preceded it, and the patriarchs, Hebrews says, were already longing for the “better” thing. Then the preacher defines it: “that is, a heavenly one” (τοῦτ’ ἔστιν ἐπουρανίου tout’ estin epouraniou). Believers are “partakers of a heavenly calling” (3:1); they have tasted “the heavenly gift” (6:4); they have come to “the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22).

Two misunderstandings should be avoided here. First, “heavenly” does not mean unreal or merely spiritual in the sense of disembodied. In Hebrews the heavenly realm is where the true realities are: the true sanctuary, the true tent, the living God himself (8:1–5; 9:24). The earthly things are copies and shadows; the heavenly things are the substance. Second, this verse does not cancel God’s promise of land as though it had been a mistake. The earthly land was a real gift and a real pledge, but its fulfillment is larger than any territory in the Middle East. The promise expands into “the world to come” (2:5), the unshakable kingdom (12:28), the inheritance of a renewed creation in which God dwells with his people (Rev 21:1–3). Jesus promised that the meek “shall inherit the earth” (Matt 5:5). The heavenly country is not the abolition of the promise but its full flowering.

“Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God” (διὸ οὐκ ἐπαισχύνεται αὐτοὺς ὁ θεὸς θεὸς ἐπικαλεῖσθαι αὐτῶν dio ouk epaischynetai autous ho theos theos epikaleisthai autōn). The Greek places the word “God” twice in succession, “God … God,” which gives the sentence a solemn ring: God is not ashamed to have “God” attached to their names. The reference is to the way God named himself when he appeared to Moses at the burning bush: אָנֹכִי אֱלֹהֵי אָבִיךָ אֱלֹהֵי אַבְרָהָם אֱלֹהֵי יִצְחָק וֵאלֹהֵי יַעֲקֹב anokhi elohei avikha, elohei avraham, elohei yitschaq, velohei yaʿaqov, “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” (Exod 3:6; compare 3:15–16).

Historical Background: Honor, Shame, and a Patron’s Name

In the ancient Mediterranean world, honor and shame were central social values. A powerful person linked his name with others only if it brought him honor; to be publicly associated with people of low status could shame him. The gods of the nations were typically identified with great cities and powerful peoples: the gods of Babylon, of Egypt, of Athens. By contrast, the God of heaven and earth chose to identify himself by the names of three nomads who owned nothing but a grave, lived in tents, and called themselves foreigners. He was “not ashamed” of them. The verb ἐπαισχύνομαι epaischynomai, “to be ashamed of,” appeared earlier in Hebrews in a closely parallel statement: the Son “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:11). The hearers had suffered shame for their faith (10:33; 13:13); Jesus himself “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2). The good news of this verse is that God and his Son publicly own those whom the world dishonors. To be called “his” is the highest honor there is. The opposite warning is also in Scripture: Jesus will be ashamed of those who are ashamed of him and his words (Mark 8:38).

When Jesus answered the Sadducees about the resurrection, he appealed to this same self-designation of God: “Have you not read in the book of Moses, in the passage about the bush, how God spoke to him, saying, ‘I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob’? He is not God of the dead, but of the living” (Mark 12:26–27). God does not attach his name to people whom death will finally swallow. The very name “the God of Abraham” is a pledge that Abraham will live and inherit. Hebrews makes a parallel inference: God’s willingness to be called their God proves that he has a future for them.

The final clause supplies the evidence: “for he has prepared for them a city” (ἡτοίμασεν γὰρ αὐτοῖς πόλιν hētoimasen gar autois polin). God’s commitment is not mere words; he has acted. The city Abraham was waiting for (11:10) is already prepared. Jesus spoke the same way to his disciples: “In my Father’s house are many dwelling places … I go to prepare a place for you” (John 14:2), and of the kingdom “prepared for you from the foundation of the world” (Matt 25:34). In the ancient world a host who invited guests prepared a place for them; a patron who acknowledged his clients provided for them. God, who is not ashamed to be called their God, has made ready their eternal home. The pilgrims are not wandering aimlessly. They are walking toward a prepared place, and the One who prepared it is waiting to receive them.

11:17

By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac; he who had received the promises was offering up his only son,

After the reflective pause of verses 13–16, the preacher returns to Abraham for the supreme test of his life, the event Jewish tradition calls the Akedah, “the binding” of Isaac (from the Hebrew verb in Gen 22:9, “he bound Isaac his son”). It was the most celebrated example of faith in Jewish memory. Sirach says of Abraham that “in testing he was found faithful” (Sir 44:20). Mattathias, on his deathbed, asked his sons, “Was not Abraham found faithful in testing, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness?” (1 Macc 2:52). The mother of the Maccabean martyrs urged her sons to remember Abraham, who hastened to sacrifice his son Isaac (4 Macc 16:20). James appeals to the same event to show that Abraham’s faith was a working faith (Jas 2:21–23). For the hearers of Hebrews, who might be called on to give up what was dearest to them for the sake of God’s promise, no example could be more searching.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 22:1–2

Hebrew (22:1a): וַיְהִי אַחַר הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה וְהָאֱלֹהִים נִסָּה אֶת־אַבְרָהָם — vayhi achar haddevarim ha’elleh veha’elohim nissah et-avraham, “And after these things God tested Abraham.”

Hebrew (22:2): קַח־נָא אֶת־בִּנְךָ אֶת־יְחִידְךָ אֲשֶׁר־אָהַבְתָּ אֶת־יִצְחָק וְלֶךְ־לְךָ אֶל־אֶרֶץ הַמֹּרִיָּה וְהַעֲלֵהוּ שָׁם לְעֹלָה עַל אַחַד הֶהָרִים אֲשֶׁר אֹמַר אֵלֶיךָ — qach-na et-binkha et-yechidkha asher-ahavta et-yitschaq, velekh-lekha el-erets hammoriyyah, veha’alehu sham leʿolah ʿal achad heharim asher omar elekha, “Take your son, your only one, whom you love, Isaac, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him up there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains of which I will tell you.”

The narrator tells the reader at once what Abraham did not know: this was a test (נִסָּה nissah, “tested, proved”). The command piles up phrases that tighten the knot with each word: “your son … your only one … whom you love … Isaac.” The same command, לֶךְ־לְךָ lekh-lekha, “go,” that began Abraham’s journey (Gen 12:1) now sends him on its hardest stage. The first time he was asked to give up his past; now he is asked to give up his future. Later tradition identified Moriah with the hill on which Solomon built the temple (2 Chr 3:1).

“When he was tested” translates πειραζόμενος peirazomenos, a present passive participle of πειράζω peirazō, which can mean either “tempt” (entice to sin) or “test” (prove the genuineness of something). God never tempts anyone to evil (Jas 1:13); he tests his people to reveal and strengthen their faith, as gold is tested by fire (Deut 8:2; 1 Pet 1:6–7). Hebrews uses the same verb of Jesus, who was “tested in every way as we are, yet without sin” (4:15; compare 2:18). The hearers were in their own season of testing.

The verse contains a remarkable shift of tenses that English can only partly reproduce. The first verb, “offered up,” is προσενήνοχεν prosenēnochen, a perfect tense: “he has offered.” The perfect presents the offering as an accomplished act whose significance abides. In intention and in God’s sight, the sacrifice was complete; Abraham had given Isaac to God, and Scripture permanently records it so. The second verb, “was offering up,” is προσέφερεν prosepheren, an imperfect tense, which describes an action in progress: he was in the act of offering him, he was on the point of doing it. Grammarians call this a “conative” imperfect, describing an action attempted but not carried through. Abraham did not actually slay Isaac, because God stopped him (Gen 22:11–12). The two tenses together say: in heart he finished the offering; in fact he was stopped in the middle of it.

The second clause also sharpens the paradox. The one offering Isaac was “he who had received the promises” (ὁ τὰς ἐπαγγελίας ἀναδεξάμενος ho tas epangelias anadexamenos). The verb ἀναδέχομαι anadechomai means “to accept, welcome, take upon oneself”; Abraham had gladly received the promises. And all those promises were bound up with this one boy. To kill Isaac was, humanly speaking, to kill the promise.

Word Study: μονογενής monogenēs

Isaac is called ton monogenē, “the only son.” The word is formed from monos, “only, alone,” and genos, “kind, family, class,” and so means “only one of its kind,” “unique,” “only.” It is used of an only child (Luke 7:12; 8:42; 9:38) and, in John’s writings, of Jesus as God’s “one and only” Son (John 1:14, 18; 3:16; 1 John 4:9). Isaac was not literally Abraham’s only son; Ishmael was older (Gen 16), and other sons came later (Gen 25:1–2). But he was the only son of the promise, the unique son through whom God had said the line would come. Genesis expresses this with the Hebrew יָחִיד yachid, “only one” (Gen 22:2, 12, 16). The Greek Septuagint translated yachid in this chapter as “beloved,” but Hebrews uses monogenēs, which reflects the Hebrew sense more closely. Josephus too calls Isaac Abraham’s monogenēs (Ant. 1.222).

Christian readers from the earliest centuries have heard in this story an anticipation of the gospel, and the New Testament gives them warrant. God commended Abraham because וְלֹא חָשַׂכְתָּ אֶת־בִּנְךָ אֶת־יְחִידְךָ מִמֶּנִּי velo chasakhta et-binkha et-yechidkha mimmenni, “you have not withheld your son, your only one, from me” (Gen 22:12). Paul seems to echo those words when he writes of God, “He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?” (Rom 8:32). What God stopped Abraham from doing, he himself did. Abraham’s son was spared, and a ram died “instead of his son” (תַּחַת בְּנוֹ tachat beno, Gen 22:13); God’s Son was not spared, and he died instead of us. Hebrews does not develop this comparison explicitly, but its whole argument about the once-for-all offering of the Son (10:10–14) stands in the background.

It is also worth saying plainly what the story does not teach. The peoples around Israel sometimes sacrificed their children to their gods (2 Kgs 3:27), and Israel was strictly forbidden to do so (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31). Through Jeremiah God said of such sacrifices, אֲשֶׁר לֹא צִוִּיתִי וְלֹא עָלְתָה עַל־לִבִּי asher lo tsivviti velo ʿaletah ʿal-libbi, “which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind” (Jer 7:31). The Akedah is not an endorsement of child sacrifice but its opposite: God tested Abraham’s heart, stopped his hand, and provided the substitute himself. What God sought was not Isaac’s death but Abraham’s unreserved trust.

11:18

of whom it had been said, “In Isaac your offspring shall be called,”

This verse explains why the test was so severe. God had tied the entire future of the promise to Isaac. The relative clause πρὸς ὃν ἐλαλήθη pros hon elalēthē can be taken in two ways. The preposition pros often means “to,” and so it could mean “to whom it had been said,” referring to Abraham, to whom God spoke the words. But pros can also mean “with reference to,” as in 1:7 (“of the angels he says”), and so it could mean “of whom it had been said,” referring to Isaac, the “only son” just mentioned. The words were in fact spoken to Abraham about Isaac, so the sense is the same either way: the promise in question concerned this very son.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 21:12

Hebrew: כֹּל אֲשֶׁר תֹּאמַר אֵלֶיךָ שָׂרָה שְׁמַע בְּקֹלָהּ כִּי בְיִצְחָק יִקָּרֵא לְךָ זָרַע — kol asher tomar elekha sarah shemaʿ beqolah, ki veyitschaq yiqqare lekha zaraʿ, “Whatever Sarah says to you, listen to her voice, for in Isaac offspring shall be named for you.”

God spoke these words when Sarah demanded that Hagar and Ishmael be sent away, and Abraham was distressed for his son Ishmael. God assured him that Ishmael too would become a nation (Gen 21:13), but that the line of promise would run through Isaac. The phrase יִקָּרֵא לְךָ זָרַע yiqqare lekha zaraʿ, “offspring shall be called for you,” means that Abraham’s true descendants, the line that would bear his name and inherit his promise, would be reckoned through Isaac. Hebrews quotes the Septuagint, which renders the Hebrew exactly: Ἐν Ἰσαὰκ κληθήσεταί σοι σπέρμα En Isaak klēthēsetai soi sperma. Paul quotes the same words to show that “not all who are descended from Israel are Israel,” but “the children of the promise are counted as offspring” (Rom 9:6–8).

The quotation sets before us the agony at the heart of the test. God had said, “In Isaac your offspring shall be called.” Now God said, “Offer Isaac as a burnt offering.” The command seemed to contradict the promise; God appeared to be at war with his own word. Abraham could not obey the command and still hold the promise unless one of them gave way, or unless God had some way of keeping both that Abraham could not see. Faith is tested most severely not when God seems absent, but when his own words seem to conflict. The hearers knew something of this: they had been promised an inheritance, and they were losing their possessions; they had been promised that God would never leave them (13:5), and they felt abandoned. Abraham’s faith did not resolve the tension by abandoning either the command or the promise. He obeyed the command and kept believing the promise, and the next verse tells how he reasoned his way through.

11:19

for he reckoned that God was able to raise even from the dead; from there, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back.

Here Hebrews opens a window into Abraham’s mind. The participle “he reckoned” (λογισάμενος logisamenos, from λογίζομαι logizomai) means to calculate, to consider carefully, to reach a conclusion by reasoning. It is the word used in the Greek of Genesis 15:6, “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (quoted in Rom 4:3). Faith, in Abraham, was not the abandonment of thought. He reasoned from what he knew of God. God had promised offspring through Isaac. God had commanded that Isaac be offered. God cannot lie (6:18). Therefore, if Isaac died, God must raise him up again.

The content of his reasoning is stated: “that God was able to raise even from the dead” (ὅτι καὶ ἐκ νεκρῶν ἐγείρειν δυνατὸς ὁ θεός hoti kai ek nekrōn egeirein dynatos ho theos). The word “even” (kai) marks the outer limit of what faith dares to believe: God’s power reaches even there. The word “able” (δυνατός dynatos) echoes the “power” (dynamis) Abraham received in verse 11. Abraham had already seen God bring life out of deadness, when a son was born from a body “as good as dead” (11:12). The God who had done that once could do it again. Paul draws the same connection: Abraham believed in “God who gives life to the dead” (Rom 4:17).

Genesis itself gives hints of this hope. As Abraham left his servants at the foot of the mountain, he said to them, וַאֲנִי וְהַנַּעַר נֵלְכָה עַד־כֹּה וְנִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה וְנָשׁוּבָה אֲלֵיכֶם va’ani vehannaʿar nelkhah ʿad-koh venishtachaveh venashuvah alekhem, “I and the boy will go over there, and we will worship, and we will come back to you” (Gen 22:5). All three verbs are first person plural: we will go, we will worship, we will return. And when Isaac asked where the lamb was, Abraham answered, אֱלֹהִים יִרְאֶה־לּוֹ הַשֶּׂה לְעֹלָה בְּנִי elohim yir’eh-lo hasseh leʿolah beni, “God will provide for himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son” (22:8). These words may be read as a father’s attempt to spare his son, but Hebrews reads them as the language of faith: Abraham expected to come back down the mountain with Isaac, whether by God’s provision of a substitute or by God’s raising of the dead.

The final clause is compressed and has been understood in more than one way: ὅθεν αὐτὸν καὶ ἐν παραβολῇ ἐκομίσατο hothen auton kai en parabolē ekomisato. The first word, ὅθεν hothen, can mean “from where” (a place) or “for which reason, hence.” Elsewhere in Hebrews it means “therefore” (2:17; 3:1; 7:25; 8:3; 9:18), but here, coming right after “from the dead,” the local sense, “from there,” that is, from the dead, is more natural. The verb ἐκομίσατο ekomisato, from κομίζω komizō, means “to receive back, recover, get what is one’s own.” It is the verb Hebrews uses for receiving the promise (10:36; 11:39). Abraham, who would not receive all the promises in his lifetime (11:13), did receive Isaac back.

Interpretive Options: “Figuratively speaking” (ἐν παραβολῇ en parabolē)

The noun παραβολή parabolē means a “comparison” or “figure,” something placed alongside something else to illustrate it. It is the word for Jesus’ parables. Hebrews used it in 9:9, where the first tabernacle is “a figure (parabolē) for the present time.”

  • “Figuratively speaking.” Abraham received Isaac back from the dead in a figurative sense, not literally. Isaac did not actually die, but since Abraham had fully given him up, and had in intention already offered him (note the perfect tense in v. 17), Isaac’s return was, in effect, a return from death. This is the most straightforward reading and is followed by most modern translations.
  • “As a type or foreshadowing.” The receiving back of Isaac was a “parable” in the sense of 9:9, an event that pointed forward to a greater reality: the resurrection. Many early Christian writers saw in Isaac carrying the wood up the mountain, in the ram offered in his place, and in the “third day” on which Abraham saw the place (Gen 22:4) a foreshadowing of Christ’s death and resurrection. Others see in it a pledge of the resurrection of all believers.
  • “Unexpectedly” or “in a hazardous venture.” A few older interpreters connected the noun with a related verb meaning “to risk, to expose oneself to danger,” and translated, “he received him back against all expectation.” This meaning is poorly supported for the noun and has few followers today.

The first two views are not really alternatives. The plain sense is that Isaac’s restoration was “figuratively” a resurrection. But because Hebrews has already used parabolē for an Old Testament reality that points forward to the new covenant (9:9), it is fair to say that the figure is also a foreshadowing. Abraham received Isaac back in a way that was a picture of resurrection, and that picture finds its reality in the God who “brought up from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep” (13:20), and who will raise all who belong to him (6:2; 11:35). Christian interpretation should be restrained here, since Hebrews does not spell out a detailed Isaac–Christ typology; but it is on solid ground in seeing the Akedah as a promise of resurrection kept in figure before it was kept in fact.

The pastoral weight of the verse is considerable. The hearers might have to give up their possessions, their reputation, even their lives. Abraham teaches them how faith reasons in such moments: it does not ask, “How can God keep his promise if I lose this?” but “God has promised, and God is able, even to raise the dead.” No sacrifice made in faith is finally lost, because the God of resurrection gives back what is given to him, and more.

11:20

By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau, even concerning things to come.

The last three examples of the patriarchal section (11:20–22) share a common setting: each man is near death, and each speaks about a future he will not live to see. Isaac blesses his sons; Jacob blesses his grandsons; Joseph gives instructions about his bones. In each case faith looks beyond the grave to what God will do for the next generations. These deathbed scenes illustrate what verse 13 said: “these all died in faith.”

The story of Isaac’s blessing (Gen 27) is not an edifying one on the surface. Isaac, old and blind, intended to give the blessing of the firstborn to Esau, his favorite (Gen 25:28). Rebekah and Jacob deceived him, and Jacob received the blessing in his brother’s place. When Esau came in and the deception was discovered, Isaac “trembled with a very great trembling,” and then said of Jacob, וָאֲבָרֲכֵהוּ גַּם־בָּרוּךְ יִהְיֶה va’avarakhehu gam-barukh yihyeh, “and I blessed him; yes, and he shall be blessed” (Gen 27:33). Where, in all this, is faith?

The answer lies in what the blessing was. In Israel, a father’s final blessing was not merely a kind wish. It was a solemn act that passed on the family’s inheritance and, in the patriarchal line, the promise God had given to Abraham. Isaac spoke words about a future far beyond his own lifetime, and he spoke them believing that God would make them come true. His blessing of Jacob promised the dew of heaven and the fatness of the earth, the service of peoples, and lordship over his brothers (Gen 27:28–29). His word to Esau foretold that Esau would live by the sword and serve his brother, but would one day break his yoke from his neck (27:39–40), a prophecy fulfilled centuries later when Edom revolted from Judah (2 Kgs 8:20–22). These were “things to come” (περὶ μελλόντων peri mellontōn), and Isaac pronounced them as one who trusted that God governs the future.

Isaac’s faith is seen especially in what he did after the deception was uncovered. He did not try to revoke the blessing. He recognized that God’s purpose had overruled his own preference, and he submitted to it: “he shall be blessed.” God had told Rebekah before the twins were born, וְרַב יַעֲבֹד צָעִיר verav yaʿavod tsaʿir, “and the elder shall serve the younger” (Gen 25:23), and Isaac at last bowed to that word. Later, when Jacob left for Haran, Isaac blessed him again, this time knowingly and deliberately, and gave him “the blessing of Abraham, to you and to your offspring with you, that you may possess the land of your sojournings” (Gen 28:4). Faith is not always flawless in the moment; Isaac’s was mixed with partiality and weakness. But faith yields to God’s word when it is made known, and that is what Isaac did.

The phrase “things to come” is characteristic of Hebrews. It speaks of “the world to come” (2:5), “the powers of the age to come” (6:5), “the good things to come” (9:11; 10:1), and “the city that is to come” (13:14). Isaac’s blessing concerned the near future of his sons’ descendants, but in the perspective of Hebrews it belonged to the long line of promises that would find their goal in the age to come. The word “even” (καί kai) emphasizes that Isaac’s faith reached that far: he blessed his sons even about things that lay beyond his death.

The order of the names is also significant: “Jacob and Esau,” the younger before the elder. That was the order of God’s choice. Paul draws on the same story to show that God’s purpose of election stands “not because of works but because of him who calls” (Rom 9:10–13; compare Mal 1:2–3). Hebrews will mention Esau once more, as a warning: he sold his birthright for a single meal, and afterward, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected (12:16–17). The two brothers stand as a permanent contrast between one who valued the promise and one who despised it.

11:21

By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, and “worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff.”

The second deathbed scene belongs to Jacob. “When dying” (ἀποθνῄσκων apothnēskōn) is a present participle: “while he was dying,” in the very process of death. Hebrews selects two acts from Jacob’s last days in Egypt, both recorded in Genesis 47–48, and in its sentence they appear in reverse order from Genesis: first the blessing of Joseph’s sons (Gen 48), then the act of worship (Gen 47:31). Both belong to the same period at the end of his life.

First, “he blessed each of the sons of Joseph.” Joseph brought his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim, to their dying grandfather. Jacob formally adopted them as his own: אֶפְרַיִם וּמְנַשֶּׁה כִּרְאוּבֵן וְשִׁמְעוֹן יִהְיוּ־לִי efrayim umenashsheh kir’uven veshimʿon yihyu-li, “Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, as Reuben and Simeon are” (Gen 48:5). Then, to Joseph’s dismay, he crossed his hands, placing his right hand, the hand of the greater blessing, on the head of Ephraim, the younger (שִׂכֵּל אֶת־יָדָיו sikkel et-yadav, “he crossed his hands,” 48:14). When Joseph tried to correct him, Jacob refused: “I know, my son, I know … but his younger brother shall be greater than he” (48:19). The almost-blind old man saw more clearly than his son. As his father Isaac had blessed the younger before the elder, so Jacob, knowingly this time, followed God’s pattern of choosing the younger. He blessed “each” (ἕκαστον hekaston) of the boys, giving each a distinct future. In blessing them, he invoked “the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has shepherded me all my life long to this day” (48:15). His blessing was an act of faith in the God who would bring his descendants out of Egypt and into the land: two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh, would one day receive their portions there.

Second, Jacob “worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff” (προσεκύνησεν ἐπὶ τὸ ἄκρον τῆς ῥάβδου αὐτοῦ prosekynēsen epi to akron tēs rhabdou autou). These words are taken word for word from the Septuagint of Genesis 47:31. That verse comes at the end of a scene in which Jacob made Joseph swear to bury him not in Egypt but with his fathers in Canaan (47:29–31). Having received that oath, which was itself an act of faith in God’s promise of the land, Jacob bowed in worship. But the Hebrew text of Genesis reads differently from the Greek.

Old Testament Text: Genesis 47:31 — “Bed” or “Staff”?

Hebrew: וַיֹּאמֶר הִשָּׁבְעָה לִי וַיִּשָּׁבַע לוֹ וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל עַל־רֹאשׁ הַמִּטָּה — vayyomer hishshavʿah li, vayyishshavaʿ lo, vayyishtachu yisra’el ʿal-rosh hammittah, “And he said, ‘Swear to me,’ and he swore to him. And Israel bowed himself upon the head of the bed.”

The Greek Septuagint, which Hebrews quotes, reads instead: “And Israel worshiped upon the top of his staff.” How did the difference arise? The answer lies in the way Hebrew was written. Ancient Hebrew was written with consonants only. The vowels were supplied by the reader from memory and tradition; the system of vowel marks found in the Hebrew text printed above was added by Jewish scholars called the Masoretes many centuries later, roughly between the seventh and tenth centuries AD, to preserve the traditional pronunciation. The key word here is written with three consonants: מטה (m-t-h). Read with one set of vowels, it is מִטָּה mittah, “bed, couch.” Read with another, it is מַטֶּה matteh, “staff, rod” (also “tribe”). Both words come from the same root meaning “to stretch out” or “to bend.” The Masoretic tradition read “bed”; the Greek translators, working in the third century BC from an unpointed text, read “staff.” The letters on the page are identical; only the vowels differ.

Which reading was original? The Hebrew tradition has good support in the context: in the next chapter Jacob “sat up on the bed” (וַיֵּשֶׁב עַל־הַמִּטָּה vayyeshev ʿal-hammittah, Gen 48:2), and when he died he “drew up his feet into the bed” (הַמִּטָּה hammittah, 49:33). Most scholars therefore think “bed” is the original reading. The phrase would then mean that the dying Jacob, unable to rise, bowed himself in worship toward the head of his bed, or bowed down upon it. On the other hand, the Greek reading is not nonsensical: an aged man rising to worship might well steady himself on his staff.

The important point is that the difference does not change the meaning that Hebrews draws from the verse. In either reading, the old patriarch, at the end of his life, having secured the promise that he would be buried in the land God had promised, bowed in worship. Hebrews quotes the form of the text its hearers knew and does not build any argument on the word “staff.” The substance, a dying man worshiping God in faith, is the same in both. The staff does, however, make a fitting image for the theme of the chapter. Jacob had once said, “with only my staff I crossed this Jordan” (Gen 32:11 Hebrew; 32:10 English, though there a different Hebrew word, מַקֵּל maqqel, is used). The traveler’s staff belongs to the pilgrim who has no permanent home. Jacob died in Egypt as he had lived in Canaan, a sojourner leaning on the God of his fathers.

One later misuse of this verse deserves a brief note. The Latin Vulgate rendered the phrase in a way that could be read as “he worshiped the top of his staff,” and this was sometimes cited in later centuries in defense of venerating sacred objects. Protestant interpreters rightly pointed out that the Greek has the preposition ἐπί epi, “upon, leaning on”: Jacob did not worship his staff; he worshiped God while leaning on it. Scripture consistently forbids bowing to any created object (Exod 20:4–5).

The verb “worshiped” (προσεκύνησεν prosekynēsen, from προσκυνέω proskyneō) means to bow down, to prostrate oneself in reverence; in Hebrews it is used of the angels worshiping the Son (1:6). The Hebrew verb וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ vayyishtachu means the same. Jacob’s last days were spent in worship and blessing. His life had been full of struggle, deception, and sorrow; he told Pharaoh that his days had been “few and evil” (Gen 47:9). But he died believing, worshiping, and blessing, his eyes fixed on a land he would enter only as a corpse carried home for burial (Gen 50:12–13). That too was faith.

11:22

By faith Joseph, at his end, made mention of the exodus of the sons of Israel and gave instructions concerning his bones.

The patriarchal section closes with Joseph. Of all the patriarchs, he had the most reason to feel at home in this world. Sold into slavery, he had risen to become second only to Pharaoh in the greatest empire of his day; he had an Egyptian name, an Egyptian wife, and Egyptian honors (Gen 41:41–45). His life is full of episodes that might have been chosen to illustrate faith: his resistance to Potiphar’s wife, his patience in prison, his forgiveness of his brothers. Hebrews passes over all of them and chooses the moment of his death, because there his faith looked furthest forward.

“At his end” translates τελευτῶν teleutōn, a present participle of τελευτάω teleutaō, “to come to an end, to die.” As with Jacob, the moment is the process of dying. Joseph “made mention of the exodus” (περὶ τῆς ἐξόδου … ἐμνημόνευσεν peri tēs exodou … emnēmoneusen). The verb μνημονεύω mnēmoneuō means “remember, call to mind, make mention of.” It is the same verb used in 11:15 of the land the patriarchs did not “remember.” They did not dwell on the land behind them; Joseph dwelt on the deliverance ahead. And the noun ἔξοδος exodos, “a going out, departure,” is the word that gave the second book of the Bible its Greek name. Luke uses it of the “departure” that Jesus would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31), and Peter of his own approaching death (2 Pet 1:15).

Old Testament Text: Genesis 50:24–25

Hebrew (50:24): אָנֹכִי מֵת וֵאלֹהִים פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד אֶתְכֶם וְהֶעֱלָה אֶתְכֶם מִן־הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת אֶל־הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּע לְאַבְרָהָם לְיִצְחָק וּלְיַעֲקֹב — anokhi met, velohim paqod yifqod etkhem vehe’elah etkhem min-ha’arets hazzot el-ha’arets asher nishbaʿ le’avraham leyitschaq ulyaʿaqov, “I am about to die, but God will surely visit you and bring you up from this land to the land that he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.”

Hebrew (50:25): וַיַּשְׁבַּע יוֹסֵף אֶת־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל לֵאמֹר פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד אֱלֹהִים אֶתְכֶם וְהַעֲלִתֶם אֶת־עַצְמֹתַי מִזֶּה — vayyashbaʿ yosef et-benei yisra’el lemor, paqod yifqod elohim etkhem, vehaʿalitem et-ʿatsmotai mizzeh, “And Joseph made the sons of Israel swear, saying, ‘God will surely visit you, and you shall bring up my bones from here.’”

The doubled verb פָּקֹד יִפְקֹד paqod yifqod, “visiting he will visit,” expresses certainty: “God will surely visit you.” The verb פָּקַד paqad means to attend to, to take note of with intent to act; when God “visits” his people, he intervenes to help them. Joseph’s words were remembered. Centuries later, God sent Moses to tell the elders of Israel, פָּקֹד פָּקַדְתִּי אֶתְכֶם paqod paqadti etkhem, “I have surely visited you” (Exod 3:16), using exactly the same doubled expression. Joseph’s dying hope became the watchword of the exodus.

Joseph’s faith rested on what God had told Abraham long before: that his offspring would be sojourners in a land not their own and be afflicted for four hundred years, but that afterward “they shall come out with great possessions” (Gen 15:13–14). Joseph, at the height of Egyptian prosperity, when Israel was honored and well fed in the land of Goshen, believed that Egypt was not their home, that a day of oppression and deliverance would come, and that God would bring them out. No visible circumstance supported that belief. It rested on God’s word alone. This is “the conviction of things not seen” (11:1).

He also “gave instructions concerning his bones” (περὶ τῶν ὀστέων αὐτοῦ ἐνετείλατο peri tōn osteōn autou eneteilato). The verb ἐντέλλομαι entellomai means “to command, give orders.” Joseph did not ask to be carried to Canaan immediately, as Jacob had been. He asked to be embalmed and to wait in Egypt until the exodus, and then to go with his people. Genesis ends with the words, “and they embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt” (Gen 50:26). The Hebrew word for his coffin, אָרוֹן aron, is the same word later used for the ark of the covenant. For generations the unburied coffin of Joseph lay in Egypt as a silent sermon: we do not belong here; God will visit us.

The promise was kept. When Israel left Egypt, וַיִּקַּח מֹשֶׁה אֶת־עַצְמוֹת יוֹסֵף עִמּוֹ vayyiqqach mosheh et-ʿatsmot yosef ʿimmo, “Moses took the bones of Joseph with him,” because Joseph had made the sons of Israel solemnly swear (Exod 13:19). Through all the years in the wilderness Joseph’s bones traveled with the people. And when the land was at last possessed, Joshua records: וְאֶת־עַצְמוֹת יוֹסֵף אֲשֶׁר־הֶעֱלוּ בְנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל מִמִּצְרַיִם קָבְרוּ בִשְׁכֶם ve’et-ʿatsmot yosef asher-heʿelu venei-yisra’el mimmitsrayim qaveru vishkhem, “And the bones of Joseph, which the sons of Israel brought up from Egypt, they buried at Shechem” (Josh 24:32), in the plot of ground Jacob had bought there (Gen 33:19). The faith of a dying man was vindicated four centuries later.

With Joseph’s mention of the exodus, the chapter is ready to turn to Moses and the exodus itself (11:23–31). The transition is deliberate. Joseph, the son of Israel who became great in Egypt, refused to let Egypt be his final resting place. Moses, the Hebrew child raised in Pharaoh’s court, will refuse to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter (11:24). Both chose identification with God’s people and God’s promise over the honors of the world’s greatest empire.

The lesson for the hearers is clear. Faith does not always see the fulfillment of what it believes. Joseph died more than four hundred years before the exodus. Yet his faith shaped what he did with the end of his life and what he asked of those who came after him, and it was not disappointed. The hearers were also called to live, and if necessary to die, with their eyes on a deliverance not yet seen: “in a very little while, the coming one will come and will not delay” (10:37).
