10:21

and having a great priest over the house of God,

The participle “having” from 10:19 carries over to this second ground of the exhortation. Believers have not only an open way (10:19–20) but a living person at the end of it: “a great priest” (ἱερέα μέγαν hierea megan). The phrase is a literal rendering of the Hebrew title for the high priest, הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל hakkohen haggadol, “the great priest” (Lev 21:10; Num 35:25; Zech 3:1). Hebrews has used the more common Greek title “high priest” (archiereus) throughout, and even “great high priest” (4:14). Here it chooses the older biblical idiom, perhaps to evoke the Old Testament office in its fullest dignity — and then to show that Jesus fills it as no son of Aaron ever could.

An open road is of little use if there is no one to receive the traveler at its end, or if the one waiting is hostile. The author’s point is that the way leads to a Priest who is both able and willing to help. He has been tested in every respect as we are, yet without sin, and so is able to sympathize with our weaknesses (4:15); he is “merciful and faithful” (2:17); he “always lives to intercede” for those who come to God through him (7:25). The way is open and the Priest is there.

He is priest “over the house of God” (ἐπὶ τὸν οἶκον τοῦ θεοῦ epi ton oikon tou theou). The phrase sends the reader back to 3:1–6, where Moses was faithful “in all God’s house” as a servant (quoting Num 12:7), while Christ is faithful “as a Son over his house” — with exactly the same construction, ἐπὶ τὸν οἶκον epi ton oikon (3:6). And 3:6 immediately defines the house: “whose house we are, if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope.” The “house of God,” then, is not a building but a people, the household of God gathered around his Son (compare 1 Tim 3:15; 1 Pet 2:5; 4:17). The connection with 3:6 is telling, because 3:6 joins the same three ideas that 10:19–23 now joins: the house, boldness, and holding fast our hope. A priest over a household is responsible for its worship and its welfare. The congregation the author addresses is not a crowd of isolated individuals approaching God one by one, but a family under one Priest — which is why the exhortations of 10:22–25 move so naturally from approaching God to caring for one another.

The two grounds of 10:19–21, taken together, answer the two great questions every worshiper must ask. May I come? Yes — the blood of Jesus has given boldness for entrance and opened a new and living way. Will I be received? Yes — a great Priest presides over God’s house and intercedes for his people. Faith needs both a sure warrant for coming and a living person to come to; here the author supplies both.

10:22

let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our body washed with pure water.

The first exhortation is προσερχώμεθα proserchōmetha, “let us draw near” — a present subjunctive, inviting a continuing, habitual approach rather than a single visit. The verb is one of Hebrews’ favorite words (4:16; 7:25; 10:1; 11:6; 12:18, 22). In the Greek Old Testament it frequently describes the priests approaching the altar or the presence of God in worship (e.g., Lev 9:7–8; 21:17), corresponding to the Hebrew qarav, “come near.” Under the old covenant, “drawing near” was the special and dangerous privilege of the priesthood; the Levitical sacrifices could never “make perfect those who draw near” (10:1). Now the whole congregation is invited to come as priests. What the law could not do, Christ has done: he has “perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14), so that they can approach with a cleansed conscience.

The author describes the manner of approach in four phrases — two describing inner disposition and two describing what has been done to the worshiper.

“With a true heart” (μετὰ ἀληθινῆς καρδίας meta alēthinēs kardias). The adjective alēthinos means “genuine, real, sincere.” Hebrews has used it of the “true tabernacle” set up by the Lord (8:2) and the “true” heavenly sanctuary of which the earthly was only a copy (9:24). The real sanctuary calls for a real heart: undivided, sincere, without pretense. The phrase stands in deliberate contrast to the “evil heart of unbelief” against which the readers were warned in 3:12, and recalls the new-covenant promise that God would write his laws on their hearts (8:10; 10:16; Jer 31:33). The Old Testament often speaks of serving God with a whole or undivided heart; Hezekiah, for example, could pray, “I have walked before you in truth and with a whole heart” (Isa 38:3; Hebrew בֶּאֱמֶת וּבְלֵב שָׁלֵם beʾemet uvelev shalem). A “true heart” is not a sinless heart — the next phrases assume cleansing — but an honest one that comes to God really meaning it.

“In full assurance of faith” (ἐν πληροφορίᾳ πίστεως en plērophoria pisteōs). The noun plērophoria means “fullness,” “full conviction,” “complete certainty.” In 6:11 the author longed for each reader to show diligence “for the full assurance of hope until the end.” The assurance here is not self-confidence; it is the settled conviction that rests on what God has said and what Christ has done. Faith in Hebrews is always directed toward the reliability of God’s word and the reality of unseen things (11:1, 6). Because the sacrifice is complete, the faith that approaches can be full.

Then come two perfect participles, both in the middle-passive voice, describing what has happened to the worshipers: “our hearts sprinkled” (ῥεραντισμένοι τὰς καρδίας rherantismenoi tas kardias) and “our body washed” (λελουσμένοι τὸ σῶμα lelousmenoi to sōma). The Greek perfect tense describes a completed action with lasting results: they have been sprinkled and washed, and they remain cleansed. The passive sense indicates that this was done to them — they did not cleanse themselves. They draw near not in order to become clean but because they have been made clean.

“Our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience.” Sprinkling with blood has been a major theme of chapter 9. The blood of goats and bulls and the sprinkled ashes of a heifer sanctified “for the purification of the flesh” (9:13); Moses sprinkled the book and all the people with the blood of the covenant (9:19–21; Exod 24:8); and the readers have come to “the blood of sprinkling that speaks better than the blood of Abel” (12:24). The old sprinklings touched only the body; the blood of Christ “will cleanse our conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14). An “evil conscience” is a conscience burdened with guilt — aware of sin and therefore afraid of God. The Levitical sacrifices left worshipers with a continuing “consciousness of sins” (10:2); the blood of Jesus removes it. Notice that the sprinkling here is applied to the hearts. What the Old Testament ritual pictured outwardly, Christ accomplishes inwardly.

Old Testament Background: The Ordination of Priests

The combination of sprinkling and washing points to a specific Old Testament scene: the consecration of Aaron and his sons as priests. God told Moses to bring them to the entrance of the tent of meeting “and wash them with water” (Exod 29:4; Hebrew וְרָחַצְתָּ אֹתָם בַּמָּיִם werachatsta otam bammayim), and later to take blood from the altar and anointing oil “and sprinkle it on Aaron and on his garments, and on his sons … and he shall be holy” (Exod 29:21; Hebrew וְהִזֵּיתָ עַל־אַהֲרֹן wehizzeita ʿal-Aharon, “and you shall sprinkle upon Aaron,” and וְקָדַשׁ הוּא weqadash hu, “and he shall be holy”). Leviticus 8 records Moses carrying this out: “he washed them with water” (Lev 8:6) and sprinkled the blood and oil upon them (Lev 8:30). The high priest also bathed his body in water before entering the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:4). In this light, Hebrews 10:22 says something remarkable: every believer has undergone a priestly ordination. All God’s people are now qualified to do what once only consecrated priests could do — draw near to God in his sanctuary (compare 1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6).

A second Old Testament text lies close by. Through Ezekiel God promised to cleanse his people in the new age: “I will sprinkle pure water upon you, and you will be clean from all your impurities … and I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you” (Ezek 36:25–26). The Greek of Ezekiel speaks of “clean water” with the same adjective Hebrews uses here (καθαρῷ katharō, “pure, clean”). The new-covenant promise of Jeremiah 31, which Hebrews has just quoted (10:16–17), and the promise of cleansing and a new heart in Ezekiel 36 belong together: both are fulfilled in the people who now draw near.

“Our body washed with pure water.” Most interpreters, ancient and modern, see here a reference to Christian baptism. The language closely parallels other New Testament texts about baptism: Paul speaks of Christ cleansing the church “by the bath of water with the word” (Eph 5:26) and of “the bath of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit” (Titus 3:5); Ananias told Paul, “Rise, be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on his name” (Acts 22:16). Hebrews itself lists “teaching about washings” among the elementary matters of the faith (6:2). The pairing of “hearts” and “body” suggests the inward reality and its outward sign: the heart is cleansed by Christ’s blood; the body is washed in baptism, the visible mark of entry into the new-covenant people.

Scripture itself guards against misunderstanding this. Peter, speaking of baptism, explains that it saves “not the putting away of the filth of the flesh but the pledge of a good conscience toward God, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ” (1 Pet 3:21). The water has no power in itself to cleanse the conscience — Hebrews has insisted that only Christ’s blood can do that (9:14). Yet the outward washing is not meaningless: it is the God-given sign and seal of the inward cleansing, received in faith. Some interpreters take “washed with pure water” as entirely metaphorical, a second picture of the same inward cleansing. That is possible, but the explicit mention of “body,” set over against “hearts,” makes a reference to baptism more natural. The balanced conclusion is that the author reminds his hearers of their baptism as the public, bodily testimony that they had been cleansed by Christ and admitted to God’s house — and therefore had every right to draw near.

Pastorally, this verse speaks to the trembling believer. The readers were tempted to hang back, perhaps feeling the old fear of the holy place, perhaps feeling unworthy. The author answers: you are not approaching as an unwashed stranger. Your heart has been sprinkled; your body has been washed; you have been consecrated as a priest. Come in.

10:23

Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful.

The second exhortation, κατέχωμεν katechōmen, “let us hold fast,” uses a verb the author has twice used in conditional warnings: we are God’s house “if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope” (3:6), and we have become partakers of Christ “if indeed we hold fast the beginning of our confidence firm until the end” (3:14). In 4:14 he used a near synonym: “let us hold fast (kratōmen) the confession.” The repetition shows what the author most fears for his hearers: not that they will openly denounce Christ overnight, but that their grip will slowly loosen.

What they are to hold is “the confession of our hope” (τὴν ὁμολογίαν τῆς ἐλπίδος tēn homologian tēs elpidos). The “confession” (3:1; 4:14) is the shared, public declaration of faith that marked the church — confessing Jesus as Son of God, Lord, and high priest — probably first made at baptism (just mentioned in 10:22) and repeated in worship. The confession is called “of hope” because its content looks forward: to the unshakable kingdom (12:28), the city that is to come (13:14), the “better resurrection” (11:35), the return of Christ for salvation (9:28). Hope in Hebrews is not wishful thinking but a settled expectation, “an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast” (6:19). To hold the confession of hope is to keep saying, publicly and together, “Jesus is Lord, and he is coming.”

Word Study: ἀκλινής aklinēs

The adjective ἀκλινῆ aklinē, found only here in the New Testament, combines the negative prefix a- with the root of klinō, “to bend, lean, incline.” It describes something that does not tip or lean to one side — “unwavering,” “steady,” “unbending.” Grammatically it agrees with “confession”: the confession itself is to be held in an unbending, upright position, neither bowed down under pressure nor leaning toward compromise. The readers were being pulled sideways by fear, shame, and weariness; the author urges them to keep their confession upright.

The reason given is not the readers’ strength but God’s character: “for he who promised is faithful” (πιστὸς γὰρ ὁ ἐπαγγειλάμενος pistos gar ho epangeilamenos). The word “faithful” stands first for emphasis. Our holding fast rests on his holding true. The phrase anticipates 11:11, where Sarah received power to conceive “since she considered him faithful who had promised” — the very same participle. It also echoes 6:13–18, where God guaranteed his promise to Abraham with an oath, so that “by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie,” we might have strong encouragement to hold fast the hope set before us.

The faithfulness of God is one of the bedrock confessions of the Old Testament. Moses told Israel, “So know that the Lord your God — he is God, the faithful God, who guards the covenant and the steadfast love toward those who love him and keep his commandments, for a thousand generations” (Deut 7:9; Hebrew הָאֵל הַנֶּאֱמָן haʾel hanneʾeman, “the faithful God”). The Hebrew word comes from the root ʾmn, “to be firm, reliable,” the same root behind “amen” and behind the Hebrew word for faith. Paul repeatedly grounds Christian perseverance in the same truth: “Faithful is God, through whom you were called into fellowship with his Son” (1 Cor 1:9); “Faithful is the one who calls you, and he will also do it” (1 Thess 5:24); “if we are unfaithful, he remains faithful, for he is not able to deny himself” (2 Tim 2:13). The believer’s unwavering confession is the right response to an unwavering God. We do not hold fast in order to make him faithful; we hold fast because he is.

10:24

And let us consider one another so as to stir one another up to love and good works,

The third exhortation turns from God-ward to one another. The verb κατανοῶμεν katanoōmen means “let us consider attentively,” “fix our minds on,” “pay close attention to.” In 3:1 the author used the same verb for the central act of the Christian life: “consider Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession.” Now he applies it to fellow believers: “let us consider one another.” Having fixed their eyes on Jesus, the readers are to turn the same thoughtful attention toward each other — noticing who is weary, who is drifting, who is suffering, who needs encouragement. Christian love begins with paying attention.

The purpose is expressed by a startling word: εἰς παροξυσμὸν ἀγάπης καὶ καλῶν ἔργων eis paroxysmon agapēs kai kalōn ergōn, literally “for a provoking of love and good works.”

Word Study: παροξυσμός paroxysmos

The noun paroxysmos (from which English “paroxysm” comes) means a sharpening, an incitement, a stirring up. Almost everywhere else it has a negative sense. The only other New Testament use describes the quarrel between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark: “a sharp disagreement (paroxysmos) arose, so that they parted from one another” (Acts 15:39). In the Greek Old Testament it describes the burning anger of God (Deut 29:28; Jer 32:37). The related verb appears in 1 Cor 13:5, where love “is not provoked” (is not irritable), and in Acts 17:16, where Paul’s spirit was “provoked” within him at the idols of Athens. Here the author deliberately takes this strong, even violent word and turns it to a good use. Believers are to “provoke” one another — not to irritation but to love; not to quarrels but to good works. The word conveys intensity: this is not mild suggestion but vigorous, deliberate stirring.

“Love and good works” belong together, because in Scripture love is not merely a feeling but active, practical care. The author has already commended this congregation for exactly this: “God is not unjust so as to forget your work and the love that you showed for his name in serving the saints, and still serving” (6:10). He will soon remind them of how their love expressed itself in the former days — sharing the sufferings of prisoners and standing with those who were abused (10:33–34). In chapter 13 he will spell out what love and good works look like: brotherly love, hospitality to strangers, remembering prisoners, faithfulness in marriage, contentment, sharing (13:1–5, 16).

With this verse the triad is complete: faith (10:22), hope (10:23), love (10:24). The three graces appear together often in the New Testament (1 Cor 13:13; Col 1:4–5; 1 Thess 1:3; 5:8). Their appearance here confirms that the author is describing the whole of the Christian life: faith that draws near to God, hope that holds fast to his promise, and love that turns toward one another.

It is worth pausing on the reflexive emphasis of this verse. The Christian life in Hebrews is never individualistic. The author warned in 3:12–13 that each must watch “lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief,” and he told them to “exhort one another every day.” He will say again in 12:15, “see to it that no one falls short of the grace of God.” Perseverance is a community project. One of God’s chief means of keeping his people faithful is the watchful, provoking love of their brothers and sisters.

10:25

not forsaking our own assembling together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near.

Two participles now explain how the mutual “provoking” of 10:24 happens — one negative and one positive. Negatively: μὴ ἐγκαταλείποντες mē enkataleipontes, “not forsaking.” The verb is a strong one. It means to leave behind, abandon, desert, leave in the lurch. It is used of God’s promise never to forsake his people — “I will never let you go, nor will I ever forsake you” (13:5, from Deut 31:6) — and of Jesus’ cry from the cross, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Mark 15:34, from Ps 22:1). Paul uses it of Demas, who “deserted” him, “having loved the present age” (2 Tim 4:10). To forsake the assembly is not a small lapse of attendance; it is to abandon the people of God.

Word Study: ἐπισυναγωγή episynagōgē

The noun ἐπισυναγωγήν episynagōgēn means “a gathering together,” “an assembling.” Its only other New Testament use is in 2 Thess 2:1, of the end-time event, “our being gathered together to him” at the coming of the Lord Jesus. The related verb describes the Son of Man’s angels who “will gather together his elect from the four winds” (Matt 24:31; Mark 13:27), and Jesus’ lament over Jerusalem: “How often I wanted to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings” (Matt 23:37). The word is a close relative of synagōgē, “synagogue,” and some interpreters suggest that the author chose the less common form to avoid confusion with the Jewish synagogue, though this cannot be proved. The eschatological associations of the word are suggestive: each time the church assembles now, it anticipates the great final gathering of God’s people around their returning Lord — a fitting thought in a verse that ends with “the Day drawing near.” The reflexive pronoun “of ourselves” (ἑαυτῶν heautōn) marks it as our own gathering, the assembly to which they belong.

“As is the habit of some” (καθὼς ἔθος τισίν kathōs ethos tisin). The word ethos means a custom or settled practice. Staying away had become a pattern for some, not an occasional absence. The author does not name their reasons, but the letter itself suggests several. Some may have feared the social and legal consequences of being identified with the Christians, remembering the abuse of the former days (10:32–34). Some may have been ashamed — the honor-conscious culture of the Roman world made association with a crucified Messiah a source of disgrace (12:2; 13:13). Some may have grown “sluggish” (5:11; 6:12) and lost their early zeal, or been disappointed that the Lord had not yet returned (compare 10:36–37; 2 Pet 3:3–4). Some may have been drifting back to the synagogue, where Judaism enjoyed a recognized standing in the empire. Whatever the reason, the author sees in it the first step on a path that ends in the apostasy of 10:26–31. The “for” at the start of 10:26 makes the link explicit: neglect of the assembly and willful sin are not unrelated.

Positively: “but encouraging one another” (ἀλλὰ παρακαλοῦντες alla parakalountes). The verb parakaleō covers a range of meanings — to exhort, encourage, urge, comfort — and is a key word of the whole letter, which the author calls “a word of exhortation” (paraklēsis, 13:22). He told them in 3:13 to “exhort one another every day, as long as it is called ‘Today,’ that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin.” The gathering is the place where this mutual exhortation happens. One cannot encourage, or be encouraged by, a congregation from which one is absent.

“And all the more as you see the Day drawing near.” The “Day” (τὴν ἡμέραν tēn hēmeran), with the article and no further description, is the great Day of the Lord announced by the prophets (Joel 2:1, 31; Amos 5:18–20; Zeph 1:14; Mal 4:1) and identified in the New Testament with the return of Christ and the final judgment (Rom 2:16; 1 Cor 3:13; 1 Thess 5:2–4; 2 Pet 3:10). Paul similarly wrote, “The night is far gone; the day has drawn near” (Rom 13:12), using the same verb engizō. Some have suggested that the author has in mind the approaching destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, which the readers might “see” coming in the gathering storm in Judea. But the immediate context points to the final Day: Christ will appear “a second time … for salvation” (9:28); there is “a fearful expectation of judgment” for the adversaries (10:27); “the Coming One will come” (10:37). In the New Testament the last days began with Christ’s first coming (1:2), and believers live with the end always near. The readers can “see” the Day approaching in the sense that they know its signs and its nearness from God’s word.

The logic is striking: the nearness of the Day is not a reason for anxious withdrawal but for more fellowship and more encouragement. The approach of the end makes the church’s gathering more urgent, not less. The Day will reveal who has persevered; therefore believers must help one another persevere until it comes.
