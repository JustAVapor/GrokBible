Hebrews 8

The Mediator of a Better Covenant

Hebrews 8 opens with a signpost unlike any other in the sermon. “Now the main point in what is being said is this,” the preacher announces (8:1). The Greek word is κεφάλαιον kephalaion, “the main thing, the chief point,” and with it he tells his hearers that everything said so far has been climbing toward this summit. Chapters 1–7 established who Jesus is: the Son through whom God has spoken his final word (1:1–2), greater than the angels (1:4–2:18), greater than Moses (3:1–6), the great high priest who has passed through the heavens (4:14), and a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, appointed by an oath and holding a priesthood that cannot pass to another (7:1–28). Now the preacher turns from the person of the priest to his work: where he ministers, what he offers, and what covenant he mediates.

Chapter 8 therefore opens the great central section of Hebrews, 8:1–10:18. That section is held together by three linked themes, all introduced in this chapter: a better sanctuary (the true tabernacle that the Lord pitched, 8:2, 5), a better sacrifice (this priest too must have “something to offer,” 8:3), and a better covenant (8:6–13). Chapter 9 will expound the sanctuary and the blood, chapter 10 the once-for-all offering, and the whole section is framed by Jeremiah’s promise of a new covenant, quoted at full length in 8:8–12 and quoted again in shortened form at its close (10:16–17). Jeremiah 31:31–34 is the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament, and the preacher gives it to us whole because the entire argument of the next two chapters hangs from it.

The chapter falls into two movements. In 8:1–6 the preacher sets the heavenly ministry of Christ beside the earthly ministry of the Levitical priests. Jesus is seated at God’s right hand (Ps 110:1) and serves in “the true tabernacle,” of which the tent Moses built was only a “copy and shadow” (8:5), made according to the pattern God showed him on Mount Sinai (Exod 25:40). In 8:7–13 he turns to the covenant itself. The very fact that God, through Jeremiah, promised a new covenant shows that the first covenant was not the last word, and that God himself had already marked it as old, aging, and “near to vanishing” (8:13).

Several key Greek words carry the argument and recur through 8:1–10:18:

  • λειτουργός leitourgos, “minister,” and λειτουργία leitourgia, “ministry” (8:2, 6) — priestly service offered on behalf of the people.
  • σκηνή skēnē, “tabernacle” (8:2, 5) — the tent-sanctuary of the wilderness, and its heavenly reality.
  • ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma, “copy,” σκιά skia, “shadow,” and τύπος typos, “pattern” (8:5) — the language of correspondence between earthly and heavenly, present and coming.
  • διαθήκη diathēkē, “covenant” (five times in this chapter, along with the related verb “I will covenant” in 8:10), and μεσίτης mesitēs, “mediator” (8:6).
  • κρείττων kreittōn, “better” — a better covenant enacted on better promises (8:6).
  • καινός kainos, “new,” set against πρῶτος prōtos, “first,” and παλαιόω palaioō, “make old” (8:7–8, 13).

As you read, watch for three things. First, notice how the preacher argues from what God later said: just as Psalm 95 showed that Joshua had not given the final rest (4:8), and Psalm 110 showed that the Levitical priesthood could not bring perfection (7:11), so Jeremiah 31 shows that the Sinai covenant was not meant to be final (8:7). Second, notice that the “fault” Jeremiah exposes lies first with the people who broke the covenant (8:8–9), and that the remedy is God’s own work in the heart and his own definitive forgiveness (8:10–12). Third, ask carefully what is “new” and what remains. The preacher will not let his hearers treat the old covenant’s priesthood, sacrifices, and sanctuary as permanent; yet the God who speaks, his holy will, his promises to Abraham, and his purpose to have a people who are his own are not discarded but brought to their goal. For a congregation tempted to drift back toward the visible securities of the temple and its rites, this chapter declares that the reality has come, and that the shadows are already fading.
