9:15

And for this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that, since a death has taken place for redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who have been called may receive the promised eternal inheritance.

The preacher now ties Christ’s sacrifice to the theme of chapter 8, the new covenant. “For this reason” (διὰ τοῦτο dia touto) points both backward and forward: because his blood cleanses the conscience (9:14), Christ is the mediator of the new covenant, whose central promise is precisely the forgiveness of sins and a heart made new (Jer 31:33–34, quoted in 8:10–12). And the sentence goes on to state the purpose of his mediation (“so that…”).

“He is the mediator of a new covenant” (διαθήκης καινῆς μεσίτης ἐστίν diathēkēs kainēs mesitēs estin). A μεσίτης mesitēs, “mediator,” is a go-between, one who stands in the middle between two parties to establish or guarantee an agreement. Moses was the mediator of the Sinai covenant (Gal 3:19; compare Deut 5:5, “I was standing between the Lord and you”). Hebrews calls Jesus “the mediator of a better covenant” (8:6) and “the mediator of a new covenant” (12:24), and earlier “the guarantor (ἔγγυος engyos) of a better covenant” (7:22). Paul says, “there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all” (1 Tim 2:5–6). A mediator who is himself both God’s Son (1:2) and fully human (2:14–17) can truly represent both sides. The present tense “is” matters: he does not merely set up the covenant and step away; he continues as its mediator at God’s right hand.

“Since a death has taken place” (θανάτου γενομένου thanatou genomenou). This is a genitive absolute, a Greek construction that sets out the circumstances of the main action—here, the ground on which the inheritance can be received. The preacher says “a death,” without the article, as though stating a principle that he will explain in the next two verses: covenant blessings come by way of death. Of course, the death meant is Christ’s.

“For redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant” (εἰς ἀπολύτρωσιν τῶν ἐπὶ τῇ πρώτῃ διαθήκῃ παραβάσεων eis apolytrōsin tōn epi tē prōtē diathēkē parabaseōn). ἀπολύτρωσις apolytrōsis is a strengthened form of the “redemption” of 9:12: release by payment of a ransom. παράβασις parabasis, “transgression,” means stepping over a line, breaking a known command (2:2, “every transgression and disobedience received a just penalty”). The phrase “committed under the first covenant” makes a striking claim: Christ’s death deals with the sins of those who lived under the Sinai covenant. Their transgressions were real, and the animal sacrifices could not truly remove them (10:4). Those sins were covered by God’s promise and forbearance, but their actual redemption awaited Christ’s death.

This means that Christ’s sacrifice reaches backward as well as forward. Paul says something similar: God set forth Christ as a propitiation “to demonstrate his righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over sins previously committed” (Rom 3:25). The faithful of the Old Testament—Abel, Abraham, Moses, David, and all the heroes of Hebrews 11—were saved by the same Redeemer as Christians, through faith in God’s promise. As Hebrews 11:39–40 puts it, they did not receive what was promised, “since God had provided something better for us, so that apart from us they should not be made perfect.” There is one people of God, one Mediator, one sacrifice, across both covenants. The phrase may also have special point for Jewish Christian hearers: the very transgressions committed under the covenant they were tempted to return to have been redeemed by Christ, not by that covenant’s rituals.

“Those who have been called” (οἱ κεκλημένοι hoi keklēmenoi). The perfect passive participle describes people who have been called by God and remain in that state. The readers are “partakers of a heavenly calling” (3:1). Calling is God’s initiative; the inheritance is a gift.

“May receive the promised eternal inheritance”, literally “the promise of the eternal inheritance” (τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν … τῆς αἰωνίου κληρονομίας tēn epangelian … tēs aiōniou klēronomias). The genitive identifies the content of the promise: what was promised is the inheritance. “Promise” is a major theme of Hebrews: God’s promise to Abraham confirmed by an oath (6:12–18), the promise of entering his rest (4:1), the promises the patriarchs greeted from afar (11:13). κληρονομία klēronomia, “inheritance,” recalls Israel’s inheritance of the land of Canaan (Hebrew נַחֲלָה nachalah), but in Hebrews the inheritance is “eternal”: the heavenly homeland, the city with foundations, the unshakable kingdom (11:10, 16; 12:28; 13:14). Christ is the appointed “heir of all things” (1:2), and believers are those who will “inherit salvation” (1:14) as joint heirs with him.

The mention of “inheritance” prepares directly for the next verses. An inheritance is received when someone dies. The preacher will now use that ordinary fact of life to illuminate why Christ had to die.

9:16

For where there is a covenant (or “will”), the death of the one who made it must be established.

Verses 16–17 are among the most debated in Hebrews. The difficulty lies in the Greek word διαθήκη diathēkē, which this study ordinarily translates “covenant.”

Word Study: διαθήκη diathēkē — “covenant” and “will”

In ordinary Greek of the period, διαθήκη diathēkē most commonly meant a “will” or “testament,” the legal disposition of one’s property to take effect at death. The related verb διατίθεμαι diatithemai means “to arrange, dispose,” and in legal contexts “to make a will”; the participle ὁ διαθέμενος ho diathemenos, used in 9:16 and 9:17, was the standard term for “the testator.”

But the translators of the Greek Old Testament chose diathēkē to render the Hebrew בְּרִית berit, “covenant,” the solemn, binding relationship God established with Noah, Abraham, Israel, and David. They may have chosen it (rather than a word for a mutual contract between equals) because a diathēkē was an arrangement set up by one party, which suited God’s sovereign initiative in his covenants. Throughout the Greek Bible, therefore, diathēkē normally means “covenant.” Hebrews uses the word seventeen times, more than any other New Testament book, and in almost all of them (7:22; 8:6–10; 9:4, 15, 18, 20; 10:16, 29; 12:24; 13:20) the meaning “covenant” is clear. The verb is used of God “making” the new covenant in the quotation of Jer 31 (8:10; 10:16)—where God himself is “the one who makes” the covenant.

The question is whether in 9:16–17 the preacher shifts to the meaning “will.” Read that way, the verses make a simple point drawn from everyday legal life: “Where there is a will, the death of the one who made it must be established. For a will takes effect only at death, since it is never in force while the one who made it is alive.” Read as “covenant,” they refer to the practice of ratifying covenants by the death of sacrificial victims. Both readings have weighty support.

Interpretive Options: Covenant or Will in 9:16–17?

  • “Will” or “testament.” Most English translations and many commentators (F. F. Bruce and Harold Attridge among them) hold that the preacher here plays on the common meaning of diathēkē. Arguments: (1) The language fits wills precisely: ho diathemenos was the ordinary term for a testator, and the statement that a diathēkē “is never in force while the one who made it is living” (9:17) describes exactly how a will works. In Greek and Roman practice, a will was a valid document from the time it was drawn up, but it took effect only at the death of the testator, who could revise it while alive. (2) The preceding verse speaks of “inheritance” (9:15), and Hebrews elsewhere speaks of Christ as “heir” (1:2) and of believers as “heirs” (1:14; 6:17); a will is the natural link between death and inheritance. (3) The words “must be established” (φέρεσθαι pheresthai, literally “be brought forward”) may reflect the legal requirement that a testator’s death be formally attested before the will could be executed.
  • “Covenant.” Other interpreters, including B. F. Westcott and, more recently, William Lane (building on a careful study by John J. Hughes), hold that diathēkē means “covenant” here as everywhere else in Hebrews. Arguments: (1) The verses are framed by covenant language—“the first covenant” in 9:15 and “not even the first [covenant] was inaugurated without blood” in 9:18—and 9:18 begins with “therefore” (ὅθεν hothen), drawing a conclusion from 9:16–17 about the Sinai covenant, which was certainly not a will. (2) Biblical covenants were ratified by sacrifice. In Gen 15 God “cut a covenant” with Abram by passing between the halves of slaughtered animals (Gen 15:9–10, 17–18); in Jer 34:18 the Lord condemns those who “cut the calf in two and passed between its parts” and then broke the covenant; Ps 50:5 speaks of those “who made a covenant with me by sacrifice.” The slain animals represented the covenant-maker, who in effect invoked death upon himself if he broke his oath. (3) The phrase “over the dead” in 9:17 (ἐπὶ νεκροῖς epi nekrois) is plural—literally “over dead [bodies]”—which suits sacrificial victims better than a single testator. (4) The verb pheresthai can also mean “to be brought in, presented,” which could refer to the presentation of the sacrificial death.
  • A deliberate play on both senses. Many interpreters conclude that the preacher is intentionally exploiting the double meaning of the word. Having spoken of an “inheritance” (9:15), he turns briefly to the familiar legal sense of diathēkē as an illustration—everyone knows that a will requires a death—and then returns in 9:18 to the biblical sense of covenant, showing that the first covenant too was established by death (blood). The illustration and the biblical reality converge in Christ.

Weighing the views. Each view faces a difficulty. The “will” view must explain why the preacher, after using diathēkē consistently for “covenant,” would switch meanings for two verses and then switch back with a “therefore” (9:18). It must also face the fact that the new covenant is not, in Scripture, a will in the ordinary sense: God, who makes it (8:10), does not die; and Christ, who died, rose again and lives to administer the inheritance himself (7:25). The “covenant” view must explain how the general statements of 9:16–17 apply to covenants: it is not literally true that a covenant “is never in force while the one who made it is living,” and taking “the one who made it” to mean “the animal that represents the covenant-maker” stretches the language considerably. The plain sense of the words, especially in 9:17, sounds very much like a description of a will.

The most satisfying conclusion, held with appropriate humility, is that the preacher is using the double meaning deliberately. His hearers, living in a Greek-speaking world, knew both senses of the word. The preacher wants to show that the new covenant, with its promised inheritance, became effective only through a death. He illustrates this with a principle everyone knows from the law of wills (9:16–17), and then shows from Scripture that the same principle held for the Sinai covenant, which was inaugurated with the blood of sacrificial victims (9:18–22). The illustration works because in Christ’s case both realities are present. He is the covenant sacrifice whose death ratifies the new covenant, as the animals ratified the old. And he is, so to speak, the one whose death releases the inheritance to the heirs—with the glorious difference that he did not remain dead, but lives forever to see that his people receive what he secured for them. No human analogy fits Christ perfectly, and the preacher is not building a doctrine on the details of inheritance law. He is using a vivid comparison to drive home one point: no death, no covenant blessing.

On any of the views, the central teaching is clear and agrees with the rest of Scripture: Christ’s death was necessary (“must,” ἀνάγκη anankē, “necessity”) for the new covenant to take effect. The forgiveness of sins, the law written on the heart, and the eternal inheritance are not given apart from the death of the Mediator. Jesus said the same on the night before he died: “This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you” (Luke 22:20).

9:17

For a covenant (or “will”) is made firm over the dead, since it is never in force while the one who made it is living.

This verse explains (“for”) the principle stated in 9:16. Three expressions call for comment.

“Is made firm” (βεβαία bebaia). βέβαιος bebaios means “firm, reliable, legally valid, guaranteed.” It belonged to the vocabulary of law and commerce, where a bebaiōsis was a legal guarantee (compare 6:16, where an oath is “for confirmation,” eis bebaiōsin). Hebrews uses the adjective for the word spoken through angels that “proved firm” (2:2), for our confidence held “firm” to the end (3:6, 14), and for hope as an anchor “sure and firm” (6:19). Here it means that the arrangement becomes legally binding and effective.

“Over the dead” (ἐπὶ νεκροῖς epi nekrois). The preposition ἐπί epi with the dative can mean “on the basis of” or “upon, over.” If the verse speaks of a will, the phrase means “on the basis of [the testator’s being] dead,” that is, “at death”; the plural would then be generic (“when people are dead”). If it speaks of a covenant, the phrase means “over dead [victims],” recalling the slain animals over which ancient covenants were sworn (Gen 15:9–17). The plural is somewhat more natural on the covenant reading, but generic plurals are common enough that it cannot settle the question.

“Since it is never in force while the one who made it is living” (ἐπεὶ μήποτε ἰσχύει ὅτε ζῇ ὁ διαθέμενος epei mēpote ischyei hote zē ho diathemenos). ἰσχύω ischyō means “to be strong, to have force, to be valid.” μήποτε mēpote here means “never”; some take the clause as a question (“Is it ever in force while the one who made it is living?”), which makes the same point more vividly. As noted above, this describes the operation of a will exactly: while the testator lives, the heirs receive nothing, and the testator can still change his mind; when he dies, the will becomes irrevocable and its benefits pass to the heirs.

The preacher’s point, applied to Christ, is powerful. The new covenant’s promises are not provisional, waiting for some further act. The death has occurred. The arrangement is now “firm,” irrevocable, “in force.” Those who have been called may receive the inheritance with full assurance, because it has been sealed by a death that cannot be undone. Yet—and this is where every human analogy falls short—the one who died now lives, “able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them” (7:25). The heirs are not left to the uncertain administration of strangers; the testator himself, risen from the dead, executes his own will.

The verse also has a sober application. The death of Christ was not an accident, a tragic interruption of his mission, or merely an example of self-sacrifice. It was the necessary means by which the covenant became effective. Without it, there would be no inheritance for anyone. The cross stands at the center of God’s plan, not at its margin.

9:18

Therefore not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood.

“Therefore” (ὅθεν hothen, “from which, hence”). The principle that a death is required (9:16–17) is now shown to have been at work in the first covenant too. The preacher returns firmly to the biblical meaning of diathēkē, “covenant,” and to the scriptural record of how the Sinai covenant was established.

“Not even the first” (οὐδὲ ἡ πρώτη oude hē prōtē). The word “covenant” is again understood, as in 9:1. “Not even” implies an argument from the lesser to the greater in reverse: if even the first covenant, with all its limitations, required blood to be established, how much more the new.

“Was inaugurated” (ἐγκεκαίνισται enkekainistai). The verb ἐγκαινίζω enkainizō means “to renew, to dedicate, to inaugurate,” that is, to put something new into use with a solemn ceremony. In the Greek Old Testament it is used of dedicating a new house (Deut 20:5) and of Solomon and Israel dedicating the temple (1 Kgs 8:63). The related noun ta enkainia, “the Dedication,” is the Greek name for the festival of Hanukkah (John 10:22), which celebrated the rededication of the temple in 164 BC (1 Macc 4:52–59). Hebrews uses the same verb in 10:20: Jesus “inaugurated for us a new and living way.” The perfect tense here describes the inauguration as an established fact of the past with lasting results: the Sinai covenant stands as a covenant inaugurated by blood.

“Without blood” (χωρὶς αἵματος chōris haimatos) echoes 9:7, “not without blood.” Whether the high priest entering the inner room or Moses inaugurating the covenant, the principle holds: access to God and relationship with God under the covenant are established through blood, through life given up in death. This is not a cruel or arbitrary requirement. It reflects the seriousness of sin, whose wage is death (Gen 2:17; Rom 6:23), and the graciousness of God, who provided a substitute so that the sinner might live. The preacher now turns to the account in Exodus 24 to show it.

9:19

For when every commandment according to the law had been spoken by Moses to all the people, he took the blood of the calves, with water and scarlet wool and hyssop, and sprinkled both the book itself and all the people,

The preacher retells the covenant ceremony at Mount Sinai. It will help to read the Hebrew account first.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 24:3–8

Hebrew (24:3): וַיָּבֹא מֹשֶׁה וַיְסַפֵּר לָעָם אֵת כָּל־דִּבְרֵי יְהוָה וְאֵת כָּל־הַמִּשְׁפָּטִים וַיַּעַן כָּל־הָעָם קוֹל אֶחָד וַיֹּאמְרוּ כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה נַעֲשֶׂה — vayyavo mosheh vaysapper la’am et kol-divre YHWH ve’et kol-hammishpatim vayya’an kol-ha’am qol echad vayyomeru kol-haddevarim asher-dibber YHWH na’aseh, “Moses came and recounted to the people all the words of the Lord and all the ordinances, and all the people answered with one voice and said, ‘All the words that the Lord has spoken we will do.’”

The account continues: Moses wrote down all the words of the Lord, built an altar with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes, and sent young men who offered burnt offerings and sacrificed bulls as peace offerings (24:4–5). Then:

Hebrew (24:6–8): וַיִּקַּח מֹשֶׁה חֲצִי הַדָּם וַיָּשֶׂם בָּאַגָּנֹת וַחֲצִי הַדָּם זָרַק עַל־הַמִּזְבֵּחַ׃ וַיִּקַּח סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית וַיִּקְרָא בְּאָזְנֵי הָעָם וַיֹּאמְרוּ כֹּל אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע׃ וַיִּקַּח מֹשֶׁה אֶת־הַדָּם וַיִּזְרֹק עַל־הָעָם — “Moses took half the blood and put it in basins, and half the blood he dashed against the altar. Then he took the book of the covenant (sefer habberit) and read it in the hearing of the people, and they said, ‘All that the Lord has spoken we will do and we will obey.’ Then Moses took the blood and dashed it on the people.”

“When every commandment according to the law had been spoken by Moses to all the people.” This summarizes Exod 24:3 and 24:7: Moses declared “all the words of the Lord and all the ordinances” and read “the book of the covenant.” The genitive absolute (“every commandment having been spoken”) sets the scene. The emphasis on “every” and “all” stresses the completeness of the covenant’s terms and the involvement of the whole nation. The word of God came first, then the blood; covenant is founded on God’s spoken word and sealed by sacrifice.

“He took the blood of the calves.” Exodus mentions “bulls” offered as peace offerings and burnt offerings (24:5). The preacher calls them “calves” (μόσχων moschōn), the Greek Old Testament’s word there.

Textual Note: “Calves and Goats”

Many manuscripts read “the blood of the calves and the goats” (καὶ τῶν τράγων kai tōn tragōn); others lack the phrase. The SBL text, followed here, omits it; the Nestle-Aland edition prints it in brackets, signaling real doubt. Exodus 24 mentions only bulls, so a scribe may have omitted “goats” to conform to Exodus; or, more likely, a scribe added “and the goats” to match the familiar pairing of 9:12–13. The meaning is unaffected.

“With water and scarlet wool and hyssop.” None of these is mentioned in Exodus 24. The preacher has drawn them from the purification rites of the law, where they appear together. In the cleansing of a person healed of a skin disease, the priest took “cedar wood and scarlet yarn and hyssop” and dipped them, with a live bird, in the blood of a slain bird over “living water,” and sprinkled the one to be cleansed seven times (Lev 14:4–7). In the red heifer rite, cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet yarn were burned with the heifer, and the ashes mixed with water were sprinkled with hyssop (Num 19:6, 17–18). At the first Passover the Israelites were told to “take a bunch of hyssop and dip it in the blood” and apply it to the doorposts (Exod 12:22). Hyssop was a small bushy plant whose branches held liquid well; with scarlet wool tied around it, it made a natural sprinkler. David prays, “Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean” (Ps 51:7).

“And sprinkled both the book itself and all the people.” Exodus says Moses dashed half the blood on the altar and half on the people; it does not say he sprinkled the book. Some have suggested that the book lay on the altar and so received blood; others, that the preacher reflects a Jewish tradition about the ceremony. We cannot be sure. What is clear is the preacher’s method. He is not giving a new, independent account of Exodus 24 but a theological summary that gathers together the related blood rites of the law into a single picture: the covenant—its words, its people, and (in 9:21) its sanctuary—was consecrated by blood. Ancient Jewish writers commonly combined related scriptural passages this way, and the preacher’s hearers would recognize each element from their Scriptures.

The verb “sprinkled” (ἐράντισεν erantisen, from ῥαντίζω rhantizō) deserves notice. The Hebrew of Exod 24:6, 8 uses זָרַק zaraq, “to toss, dash” (the word used for dashing blood against the altar). Hebrews uses the word for sprinkling in purification rites (9:13, the ashes of the heifer), which links the covenant ceremony with cleansing. The same word appears in 10:22, where believers have “hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience,” and in 12:24, where we have come to “the sprinkled blood that speaks better than the blood of Abel.” Peter likewise speaks of believers chosen “for obedience and for sprinkling with the blood of Jesus Christ” (1 Pet 1:2), which is an allusion to this very scene: at Sinai the people promised obedience and were sprinkled with blood.

9:20

saying, “This is the blood of the covenant that God commanded for you.”

Moses’ words at the climax of the Sinai ceremony are quoted from Exod 24:8.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 24:8

Hebrew: וַיִּקַּח מֹשֶׁה אֶת־הַדָּם וַיִּזְרֹק עַל־הָעָם וַיֹּאמֶר הִנֵּה דַם־הַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר כָּרַת יְהוָה עִמָּכֶם עַל כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה — vayyiqqach mosheh et-haddam vayyizroq al-ha’am vayyomer hinneh dam-habberit asher karat YHWH immakhem al kol-haddevarim ha’elleh, “Moses took the blood and dashed it on the people and said, ‘Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord has cut with you on the basis of all these words.’”

“Cut a covenant” (כָּרַת בְּרִית karat berit) is the standard Hebrew idiom for making a covenant, probably reflecting the cutting of sacrificial animals in covenant ceremonies (Gen 15:10, 18; Jer 34:18). The Greek Old Testament renders it, “Behold, the blood of the covenant that the Lord covenanted with you.”

The preacher’s wording differs in three small ways from both the Hebrew and the usual Greek, and each difference seems purposeful.

  • “This is” instead of “Behold.” The Hebrew הִנֵּה hinneh, “behold,” becomes τοῦτο touto, “this [is].” Many commentators hear in this an echo of Jesus’ words at the Last Supper: “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Mark 14:24); “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins” (Matt 26:28). Jesus himself drew on Exod 24:8 when he gave the cup, presenting his death as the covenant sacrifice. Whether or not the preacher consciously echoes the Supper wording, his hearers, who knew the Lord’s Supper, could hardly miss the connection.
  • “God” instead of “the Lord.” A minor variation, possibly to avoid confusion with “the Lord” Jesus, or simply stylistic.
  • “Commanded for you” instead of “cut with you” or “covenanted with you.” The verb ἐνετείλατο eneteilato, “commanded,” emphasizes that the Sinai covenant came as God’s authoritative command, connecting with “every commandment” in 9:19. It also suits the preacher’s portrayal of the first covenant as a system of regulations (9:1, 10). The covenant was God’s initiative, not a negotiated agreement.

The phrase “the blood of the covenant” is the heart of the verse. The covenant between God and Israel was sealed in blood. Israel’s pledge, “All that the Lord has spoken we will do,” was made under the sign of death—the blood of victims standing between a holy God and a people who would, as it turned out, soon break their word (Exod 32). The blood consecrated them to God and, by implication, solemnly bound them under the covenant’s sanctions.

The new covenant also comes with blood, but of an entirely different kind. At Sinai, the blood of animals ratified a covenant that depended on Israel’s obedience, which failed. At the Last Supper and the cross, the blood of Christ ratified a covenant in which God himself undertakes to write his law on his people’s hearts and to remember their sins no more (8:10–12). Jesus took the words of Moses and filled them with a meaning Moses could not have given them: “This is my blood of the covenant.”

9:21

And in the same way he sprinkled with the blood both the tabernacle and all the vessels of the ministry.

The preacher extends the picture from the covenant ceremony to the tabernacle and its equipment. “The vessels of the ministry” (τὰ σκεύη τῆς λειτουργίας ta skeuē tēs leitourgias) means all the utensils used in the priestly service: basins, firepans, bowls, lampstand tools, and so on. λειτουργία leitourgia, “public service, ministry,” is the word used of Christ’s “more excellent ministry” in 8:6.

Exodus 24 could not have included the tabernacle, which had not yet been built. The preacher is now summarizing the consecration of the tabernacle at a later time. According to Exodus 40 and Leviticus 8, Moses anointed the tabernacle and everything in it with oil to consecrate it (Exod 40:9–11; Lev 8:10–11), but blood was also used: Moses put blood on the horns of the altar, “and purified the altar, and poured out the blood at the base of the altar, and consecrated it, to make atonement for it” (Lev 8:15), and sprinkled Aaron and his sons and their garments with oil and blood (Lev 8:30). Every year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest sprinkled blood on and before the mercy seat and on the altar to cleanse them “from the uncleannesses of the sons of Israel” (Lev 16:14–19). The historian Josephus, retelling the dedication of the tabernacle, says that Moses purified the tabernacle and its vessels with oil and with the blood of bulls and goats (Ant. 3.206), which shows that the preacher’s summary agrees with how Jews of his time understood these events.

“In the same way” (ὁμοίως homoiōs) links the consecration of the sanctuary with the ratification of the covenant. The people, the book, the tent, and its vessels—everything connected with Israel’s relationship to God—were touched by blood. Even the holy things needed cleansing, because they were used by sinful people and stood among a sinful nation. Lev 16:16 states the reason plainly: atonement was made for the sanctuary “because of the uncleannesses of the sons of Israel and because of their transgressions, for all their sins; and so he shall do for the tent of meeting, which dwells with them in the midst of their uncleannesses.” That principle will become important in 9:23.

9:22

Indeed, according to the law almost everything is purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.

The preacher now states the general principle that underlies the examples just given. The sentence has two parts.

“According to the law almost everything is purified with blood.” The word σχεδόν schedon, “almost,” shows the preacher’s care and his close knowledge of the law. Not everything in the Levitical system was purified with blood. Some things were cleansed by water alone (for example, bodily discharges, Lev 15:5–13), some by fire and water (metal spoils of war, Num 31:22–23), and a poor Israelite who could not afford two birds could bring a tenth of an ephah of fine flour as a sin offering, and “the priest shall make atonement for him … and he shall be forgiven” (Lev 5:11–13). Atonement money (Exod 30:15–16) and gold offered by the army after the war with Midian (Num 31:50) are also described as making atonement. So the preacher says “almost.” But the general rule was clear: purification and atonement came through blood.

“And without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (καὶ χωρὶς αἱματεκχυσίας οὐ γίνεται ἄφεσις kai chōris haimatekchysias ou ginetai aphesis). This is one of the best-known sentences in Hebrews, and it is the theological foundation for everything the chapter has said about blood.

Word Study: αἱματεκχυσία haimatekchysia — “shedding of blood”

The word αἱματεκχυσία haimatekchysia is a compound of αἷμα haima, “blood,” and ἐκχύνω ekchynō, “to pour out.” It is not found in any Greek writing earlier than Hebrews, and the preacher may have coined it. It probably echoes the Greek Old Testament phrase for “pouring out blood” (ἐκχεῖν αἷμα ekchein haima), used both of killing (Gen 9:6) and of the priest pouring out the remaining blood of a sacrifice at the base of the altar (Lev 4:7, 18, 25, 30, 34; Hebrew וְאֵת כָּל־דַּם הַפָּר יִשְׁפֹּךְ אֶל־יְסוֹד מִזְבַּח הָעֹלָה ve’et kol-dam happar yishpokh el-yesod mizbach ha’olah, “and all the blood of the bull he shall pour out at the base of the altar of burnt offering,” Lev 4:7).

Interpreters debate whether the word refers primarily to the slaughter of the victim (its blood shed in death) or to the ritual application of the blood at the altar. In context, the two belong together: the blood had to be shed in death and then applied in the prescribed way. The emphasis falls on the giving up of life. The word also recalls Jesus’ words at the Supper about his blood “poured out (ἐκχυννόμενον ekchynnomenon) for many for the forgiveness (ἄφεσιν aphesin) of sins” (Matt 26:28), where the same two ideas—blood poured out and forgiveness—stand side by side.

Why should forgiveness require the shedding of blood? The preacher does not explain the principle here because it was so deeply rooted in Scripture that his hearers would not need it explained. The key text is Lev 17:11:

Old Testament Text: Leviticus 17:11

Hebrew: כִּי נֶפֶשׁ הַבָּשָׂר בַּדָּם הִוא וַאֲנִי נְתַתִּיו לָכֶם עַל־הַמִּזְבֵּחַ לְכַפֵּר עַל־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם כִּי־הַדָּם הוּא בַּנֶּפֶשׁ יְכַפֵּר — ki nefesh habbasar baddam hi va’ani netattiv lakhem al-hammizbeach lekhapper al-nafshotekhem ki-haddam hu bannefesh yekhapper, “For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I myself have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your lives; for it is the blood that makes atonement by means of the life.”

Three things in this verse illuminate Heb 9:22. (1) Blood represents life—נֶפֶשׁ nefesh, “life, soul”—and shed blood represents life given up in death. (2) The verse speaks of substitution: the life (nefesh) of the animal makes atonement for “your lives” (nafshotekhem). A life is given in place of a life. (3) Most important, God himself says, “I have given it to you.” The sacrificial system was not a human attempt to appease an angry deity; it was God’s own gracious provision for sinners. The God who required the shedding of blood also supplied the means.

The principle reflects the biblical teaching that sin brings death (Gen 2:17; Ezek 18:4; Rom 6:23). Forgiveness is not God simply overlooking sin, as though sin did not matter. It is release (ἄφεσις aphesis, “release, cancellation, forgiveness”) from sin’s guilt and penalty through the death of a substitute. The word aphesis is used in the Greek Old Testament for the “release” of the Jubilee year (Lev 25:10), when debts were canceled and slaves set free; it returns in 10:18, which concludes the whole argument: “where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin.”

But if forgiveness requires blood, and “it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (10:4), then the old system pointed to its own insufficiency. It established the principle without supplying the reality. Only the blood of the sinless Son of God, offered through the eternal Spirit, fully satisfies the principle stated here. The verse is therefore not a mere summary of Levitical practice; it is a statement of a divine principle that finds its true fulfillment at the cross. There is forgiveness, full and free—but there is no forgiveness apart from the shed blood of Christ. That is why the gospel cannot be reduced to moral teaching or good example, and why Christians in every age have loved to sing of the blood of the Lamb (Rev 5:9; 7:14; 12:11).
