How to Use This Study

Reading Hebrews with the Original Languages in View

This book walks through the Epistle to the Hebrews one verse at a time. It is written for the reader who does not read Greek or Hebrew but wants to sit as close to the original text as possible, and who is willing to let the wording of the text—not tradition, not a favorite translation, not a commentator—have the final say. Every effort has been made to show what the words actually say, to explain why that matters, and to let the rest of Scripture shed light on each verse.

The Translation

All quotations of Scripture in this study—Hebrews itself and every cross-reference—are fresh translations made from the original languages for this book. They are not taken from any published English version. The Greek text of Hebrews followed is that of the SBL Greek New Testament, which agrees with the standard critical text (Nestle-Aland, 28th edition) in nearly every place; where the manuscripts differ in a way that affects meaning, a Textual Note explains the evidence. In a few places (for example, 2:9 and 12:3) the study judges the evidence to favor the reading printed in the Nestle-Aland edition rather than the SBL text, and the note explains why. Old Testament passages are translated from the Masoretic Hebrew text (the Westminster Leningrad Codex, the basis of Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia).

The translation aims to be faithful first and readable second. Where the Greek can be rendered more than one way, the commentary explains the options. A few conventions:

  • When the Hebrew Old Testament uses the personal name of God (the four letters YHWH), it is rendered “the Lord” in small capitals, following long custom. When Hebrews quotes such a verse in Greek, the Greek word is κύριος kyrios, “Lord,” and it is printed “Lord.”
  • Pronouns referring to God are not capitalized (“he,” “his”), since neither Greek nor Hebrew marks them.
  • Words of key importance in the argument of Hebrews are translated consistently throughout (for example, ἀρχηγός archēgos is always “pioneer,” and παρρησία parrēsia is always “boldness”), so that the reader can follow the author’s repetitions. Appendix B gathers these key words.

Greek and Hebrew for the English Reader

Greek and Hebrew words are printed in their own scripts so that the reader can see the very word on the page, but each is always followed by a transliteration in italics (the word spelled in English letters) and an English meaning. You never need to read the original script to follow the argument. A Greek word appears like this: ἀπαύγασμα apaugasma, “radiance.” A Hebrew word appears like this: בְּרִית berit, “covenant.”

Transliteration follows a simple form of the Society of Biblical Literature’s general-purpose style. In Greek, ē represents the long vowel eta (pronounced like the “a” in “late”), and ō represents omega (like the “o” in “note”). The letters ch represent the Greek letter chi, a throaty “k” sound, as in German “Bach.” An initial h marks a Greek word that began with a breathed “h” sound. Hebrew is transliterated simply, without special marks.

From time to time the study explains a point of Greek grammar—the tense of a verb, its voice, the presence or absence of the definite article, the order of words. These are not decorations. Greek is a precise language, and the author of Hebrews was one of its finest writers in the New Testament. The following brief guide explains the terms most often used:

  • Aorist tense: presents an action as a whole, a single completed event, without describing its progress (“he sat down”). It is the default past tense of narrative.
  • Present tense: presents an action as ongoing or in progress (“he is upholding”), or as habitual.
  • Perfect tense: presents a past action whose results stand in the present (“he has sat down,” and is still seated). Hebrews uses it often and pointedly.
  • Participle: a verbal adjective (“having made purification,” “being the radiance”). Greek builds long sentences by stacking participles, and their tense shows how actions relate in time.
  • Active, middle, passive voice: whether the subject acts (“he offered”), acts with special reference to himself (“he made for himself”), or is acted upon (“he was offered”).
  • Subjunctive and imperative: the moods of exhortation. Hebrews’ characteristic “let us draw near,” “let us hold fast” are hortatory subjunctives—the preacher includes himself in the call.
  • The article: Greek has a definite article (“the”) but no indefinite article (“a”). When a noun lacks the article, Greek is often stressing its quality or kind: “in a Son” (1:2) means “in one who is Son.”

The Septuagint

The author of Hebrews quotes the Old Testament constantly—more densely than any other New Testament book—and he almost always quotes it from the Greek translation made by Jews in the centuries before Christ, known as the Septuagint (abbreviated LXX, “the Seventy,” after the tradition that seventy-two elders produced the translation of the Law). This was the Bible of Greek-speaking synagogues and of the early churches. In most places the Septuagint renders the Hebrew closely. In a handful of places that matter for Hebrews—Psalm 8, Psalm 40, Psalm 95, Deuteronomy 32:43, Genesis 47:31, Habakkuk 2:3–4, Proverbs 3:11–12, and others—it differs from the Masoretic Hebrew. Wherever that happens, this study prints the Hebrew, translates both forms, and shows how the author’s argument stands on the meaning shared by both, or how the Septuagint’s wording draws out something truly present in the Hebrew.

The Layout of Each Section

The study follows the chapters of Hebrews. Within each chapter the text is divided into sections (paragraph units of thought), following the familiar section divisions of modern English Bibles. Several of the author’s units of thought run across our chapter divisions (for example, 3:7–4:13 and 5:11–6:12); in those places the section is divided at the chapter break and a note tells you where it began or continues. Each section contains:

  1. Translation: the whole section translated afresh, so that it can be read in one sweep before it is studied in detail.
  2. Setting and Structure: where the section fits in the argument of Hebrews, how it is built, and any historical background needed for the whole.
  3. Verse by Verse: each verse is printed again and explained. Within these entries you will find boxed notes:
    • Word Study: a closer look at a key Greek or Hebrew word.
    • Historical Background: the customs, institutions, events, and literature of the ancient world that the first readers knew and we must learn.
    • Old Testament Text: the Hebrew text of a passage Hebrews quotes, translated and compared with the Greek.
    • Textual Note: where ancient manuscripts differ, what the evidence is, and why the reading followed here is preferred.
    • Interpretive Options: where faithful interpreters have differed, the main views, weighed by the text.
  4. For Reflection: questions for personal study or group discussion.

The Use of Commentary

The study draws on the long history of the church’s reading of Hebrews—from John Chrysostom’s fourth-century sermons, through the Reformers John Calvin and John Owen, to the great critical and expository commentaries of the nineteenth, twentieth, and twenty-first centuries (Westcott, Delitzsch, Bruce, Hughes, Lane, Ellingworth, Attridge, Koester, deSilva, Cockerill, Schreiner, and others; see the Bibliography). Commentators are cited to illuminate the text and the author’s intent, never to overrule it. The governing principle throughout is the one the Reformers called Scripture interprets Scripture: an interpretation that the Bible itself contradicts has been set aside, however eminent its source. Where good interpreters genuinely differ, the main views are set out fairly, weighed by the wording of the Greek, the flow of the argument, and the witness of the whole canon, and a conclusion is offered—with the humility that hard texts deserve.

The commentators’ views are summarized in the study’s own words rather than quoted, except for a few brief, well-known sayings from ancient writers.
