6:6

and then have fallen away—it is impossible to renew them again to repentance, since they are crucifying the Son of God again for themselves and holding him up to public contempt.

“And then have fallen away.” The fifth participle, παραπεσόντας parapesontas, comes from παραπίπτω parapiptō, literally “fall beside, fall away, go astray.” It occurs only here in the New Testament. In the Greek translation of Ezekiel it repeatedly renders the Hebrew idea of acting faithlessly against God, breaking faith with him (for example, Ezek 14:13; 18:24; 20:27). The context of Hebrews makes the meaning unmistakable. This is not a moral lapse, a season of coldness, or even a grievous sin of weakness; it is the deliberate and settled abandonment of Christ. The author has already named it in 3:12: “See to it, brothers, that there not be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, in falling away (ἀποστῆναι apostēnai) from the living God.” He will describe it again in 10:26–29 as sinning “willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth,” and as trampling underfoot the Son of God, counting the blood of the covenant a common thing, and insulting the Spirit of grace. The sin in view is apostasy: a knowing, public repudiation of the Son after having experienced the grace that comes through him.

This observation alone resolves a good deal of pastoral anguish. The passage is not about believers who stumble, even badly. Peter denied the Lord three times with oaths, yet Jesus had prayed that his faith would not fail, and he was restored (Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–19). The Corinthian church contained believers guilty of serious sin who were called to repentance and received back (2 Cor 2:5–11; 7:9–10). John writes to Christians that “if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9). Hebrews itself presents Jesus as the high priest who “is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward” (5:2) and who invites the weak to draw near to the throne of grace “for help in time of need” (4:15–16). The person who is grieved over sin and longs to return to Christ is, by that very longing, showing that he is not the person described here; for the apostate is not seeking Christ but rejecting him.

“To renew them again to repentance.” The infinitive ἀνακαινίζειν anakainizein means “to renew, restore.” “Again” (πάλιν palin) picks up the “again” of 6:1: there is no laying the foundation “again,” and there is no renewing to repentance “again.” The phrase implies that these people had once come to repentance—they had made that initial turning which is the first stone of the foundation (6:1). The infinitive has no expressed subject. Some have argued that the impossibility is limited to human ministers (“it is impossible for us to renew them”), leaving open the possibility that God might. But the sentence makes no such limitation, the author’s use of “impossible” is absolute elsewhere, and the following clause grounds the impossibility not in the limits of preachers but in what the apostates themselves are doing to Christ. Repentance, moreover, is itself God’s gift (Acts 11:18; 2 Tim 2:25). If God does not grant it—“if God permits” (6:3)—no human effort can produce it. Esau, “afterward wishing to inherit the blessing, was rejected, for he found no place for repentance, though he sought it with tears” (12:17).

“Since they are crucifying the Son of God again.” Now the participles change from aorist to present: ἀνασταυροῦντας ἑαυτοῖς τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ παραδειγματίζοντας anastaurountas heautois ton huion tou theou kai paradeigmatizontas. A Greek participle of this kind can express several relations to the main verb, and two are possible here. It may be temporal: “while they are crucifying …” —that is, renewal is impossible as long as they persist in this attitude. Or it may be causal: “since they are crucifying …”—that is, renewal is impossible because of what they are doing. The temporal reading has appealed to those who wish to leave the door open for later return. But many commentators have objected that it reduces the solemn warning to a truism (no one can be brought to repentance while he is refusing to repent), and that it fits poorly with the author’s parallel statements in 10:26 (“there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins”) and 12:17. The causal reading gives the reason for the impossibility: by turning from Christ, the apostate has repudiated the one sacrifice that brings forgiveness and the one source of repentance, and there is no other. Even on the temporal reading, however, the author does not contemplate the apostate’s later change of heart; the present tense describes a settled stance, the condition of a heart hardened by sin’s deceit (3:13).

Word Study: ἀνασταυρόω anastauroō and παραδειγματίζω paradeigmatizō

Anastauroō is formed from ἀνά ana (“up” or “again”) and σταυρόω stauroō (“crucify”). In ordinary Greek, including Josephus, the compound usually means simply “to crucify,” to raise up on a stake. The Greek-speaking church fathers, however, including Chrysostom, understood it here as “crucify again,” and the context strongly favors that sense: the sentence is full of “again” (6:1, 6), and the author’s point is that the apostates repeat in their own persons the verdict of those who crucified Jesus. The dative ἑαυτοῖς heautois, “for themselves” or “to their own harm,” suggests both: they crucify him as far as they themselves are concerned, endorsing the judgment of the cross as their own; and they do it to their own ruin.

Paradeigmatizō means “to make a public example of, expose to public disgrace.” In the Greek Old Testament it describes the public exposure of executed offenders (Num 25:4), and a related verb is used when Joseph did not want to expose Mary to public shame (Matt 1:19). Crucifixion itself was designed for exactly this purpose: it was the Roman world’s most degrading death, staged in public places so that the condemned would be displayed as an object of contempt. The apostate, by publicly renouncing Jesus, sides with those who mocked him at Golgotha and holds him up once more to contempt before the watching world.

In the honor-and-shame culture of the first century, this language would have been felt keenly. The hearers had once “endured a hard struggle with sufferings,” being “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:32–33). To abandon Christ in order to escape that shame would be to transfer the shame back onto him, declaring before family, synagogue, and city that the crucified one deserved his cross. A generation later, the Roman governor Pliny reported to the emperor Trajan that those accused of being Christians could clear themselves by offering incense to the emperor’s image and by cursing Christ, something, he had heard, that genuine Christians could not be forced to do (Letters 10.96). Such an act is exactly the kind of public repudiation Hebrews has in mind. Compare Paul’s words: “no one speaking by the Spirit of God says, ‘Jesus is accursed’” (1 Cor 12:3).

The title “the Son of God” is chosen with care. The whole sermon began with the majesty of the Son, through whom God has spoken his final word (1:1–4), who is superior to angels, to Moses, and to the Levitical priests. To turn from such a Son is not to choose a lesser good; it is to reject the very one through whom God speaks and saves. There is no one else to whom to go (compare John 6:68). That is why renewal is impossible: the apostate has not merely left a church; he has rejected the only remedy.

Historical Background: The Passage in the Early Church

Hebrews 6:4–6 played a large part in early Christian debates about whether serious sins committed after baptism could be forgiven. The Shepherd of Hermas, written at Rome in the second century, taught that after baptism there is one further opportunity for repentance and no more (Mandate 4.3). Tertullian, in his later rigorist period, quoted this passage (attributing Hebrews to Barnabas) to argue that adulterers and fornicators who had been baptized could not be restored to the church (On Modesty 20). In the third century the Novatianists appealed to it to refuse readmission to those who had lapsed under persecution. The use of the passage by such rigorist groups is one reason why the Latin West was slow to accept Hebrews as canonical. Other fathers read it differently: Chrysostom, for example, understood the impossibility to refer to a second baptism—one cannot be baptized again—while insisting that repentance remains open to sinners.

At the Reformation, Martin Luther found the passage (with 10:26 and 12:17) a hard knot, because it seemed to him to deny repentance to sinners after baptism, against the teaching of the Gospels and Paul; this was one reason he placed Hebrews near the end of his German New Testament. These historical difficulties largely arise from reading the passage as though it concerned any serious sin after conversion. Read in its context—as describing a deliberate renunciation of Christ himself (3:12; 10:26–29)—it does not contradict the gospel’s free offer of forgiveness to repentant sinners; it warns against the one sin that refuses that forgiveness at its only source.

Interpretive Options: Who Are “Those Who Have Fallen Away”?

Christians who equally love Scripture have read this passage in several ways. The following summarizes the major views as fairly as possible; a weighing of them, in light of the verses that follow, is given after 6:12.

  • View 1: Genuine believers who lose their salvation. On this reading, verses 4–5 describe true Christians in the fullest sense: enlightened, partakers of the Spirit, recipients of the heavenly gift. The “again” of verse 6 implies that their first repentance was real. Such believers can, by deliberate apostasy, forfeit the salvation they once possessed, and when they do, their loss is irreversible. This view is held widely in the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions. Its strength is that it takes the descriptions of 6:4–5 at their most natural face value and gives full weight to the seriousness of the warning. Some modern scholars, such as David deSilva, read the passage against the background of ancient patronage: a client who publicly insults his benefactor after receiving his gifts places himself beyond the benefactor’s favor; so the apostate, having received God’s gifts, dishonors the divine Patron and forfeits his grace.
  • View 2: Professing but unregenerate members of the covenant community. On this reading, the people described had real and profound experiences of the Spirit’s work in the church—illumination, conviction, the joy of the word, the sight of miracles—but had never been born again. Their falling away reveals that they never truly belonged to Christ. Calvin taught that God gives even the reprobate a certain taste of his grace and some sparks of his light, without the Spirit’s regenerating work; John Owen argued at length that none of the descriptions in 6:4–5 names a grace that is distinctive of the regenerate. Supporters point to 3:14 (“we have become sharers of Christ, if we hold fast”), to 1 John 2:19 (“they went out from us, but they were not of us”), to Jesus’ parable of the soils, in which the rocky ground “receives the word with joy” yet falls away in time of testing (Luke 8:13), and above all to the author’s own contrast in 6:9 between these experiences and “things that belong to salvation.”
  • View 3: A real warning used by God as a means of perseverance. On this reading, the warning is addressed to the whole church, including the truly regenerate, and it describes what would certainly happen to anyone who turned away from Christ. Its purpose is not primarily to classify past apostates but to keep present hearers from becoming such. God preserves his elect, but he does so through means—including warnings like this one, which genuine believers heed. Thomas Schreiner has argued for this view (with A. B. Caneday): the warnings are conditional rather than merely hypothetical; the condition is real and the consequence is eternal, and the warnings are one of the ways God brings his people safely to the end. A cruder form of this view—that the warning describes something that is simply impossible and therefore unreal—has been held by some, but it struggles to explain why an author would solemnly warn against a danger that does not exist.
  • View 4: Genuine believers who lose reward, not salvation. Some interpreters hold that the passage concerns true Christians who, by falling away, cannot be brought back to fruitful repentance in this life and will suffer loss of reward and divine discipline, though they remain saved (they appeal to 1 Cor 3:15, “he himself will be saved, but only as through fire”). This view takes seriously both the description of genuine believers and the promise of eternal security. But it must explain away the language of Hebrews, which speaks elsewhere of the same danger as “eternal judgment” (6:2), a “fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27), and “shrinking back to destruction” (ἀπώλεια apōleia, 10:39), in contrast to “the preserving of the soul.” The “burning” of 6:8 is set over against “salvation” in 6:9. The context does not allow the consequence to be reduced to lost rewards.
  • Other proposals. Some ancient interpreters, as noted above, understood the impossibility as the impossibility of a second baptism; others limited it to human ministers. Neither reading accounts adequately for the absolute “impossible” or for the reason given in 6:6b.

6:7

For land that drinks the rain that often comes upon it, and brings forth vegetation useful to those for whose sake it is also farmed, receives a share of blessing from God;

The word “for” (γάρ gar) introduces an illustration that supports and explains the warning. The author turns from theological description to a picture any farmer would understand. The parable has two halves (6:7 and 6:8), and the decisive feature is that both halves begin from the same situation. A single subject, “land” (γῆ gē), governs both halves: it drinks the rain, and then it either brings forth useful vegetation or bears thorns. What differs is not the rain but what the land produces. The point is not that God gives one field rain and withholds it from another. Both receive “the rain that often comes upon it.” The difference lies in the response.

The participle πιοῦσα piousa, “having drunk,” pictures the soil soaking up the rain. “Often” (πολλάκις pollakis) underlines the abundance and repetition of God’s gifts. In the parable’s application, the rain corresponds to the blessings of 6:4–5: enlightenment, the heavenly gift, the Spirit, the good word of God, the powers of the age to come. Those blessings had fallen richly upon the hearers. The question is what they would produce.

Old Testament Text: The Land That Drinks the Rain (Deut 11:11–12)

Hebrew: אֶרֶץ הָרִים וּבְקָעֹת לִמְטַר הַשָּׁמַיִם תִּשְׁתֶּה־מָּיִם — erets harim uvqaot limtar hashamayim tishteh-mayim, “a land of hills and valleys; by the rain of heaven it drinks water.” The next verse continues: “a land that the Lord your God cares for; the eyes of the Lord your God are always on it, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year” (Deut 11:12).

Moses contrasted the promised land with Egypt, which depended on irrigation from the Nile. Canaan would drink rain directly from heaven, a constant reminder of dependence on God. The same chapter sets before Israel “a blessing and a curse” (Deut 11:26–28): blessing if they obeyed, curse if they turned aside. Hebrews’ language of land that drinks the rain and receives “blessing,” or else lies “near to a curse,” echoes this covenant setting. The audience, like Israel on the edge of Canaan, stands between blessing and curse.

The good land “brings forth vegetation useful to those for whose sake it is also farmed.” The verb τίκτουσα tiktousa literally means “giving birth,” a vivid image of fruitfulness. The word βοτάνη botanē means plants, vegetation, herbage, and εὔθετος euthetos means “fit, suitable, useful” (compare Luke 9:62, “fit for the kingdom of God”; Luke 14:35, salt “fit” neither for the soil nor for the manure pile). The land is farmed (γεωργεῖται geōrgeitai, “is cultivated”) for the benefit of others: its fruit serves those who tend it. Already the author is preparing for 6:10, where the fruit he sees in his hearers is their loving service “to the saints.” A fruitful Christian life is not self-enclosed; its crop feeds others.

Such land “receives a share of blessing from God” (μεταλαμβάνει εὐλογίας ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ metalambanei eulogias apo tou theou). The blessing is not earned by the land, which produced its crop only because God sent the rain. It is the further blessing God gives to what has received his first gifts fruitfully—the principle Jesus stated: “to everyone who has, more will be given” (Matt 25:29). The word “blessing” (εὐλογία eulogia) will reappear in 6:14 in God’s oath to Abraham, “blessing I will bless you,” and in 12:17, the blessing that Esau forfeited.

Jesus’ own agricultural parables are the nearest New Testament parallels. In the parable of the soils, the same seed falls on different ground; the rocky ground “receives the word with joy,” yet “has no root” and in time of testing “falls away” (ἀφίστανται aphistantai, Luke 8:13), while the good soil bears fruit “with endurance” (ἐν ὑπομονῇ en hypomonē, Luke 8:15). Endurance is precisely what Hebrews urges (10:36; 12:1). And in John 15, branches that do not bear fruit are gathered and burned (John 15:6). In each case the visible fruit, borne over time, shows what kind of ground or branch it was.

6:8

but if it bears thorns and thistles, it is worthless and near to a curse, and its end is to be burned.

The second half of the parable presents the same land producing a different crop: ἐκφέρουσα δὲ ἀκάνθας καὶ τριβόλους ekpherousa de akanthas kai tribolous, “but bearing thorns and thistles.” The present participle is conditional in force (“if it bears”). Thorns and thistles are not merely a lack of fruit; they are a harmful crop, choking whatever else might grow and injuring those who walk through them.

Old Testament Text: Thorns and Thistles (Gen 3:17–18; Isa 5:1–7)

The phrase comes directly from the curse on the ground after Adam’s sin. Hebrew: אֲרוּרָה הָאֲדָמָה בַּעֲבוּרֶךָ — arurah haadamah baavurekha, “cursed is the ground because of you” (Gen 3:17); וְקוֹץ וְדַרְדַּר תַּצְמִיחַ לָךְ — veqots vedardar tatsmiach lakh, “and thorns and thistles it shall sprout for you” (Gen 3:18). The Greek translation of Genesis renders “thorns and thistles” with exactly the two words Hebrews uses, ἀκάνθας καὶ τριβόλους akanthas kai tribolous. The listener familiar with the Greek Bible would hear in this phrase the language of the curse.

Isaiah’s Song of the Vineyard (Isa 5:1–7) offers an even closer parallel. The owner did everything for his vineyard and “waited for it to produce grapes, but it produced stinking grapes” (וַיְקַו לַעֲשׂוֹת עֲנָבִים וַיַּעַשׂ בְּאֻשִׁים vayqav laasot anavim vayyaas beushim, Isa 5:2). He asks, “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it?” (Isa 5:4). So he declares that it will be laid waste and overgrown: וְעָלָה שָׁמִיר וָשָׁיִת וְעַל הֶעָבִים אֲצַוֶּה מֵהַמְטִיר עָלָיו מָטָר — vealah shamir vashayit veal heavim atsavveh mehamtir alav matar, “and briers and thorns shall come up, and I will command the clouds not to rain rain upon it” (Isa 5:6). The vineyard is “the house of Israel” (Isa 5:7). In both Isaiah and Hebrews, the people of God receive lavish care, and fruitlessness after such care invites judgment.

The land that bears thorns is ἀδόκιμος adokimos, “worthless,” more precisely “failing the test, disqualified, rejected after testing.” The word was used of coins or metals that failed assay. Paul uses it of himself in 1 Corinthians 9:27 (“lest … I myself should be disqualified”) and in 2 Corinthians 13:5–7 (“test yourselves … unless indeed you fail the test”). Testing is a major theme of Hebrews: Israel “tested” God in the wilderness (3:9); Christ was “tested” in every respect (4:15); Abraham was “tested” (11:17). The barren land, after many rains, has shown what it is.

It is “near to a curse” (κατάρας ἐγγύς kataras engys). Chrysostom drew comfort from the word “near”: the land is near a curse, not yet under it; there is still time. That observation fits the author’s purpose. He does not say that any of his hearers have already become such land; he holds up the picture while there is still “today.” But the final clause allows no softening of the outcome: ἧς τὸ τέλος εἰς καῦσιν hēs to telos eis kausin, “whose end is for burning.” Ancient farmers sometimes burned off weeds and stubble, and a few interpreters have suggested that the burning is therefore purifying, preparing the field for a new crop. But the word “end” (τέλος telos) speaks of final outcome, and fire in Hebrews is the fire of judgment: “a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27); “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29). The same picture appears in John the Baptist’s preaching (Matt 3:10, 12) and in Jesus’ words about fruitless branches (John 15:6). The burning is the end of a land that has persistently refused to bear fruit after all God’s rain.

The parable thus explains the warning of 6:4–6 in a way that helps us read it rightly. It does not focus on the quality of the rain—the rain is the same in both cases—but on the crop. The question it presses on the hearers is not, “Did God’s blessings come to you?” They had. The question is, “What has grown from them?” The person who has received much from God and responds with the thorns of unbelief and renunciation shows by that crop what kind of ground he is. The author will turn immediately to the crop he sees in his hearers.
