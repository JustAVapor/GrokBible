6:6

and then have fallen away—it is impossible to renew them again to repentance, since they are crucifying the Son of God again for themselves and holding him up to public contempt.

“And then have fallen away.” The fifth participle, παραπεσόντας parapesontas, comes from παραπίπτω parapiptō, literally “fall beside, fall away, go astray.” It occurs only here in the New Testament. In the Greek translation of Ezekiel it repeatedly renders the Hebrew idea of acting faithlessly against God, breaking faith with him (for example, Ezek 14:13; 18:24; 20:27). The context of Hebrews makes the meaning unmistakable. This is not a moral lapse, a season of coldness, or even a grievous sin of weakness; it is the deliberate and settled abandonment of Christ. The author has already named it in 3:12: “See to it, brothers, that there not be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, in falling away (ἀποστῆναι apostēnai) from the living God.” He will describe it again in 10:26–29 as sinning “willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth,” and as trampling underfoot the Son of God, counting the blood of the covenant a common thing, and insulting the Spirit of grace. The sin in view is apostasy: a knowing, public repudiation of the Son after having experienced the grace that comes through him.

This observation alone resolves a good deal of pastoral anguish. The passage is not about believers who stumble, even badly. Peter denied the Lord three times with oaths, yet Jesus had prayed that his faith would not fail, and he was restored (Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–19). The Corinthian church contained believers guilty of serious sin who were called to repentance and received back (2 Cor 2:5–11; 7:9–10). John writes to Christians that “if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9). Hebrews itself presents Jesus as the high priest who “is able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward” (5:2) and who invites the weak to draw near to the throne of grace “for help in time of need” (4:15–16). The person who is grieved over sin and longs to return to Christ is, by that very longing, showing that he is not the person described here; for the apostate is not seeking Christ but rejecting him.

“To renew them again to repentance.” The infinitive ἀνακαινίζειν anakainizein means “to renew, restore.” “Again” (πάλιν palin) picks up the “again” of 6:1: there is no laying the foundation “again,” and there is no renewing to repentance “again.” The phrase implies that these people had once come to repentance—they had made that initial turning which is the first stone of the foundation (6:1). The infinitive has no expressed subject. Some have argued that the impossibility is limited to human ministers (“it is impossible for us to renew them”), leaving open the possibility that God might. But the sentence makes no such limitation, the author’s use of “impossible” is absolute elsewhere, and the following clause grounds the impossibility not in the limits of preachers but in what the apostates themselves are doing to Christ. Repentance, moreover, is itself God’s gift (Acts 11:18; 2 Tim 2:25). If God does not grant it—“if God permits” (6:3)—no human effort can produce it. Esau, “afterward wishing to inherit the blessing, was rejected, for he found no place for repentance, though he sought it with tears” (12:17).

“Since they are crucifying the Son of God again.” Now the participles change from aorist to present: ἀνασταυροῦντας ἑαυτοῖς τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ παραδειγματίζοντας anastaurountas heautois ton huion tou theou kai paradeigmatizontas. A Greek participle of this kind can express several relations to the main verb, and two are possible here. It may be temporal: “while they are crucifying …” —that is, renewal is impossible as long as they persist in this attitude. Or it may be causal: “since they are crucifying …”—that is, renewal is impossible because of what they are doing. The temporal reading has appealed to those who wish to leave the door open for later return. But many commentators have objected that it reduces the solemn warning to a truism (no one can be brought to repentance while he is refusing to repent), and that it fits poorly with the author’s parallel statements in 10:26 (“there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins”) and 12:17. The causal reading gives the reason for the impossibility: by turning from Christ, the apostate has repudiated the one sacrifice that brings forgiveness and the one source of repentance, and there is no other. Even on the temporal reading, however, the author does not contemplate the apostate’s later change of heart; the present tense describes a settled stance, the condition of a heart hardened by sin’s deceit (3:13).

Word Study: ἀνασταυρόω anastauroō and παραδειγματίζω paradeigmatizō

Anastauroō is formed from ἀνά ana (“up” or “again”) and σταυρόω stauroō (“crucify”). In ordinary Greek, including Josephus, the compound usually means simply “to crucify,” to raise up on a stake. The Greek-speaking church fathers, however, including Chrysostom, understood it here as “crucify again,” and the context strongly favors that sense: the sentence is full of “again” (6:1, 6), and the author’s point is that the apostates repeat in their own persons the verdict of those who crucified Jesus. The dative ἑαυτοῖς heautois, “for themselves” or “to their own harm,” suggests both: they crucify him as far as they themselves are concerned, endorsing the judgment of the cross as their own; and they do it to their own ruin.

Paradeigmatizō means “to make a public example of, expose to public disgrace.” In the Greek Old Testament it describes the public exposure of executed offenders (Num 25:4), and a related verb is used when Joseph did not want to expose Mary to public shame (Matt 1:19). Crucifixion itself was designed for exactly this purpose: it was the Roman world’s most degrading death, staged in public places so that the condemned would be displayed as an object of contempt. The apostate, by publicly renouncing Jesus, sides with those who mocked him at Golgotha and holds him up once more to contempt before the watching world.

In the honor-and-shame culture of the first century, this language would have been felt keenly. The hearers had once “endured a hard struggle with sufferings,” being “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:32–33). To abandon Christ in order to escape that shame would be to transfer the shame back onto him, declaring before family, synagogue, and city that the crucified one deserved his cross. A generation later, the Roman governor Pliny reported to the emperor Trajan that those accused of being Christians could clear themselves by offering incense to the emperor’s image and by cursing Christ, something, he had heard, that genuine Christians could not be forced to do (Letters 10.96). Such an act is exactly the kind of public repudiation Hebrews has in mind. Compare Paul’s words: “no one speaking by the Spirit of God says, ‘Jesus is accursed’” (1 Cor 12:3).

The title “the Son of God” is chosen with care. The whole sermon began with the majesty of the Son, through whom God has spoken his final word (1:1–4), who is superior to angels, to Moses, and to the Levitical priests. To turn from such a Son is not to choose a lesser good; it is to reject the very one through whom God speaks and saves. There is no one else to whom to go (compare John 6:68). That is why renewal is impossible: the apostate has not merely left a church; he has rejected the only remedy.

Historical Background: The Passage in the Early Church

Hebrews 6:4–6 played a large part in early Christian debates about whether serious sins committed after baptism could be forgiven. The Shepherd of Hermas, written at Rome in the second century, taught that after baptism there is one further opportunity for repentance and no more (Mandate 4.3). Tertullian, in his later rigorist period, quoted this passage (attributing Hebrews to Barnabas) to argue that adulterers and fornicators who had been baptized could not be restored to the church (On Modesty 20). In the third century the Novatianists appealed to it to refuse readmission to those who had lapsed under persecution. The use of the passage by such rigorist groups is one reason why the Latin West was slow to accept Hebrews as canonical. Other fathers read it differently: Chrysostom, for example, understood the impossibility to refer to a second baptism—one cannot be baptized again—while insisting that repentance remains open to sinners.

At the Reformation, Martin Luther found the passage (with 10:26 and 12:17) a hard knot, because it seemed to him to deny repentance to sinners after baptism, against the teaching of the Gospels and Paul; this was one reason he placed Hebrews near the end of his German New Testament. These historical difficulties largely arise from reading the passage as though it concerned any serious sin after conversion. Read in its context—as describing a deliberate renunciation of Christ himself (3:12; 10:26–29)—it does not contradict the gospel’s free offer of forgiveness to repentant sinners; it warns against the one sin that refuses that forgiveness at its only source.

Interpretive Options: Who Are “Those Who Have Fallen Away”?

Christians who equally love Scripture have read this passage in several ways. The following summarizes the major views as fairly as possible; a weighing of them, in light of the verses that follow, is given after 6:12.

  • View 1: Genuine believers who lose their salvation. On this reading, verses 4–5 describe true Christians in the fullest sense: enlightened, partakers of the Spirit, recipients of the heavenly gift. The “again” of verse 6 implies that their first repentance was real. Such believers can, by deliberate apostasy, forfeit the salvation they once possessed, and when they do, their loss is irreversible. This view is held widely in the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions. Its strength is that it takes the descriptions of 6:4–5 at their most natural face value and gives full weight to the seriousness of the warning. Some modern scholars, such as David deSilva, read the passage against the background of ancient patronage: a client who publicly insults his benefactor after receiving his gifts places himself beyond the benefactor’s favor; so the apostate, having received God’s gifts, dishonors the divine Patron and forfeits his grace.
  • View 2: Professing but unregenerate members of the covenant community. On this reading, the people described had real and profound experiences of the Spirit’s work in the church—illumination, conviction, the joy of the word, the sight of miracles—but had never been born again. Their falling away reveals that they never truly belonged to Christ. Calvin taught that God gives even the reprobate a certain taste of his grace and some sparks of his light, without the Spirit’s regenerating work; John Owen argued at length that none of the descriptions in 6:4–5 names a grace that is distinctive of the regenerate. Supporters point to 3:14 (“we have become sharers of Christ, if we hold fast”), to 1 John 2:19 (“they went out from us, but they were not of us”), to Jesus’ parable of the soils, in which the rocky ground “receives the word with joy” yet falls away in time of testing (Luke 8:13), and above all to the author’s own contrast in 6:9 between these experiences and “things that belong to salvation.”
  • View 3: A real warning used by God as a means of perseverance. On this reading, the warning is addressed to the whole church, including the truly regenerate, and it describes what would certainly happen to anyone who turned away from Christ. Its purpose is not primarily to classify past apostates but to keep present hearers from becoming such. God preserves his elect, but he does so through means—including warnings like this one, which genuine believers heed. Thomas Schreiner has argued for this view (with A. B. Caneday): the warnings are conditional rather than merely hypothetical; the condition is real and the consequence is eternal, and the warnings are one of the ways God brings his people safely to the end. A cruder form of this view—that the warning describes something that is simply impossible and therefore unreal—has been held by some, but it struggles to explain why an author would solemnly warn against a danger that does not exist.
  • View 4: Genuine believers who lose reward, not salvation. Some interpreters hold that the passage concerns true Christians who, by falling away, cannot be brought back to fruitful repentance in this life and will suffer loss of reward and divine discipline, though they remain saved (they appeal to 1 Cor 3:15, “he himself will be saved, but only as through fire”). This view takes seriously both the description of genuine believers and the promise of eternal security. But it must explain away the language of Hebrews, which speaks elsewhere of the same danger as “eternal judgment” (6:2), a “fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27), and “shrinking back to destruction” (ἀπώλεια apōleia, 10:39), in contrast to “the preserving of the soul.” The “burning” of 6:8 is set over against “salvation” in 6:9. The context does not allow the consequence to be reduced to lost rewards.
  • Other proposals. Some ancient interpreters, as noted above, understood the impossibility as the impossibility of a second baptism; others limited it to human ministers. Neither reading accounts adequately for the absolute “impossible” or for the reason given in 6:6b.

6:7

For land that drinks the rain that often comes upon it, and brings forth vegetation useful to those for whose sake it is also farmed, receives a share of blessing from God;

The word “for” (γάρ gar) introduces an illustration that supports and explains the warning. The author turns from theological description to a picture any farmer would understand. The parable has two halves (6:7 and 6:8), and the decisive feature is that both halves begin from the same situation. A single subject, “land” (γῆ gē), governs both halves: it drinks the rain, and then it either brings forth useful vegetation or bears thorns. What differs is not the rain but what the land produces. The point is not that God gives one field rain and withholds it from another. Both receive “the rain that often comes upon it.” The difference lies in the response.

The participle πιοῦσα piousa, “having drunk,” pictures the soil soaking up the rain. “Often” (πολλάκις pollakis) underlines the abundance and repetition of God’s gifts. In the parable’s application, the rain corresponds to the blessings of 6:4–5: enlightenment, the heavenly gift, the Spirit, the good word of God, the powers of the age to come. Those blessings had fallen richly upon the hearers. The question is what they would produce.

Old Testament Text: The Land That Drinks the Rain (Deut 11:11–12)

Hebrew: אֶרֶץ הָרִים וּבְקָעֹת לִמְטַר הַשָּׁמַיִם תִּשְׁתֶּה־מָּיִם — erets harim uvqaot limtar hashamayim tishteh-mayim, “a land of hills and valleys; by the rain of heaven it drinks water.” The next verse continues: “a land that the Lord your God cares for; the eyes of the Lord your God are always on it, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year” (Deut 11:12).

Moses contrasted the promised land with Egypt, which depended on irrigation from the Nile. Canaan would drink rain directly from heaven, a constant reminder of dependence on God. The same chapter sets before Israel “a blessing and a curse” (Deut 11:26–28): blessing if they obeyed, curse if they turned aside. Hebrews’ language of land that drinks the rain and receives “blessing,” or else lies “near to a curse,” echoes this covenant setting. The audience, like Israel on the edge of Canaan, stands between blessing and curse.

The good land “brings forth vegetation useful to those for whose sake it is also farmed.” The verb τίκτουσα tiktousa literally means “giving birth,” a vivid image of fruitfulness. The word βοτάνη botanē means plants, vegetation, herbage, and εὔθετος euthetos means “fit, suitable, useful” (compare Luke 9:62, “fit for the kingdom of God”; Luke 14:35, salt “fit” neither for the soil nor for the manure pile). The land is farmed (γεωργεῖται geōrgeitai, “is cultivated”) for the benefit of others: its fruit serves those who tend it. Already the author is preparing for 6:10, where the fruit he sees in his hearers is their loving service “to the saints.” A fruitful Christian life is not self-enclosed; its crop feeds others.

Such land “receives a share of blessing from God” (μεταλαμβάνει εὐλογίας ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ metalambanei eulogias apo tou theou). The blessing is not earned by the land, which produced its crop only because God sent the rain. It is the further blessing God gives to what has received his first gifts fruitfully—the principle Jesus stated: “to everyone who has, more will be given” (Matt 25:29). The word “blessing” (εὐλογία eulogia) will reappear in 6:14 in God’s oath to Abraham, “blessing I will bless you,” and in 12:17, the blessing that Esau forfeited.

Jesus’ own agricultural parables are the nearest New Testament parallels. In the parable of the soils, the same seed falls on different ground; the rocky ground “receives the word with joy,” yet “has no root” and in time of testing “falls away” (ἀφίστανται aphistantai, Luke 8:13), while the good soil bears fruit “with endurance” (ἐν ὑπομονῇ en hypomonē, Luke 8:15). Endurance is precisely what Hebrews urges (10:36; 12:1). And in John 15, branches that do not bear fruit are gathered and burned (John 15:6). In each case the visible fruit, borne over time, shows what kind of ground or branch it was.

6:8

but if it bears thorns and thistles, it is worthless and near to a curse, and its end is to be burned.

The second half of the parable presents the same land producing a different crop: ἐκφέρουσα δὲ ἀκάνθας καὶ τριβόλους ekpherousa de akanthas kai tribolous, “but bearing thorns and thistles.” The present participle is conditional in force (“if it bears”). Thorns and thistles are not merely a lack of fruit; they are a harmful crop, choking whatever else might grow and injuring those who walk through them.

Old Testament Text: Thorns and Thistles (Gen 3:17–18; Isa 5:1–7)

The phrase comes directly from the curse on the ground after Adam’s sin. Hebrew: אֲרוּרָה הָאֲדָמָה בַּעֲבוּרֶךָ — arurah haadamah baavurekha, “cursed is the ground because of you” (Gen 3:17); וְקוֹץ וְדַרְדַּר תַּצְמִיחַ לָךְ — veqots vedardar tatsmiach lakh, “and thorns and thistles it shall sprout for you” (Gen 3:18). The Greek translation of Genesis renders “thorns and thistles” with exactly the two words Hebrews uses, ἀκάνθας καὶ τριβόλους akanthas kai tribolous. The listener familiar with the Greek Bible would hear in this phrase the language of the curse.

Isaiah’s Song of the Vineyard (Isa 5:1–7) offers an even closer parallel. The owner did everything for his vineyard and “waited for it to produce grapes, but it produced stinking grapes” (וַיְקַו לַעֲשׂוֹת עֲנָבִים וַיַּעַשׂ בְּאֻשִׁים vayqav laasot anavim vayyaas beushim, Isa 5:2). He asks, “What more was there to do for my vineyard that I have not done in it?” (Isa 5:4). So he declares that it will be laid waste and overgrown: וְעָלָה שָׁמִיר וָשָׁיִת וְעַל הֶעָבִים אֲצַוֶּה מֵהַמְטִיר עָלָיו מָטָר — vealah shamir vashayit veal heavim atsavveh mehamtir alav matar, “and briers and thorns shall come up, and I will command the clouds not to rain rain upon it” (Isa 5:6). The vineyard is “the house of Israel” (Isa 5:7). In both Isaiah and Hebrews, the people of God receive lavish care, and fruitlessness after such care invites judgment.

The land that bears thorns is ἀδόκιμος adokimos, “worthless,” more precisely “failing the test, disqualified, rejected after testing.” The word was used of coins or metals that failed assay. Paul uses it of himself in 1 Corinthians 9:27 (“lest … I myself should be disqualified”) and in 2 Corinthians 13:5–7 (“test yourselves … unless indeed you fail the test”). Testing is a major theme of Hebrews: Israel “tested” God in the wilderness (3:9); Christ was “tested” in every respect (4:15); Abraham was “tested” (11:17). The barren land, after many rains, has shown what it is.

It is “near to a curse” (κατάρας ἐγγύς kataras engys). Chrysostom drew comfort from the word “near”: the land is near a curse, not yet under it; there is still time. That observation fits the author’s purpose. He does not say that any of his hearers have already become such land; he holds up the picture while there is still “today.” But the final clause allows no softening of the outcome: ἧς τὸ τέλος εἰς καῦσιν hēs to telos eis kausin, “whose end is for burning.” Ancient farmers sometimes burned off weeds and stubble, and a few interpreters have suggested that the burning is therefore purifying, preparing the field for a new crop. But the word “end” (τέλος telos) speaks of final outcome, and fire in Hebrews is the fire of judgment: “a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27); “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29). The same picture appears in John the Baptist’s preaching (Matt 3:10, 12) and in Jesus’ words about fruitless branches (John 15:6). The burning is the end of a land that has persistently refused to bear fruit after all God’s rain.

The parable thus explains the warning of 6:4–6 in a way that helps us read it rightly. It does not focus on the quality of the rain—the rain is the same in both cases—but on the crop. The question it presses on the hearers is not, “Did God’s blessings come to you?” They had. The question is, “What has grown from them?” The person who has received much from God and responds with the thorns of unbelief and renunciation shows by that crop what kind of ground he is. The author will turn immediately to the crop he sees in his hearers.

6:9

But concerning you, beloved, we are persuaded of better things—things that belong to salvation—even though we speak in this way.

The tone changes abruptly. The little word δέ de, “but,” sets what follows over against the dark picture of 6:4–8. Then comes a word found nowhere else in Hebrews: ἀγαπητοί agapētoi, “beloved.” Having spoken the sternest words of the sermon, the preacher immediately assures his hearers of his love. The warning was not the outburst of an angry man; it was the cry of a pastor who loves them too much to let them drift toward the fire without a word.

“We are persuaded” translates πεπείσμεθα pepeismetha, the perfect passive of πείθω peithō, “persuade.” The Greek perfect describes a present state resulting from a past action: “we have become convinced and remain so.” This is a settled conviction, not a passing hope. Paul uses the same verb in the same perfect tense in his great statement of assurance: “I am persuaded that neither death nor life … will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Rom 8:38–39), and in Romans 15:14, “I am persuaded concerning you, my brothers, that you yourselves are full of goodness.”

What is the author persuaded of? τὰ κρείσσονα καὶ ἐχόμενα σωτηρίας ta kreissona kai echomena sōtērias, “the better things, and things belonging to salvation.” “Better” (κρείσσονα kreissona, from κρείττων kreittōn) is the signature word of Hebrews: a better name (1:4), a better hope (7:19), a better covenant (7:22; 8:6), better promises (8:6), better sacrifices (9:23), a better possession (10:34), a better country (11:16), a better resurrection (11:35), something better for us (11:40), blood that speaks better (12:24). Here the “better things” stand in contrast to the burning of 6:8: the author expects of his hearers not thorns but a harvest, not curse but blessing.

Word Study: ἐχόμενα σωτηρίας echomena sōtērias

The phrase uses the middle participle of ἔχω echō, “have, hold.” In the middle voice with a genitive, it means “to hold oneself close to, to be closely connected with, to belong to.” In ordinary Greek it could describe what lies next to something (a neighboring town) or what goes with something. BDAG gives the sense here as “things that belong to salvation” or “that lead to salvation.” The phrase therefore points to what accompanies salvation, what is bound up with it and characteristic of it.

The noun σωτηρία sōtēria, “salvation,” is in Hebrews chiefly future and final: the angels serve “those who are going to inherit salvation” (1:14); Christ became “the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him” (5:9); he will appear a second time “for salvation to those who eagerly await him” (9:28). The author is persuaded that his hearers’ lives show the marks of people on the way to that salvation.

This verse is of first importance for interpreting 6:4–6. The author has just described people who had experienced remarkable blessings and then fallen away. Now he says that, concerning his hearers, he is persuaded of “better things, things that belong to salvation.” The natural implication is that the experiences of 6:4–5, impressive as they were, are not in themselves the “things that belong to salvation.” A person can receive the rain without bearing the crop. What the author sees in his hearers, and what gives him confidence, is not merely that they have been enlightened and have tasted God’s gifts; it is the fruit of 6:10—their work, their love for God’s name, their continuing service to the saints. Those are the “better things.” Interpreters who hold View 1 respond that the contrast is simply between falling away and persevering, not between two kinds of experience; and that is partly true, since the author’s confidence concerns his hearers’ future as well as their present. But the very fact that he can describe the rich experiences of 6:4–5 and then speak of “things that belong to salvation” as something additional suggests that, in his mind, the presence of those experiences did not by itself settle the question of salvation.

The closing clause, εἰ καὶ οὕτως λαλοῦμεν ei kai houtōs laloumen, “even though we speak in this way,” explains the purpose of the warning. The author speaks this way not because he has concluded that his hearers are apostates, but because he is persuaded they are not and wants them to remain so. The warning is a means to their perseverance, not a verdict on their state. Calvin remarks that the apostle spoke so severely not to cast down the faithful but to rouse the sluggish; the godly, when they hear such threatenings, are moved to humility and vigilance. In a well-ordered church the same sermon can both alarm the careless and comfort the trembling, and this author intends both effects.

6:10

For God is not unjust, so as to forget your work and the love that you showed toward his name, in having served the saints and in serving them still.

The “for” (γάρ gar) gives the ground of the author’s confidence. It is not, in the first place, his estimate of his hearers; it is the character of God. οὐ γὰρ ἄδικος ὁ θεός ou gar adikos ho theos, “for God is not unjust.” The construction with the infinitive that follows (ἐπιλαθέσθαι epilathesthai, “to forget”) means “God is not so unjust as to forget.” The language is an understatement for its opposite: God is faithful and will certainly remember.

What he remembers is “your work and the love that you showed toward his name.” The singular “work” (ἔργον ergon) views their conduct as a whole, and “love” (ἀγάπη agapē) names its root. The verb ἐνεδείξασθε enedeixasthe, “you showed, demonstrated,” is an aorist, pointing to definite past acts. Their love was shown “toward his name”—that is, it was love for God himself, expressed in love for those who bear his name. The specific form it took is spelled out in two participles: διακονήσαντες τοῖς ἁγίοις καὶ διακονοῦντες diakonēsantes tois hagiois kai diakonountes, “having served the saints and still serving.” The aorist looks back to a past season of service; the present affirms that it continues. We learn later what that past service was: in the former days, the hearers “became partners with those who were so treated” and “had compassion on those in prison” (10:33–34). In the ancient world, prisoners depended on friends and family to bring them food and necessities, and to visit a prisoner was to identify with his shame and risk the same fate. The hearers had done it, and they were still doing it (13:1–3).

Textual Note

Many later manuscripts read “your work and the labor of love” (adding τοῦ κόπου tou kopou), an expression familiar from 1 Thessalonians 1:3 (“your work of faith and labor of love”). The earliest and best manuscripts lack the addition, which was probably introduced by scribes who recalled Paul’s phrase. The meaning is unaffected.

These are the “better things” of 6:9, the useful vegetation of 6:7 that benefits “those for whose sake it is farmed.” The parable and the reassurance fit together precisely: the good land bears a crop useful to others, and the author can point to his hearers’ crop of love for fellow believers.

But how can it be said that God would be “unjust” to forget their work? Does this mean that their good works earn salvation? The rest of Scripture, and Hebrews itself, forbid that conclusion. Salvation is by the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ, by which “he has perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (10:14). The point is rather that God is faithful to his own work in his people. The love that the hearers showed was the fruit of grace; it was the crop that sprang from God’s rain. God does not abandon what he has begun: “he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion until the day of Christ Jesus” (Phil 1:6). God has bound himself by his promises to reward those who seek him (11:6) and to remember even a cup of cold water given in Jesus’ name (Matt 10:42; compare Matt 25:40). For God to ignore the fruit of his own Spirit in his children would be to deny himself. Calvin, while insisting that no work merits salvation, notes that God, having freely promised to reward good works, would be acting against his own faithfulness if he overlooked them; his “justice” here is his faithfulness to his promise. Seen this way, the hearers’ love is not the ground of their salvation but the evidence that they are the good land, and God’s justice is the guarantee that he will finish what that evidence reveals he has begun.

The verse also gently corrects any despondency the warning might have produced. A believer who looks honestly at his own life may find much sluggishness. But if he finds also, however imperfectly, love for God’s name expressed in love for God’s people, he may take comfort: God does not forget. The author directs the hearers’ eyes first to God’s character and only then to their own fruit.

6:11

And we long for each one of you to show the same earnestness toward the full assurance of the hope until the end,

Confidence about the hearers does not lead the author to relax his appeal; it fuels it. “We long” translates ἐπιθυμοῦμεν epithymoumen, a strong word of desire (it can be used of evil craving, but here, as in Jesus’ words at the Last Supper in Luke 22:15, of earnest longing). The object of his longing is “each one of you” (ἕκαστον ὑμῶν hekaston hymōn). The individual emphasis is typical of the letter’s pastoral concern: “see to it that there not be in any of you an evil heart” (3:12); “exhort one another each day … that none of you be hardened” (3:13); “let us fear lest any of you should seem to have fallen short” (4:1); “see to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God” (12:15). A congregation may as a whole be bearing fruit while particular members are drifting. The author is not satisfied with a good report of the church in general; he wants every one of them to reach the end.

He wants them to show “the same earnestness” (τὴν αὐτὴν … σπουδήν tēn autēn … spoudēn). The verb ἐνδείκνυσθαι endeiknysthai, “to show,” is the same verb used in 6:10 (“the love that you showed”). The same zeal they had shown in loving service is now to be directed toward their hope. σπουδή spoudē means “eagerness, diligence, earnest effort.” Its verb appears in 4:11: “let us therefore be diligent to enter that rest.” It is the opposite of the sluggishness the author deplores (5:11; 6:12).

The goal is “the full assurance of the hope” (τὴν πληροφορίαν τῆς ἐλπίδος tēn plērophorian tēs elpidos). πληροφορία plērophoria means fullness, complete certainty, settled conviction. Hebrews uses it again in 10:22, “let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith,” and Paul speaks of the gospel coming to the Thessalonians “in power and in the Holy Spirit and with full assurance” (1 Thess 1:5; compare Col 2:2). The word “toward” (πρός pros) may mean either “with a view to attaining” full assurance or “in keeping with” it; in either case, diligence and assurance belong together. Hope, in Hebrews, is not a wistful wish but a confident expectation grounded in God’s promise (3:6; 6:18–19; 7:19; 10:23). The author wants his hearers to hold that hope with full conviction.

This verse teaches something important about Christian assurance. Assurance is not obtained by looking back to a past experience alone, however vivid—the people of 6:4–5 had vivid experiences. Nor is it a cold deduction from a doctrine held in the abstract. It grows in the soil of earnest, continuing faithfulness, as the believer goes on trusting God’s promise and bearing the fruit of love. Peter makes the same connection: “be all the more diligent to make your calling and election sure, for if you practice these things you will never stumble” (2 Pet 1:10). The ground of assurance is God’s promise and oath (6:13–18); the experience of full assurance is nourished by diligent perseverance.

Finally, this earnestness is to continue “until the end” (ἄχρι τέλους achri telous). The phrase deliberately recalls 3:14, “if we hold our original confidence firm until the end” (μέχρι τέλους mechri telous). The author is not content with a good beginning. The race is run to the finish (12:1–2). And notice the contrast: the barren land had an “end” (τέλος telos) of burning (6:8); the diligent believer presses on with hope “until the end.” The same word marks two very different destinies.

6:12

so that you may not become sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises.

The purpose of the earnestness just urged is stated negatively and positively. Negatively: ἵνα μὴ νωθροὶ γένησθε hina mē nōthroi genēsthe, “so that you may not become sluggish.” This is the word that opened the exhortation in 5:11, “you have become sluggish (νωθροί nōthroi) in hearing,” and its return here closes the frame. Some have seen a contradiction: in 5:11 they have become sluggish, in 6:12 they must not become sluggish. The best explanation is that in 5:11 the sluggishness is specifically “in hearing”—a dullness in receiving teaching—while here the warning concerns their whole life of faith. Their ears have grown dull; the author is determined that their hope and endurance shall not. Dullness of hearing, left uncorrected, spreads into dullness of living; the cure is fresh attention to the word and fresh diligence in hope. The adjective νωθρός nōthros describes the slowness of a lazy or listless person; Sirach uses it when he warns against being sluggish and slack in one’s deeds (Sir 4:29). It is the spiritual condition in which a person drifts (2:1) without ever consciously deciding to turn away.

Positively: “but imitators” (μιμηταὶ δέ mimētai de). Imitation of worthy examples was central to ancient moral education. Teachers held up the lives of great men to be copied, and the young were trained by learning to walk in the steps of their elders. The New Testament uses the same pattern: Paul urges, “be imitators of me, as I am of Christ” (1 Cor 11:1), and praises the Thessalonians for becoming imitators of the churches in Judea (1 Thess 2:14). Hebrews will give its hearers a whole gallery of examples in chapter 11 and will urge them to “consider the outcome” of their leaders’ way of life and “imitate their faith” (13:7). Here the author introduces the theme that will dominate chapter 11 and, at once, the first great example: Abraham (6:13–15).

The people to be imitated are “those who through faith and patience are inheriting the promises.” Two qualities are named. “Faith” (πίστις pistis) is trust in God’s promise, the conviction of things not seen (11:1). “Patience” (μακροθυμία makrothymia) means long-suffering, patient waiting, the capacity to endure delay without giving up. The pairing is deliberate. Faith without patience flares up and burns out, like the rocky ground that received the word with joy but had no root. Patience without faith becomes mere stoic resignation. Together they make the steady, expectant endurance of those who wait for God. Three verses later the author picks up the second quality with the related verb: Abraham, “having patiently waited” (μακροθυμήσας makrothymēsas), obtained the promise (6:15).

The participle κληρονομούντων klēronomountōn, “inheriting,” is present tense. It may be timeless (“those who inherit”), but it may also hint that the heirs of faith are, even now, in possession of their inheritance—the faithful dead who, as 12:23 will say, are “the spirits of the righteous made perfect.” The language of inheritance links the hearers to the promise of “rest” in chapters 3–4, to the “heirs of the promise” in 6:17, and to the Son, who is “heir of all things” (1:2). The believers’ inheritance is secure because it is held in the Son.

With this verse the exhortation that began at 5:11 is complete. It has moved from rebuke (5:11–14), to resolve (6:1–3), to warning (6:4–8), to reassurance (6:9–10), to appeal (6:11–12). And it ends by pointing to examples whose faith and patience were rewarded—a perfect transition to the certainty of the promise given to Abraham.

Weighing the Warning (6:4–12)

Having walked through the passage verse by verse, we are in a position to weigh the interpretations summarized under 6:6. Several conclusions emerge from the text itself.

1. The warning is real and its consequence is eternal. The author’s “impossible” is absolute (compare 6:18; 10:4; 11:6). The outcome of the barren land is “burning” (6:8), set in contrast to “salvation” (6:9), and the parallel warnings speak of “a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” (10:27) and “destruction” (10:39). Any interpretation that reduces the danger to a loss of rewards, or treats the warning as describing something that could never be a live danger to anyone, fails to do justice to the text. The warning is addressed to a real congregation in which some were drifting and some had stopped meeting (10:25). The author fears for them, and he wants them to fear (4:1).

2. The people described had real and rich experience within the covenant community. The language of 6:4–5 cannot be reduced to mere outward attendance. Those described had received the light of the gospel, had tasted God’s gifts, had shared in the work of the Spirit, had found God’s word good, and had seen his power. They were baptized members of the church, and their experiences were genuine experiences of God’s grace at work among his people. Here View 1 rightly protests against any reading that makes these descriptions empty.

3. Yet the author himself distinguishes these experiences from “things that belong to salvation.” In 6:9 he contrasts the case of 6:4–8 with “better things—things that belong to salvation,” and in 6:10 he identifies those better things as the enduring fruit of love. The parable of 6:7–8 makes the same distinction: the rain is the same; the crop differs. The experiences of 6:4–5 correspond to the rain. Salvation is shown by the crop, borne “with endurance” (Luke 8:15). This fits the wilderness background, where an entire generation saw God’s glory and signs and yet did not enter because of unbelief (3:19). It fits Jesus’ parable of the soils, where the rocky ground receives the word with joy and then falls away (Luke 8:13). It fits Jesus’ sobering words about those who prophesied, cast out demons, and did mighty works in his name, to whom he will say, “I never knew you” (Matt 7:22–23)—not “I once knew you,” but “never.” And it fits Judas, who shared fully in the ministry of the Twelve, yet was “the son of destruction” (John 17:12).

4. Hebrews itself defines true belonging by perseverance. The decisive statements are the author’s own: “we are his house, if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope” (3:6); “we have become sharers of Christ, if we hold our original confidence firm to the end” (3:14). The perfect tense in 3:14 means that holding fast to the end does not make someone a sharer of Christ; it shows that he already is one. The converse is also implied: one who does not hold fast shows that he had not become a sharer of Christ in this saving sense, whatever else he had shared in—including, as 6:4 puts it, sharing in the Holy Spirit’s work among God’s people. John states the same principle in plain words: “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have remained with us. But they went out, so that it might become plain that they all are not of us” (1 John 2:19). And Hebrews concludes the parallel warning of chapter 10 by saying, “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39).

5. God keeps his own, and he keeps them through means such as this warning. The whole of Scripture teaches that those whom God has truly made his own he will keep to the end. Jesus says, “My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. And I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father’s hand” (John 10:27–29). “He who began a good work in you will bring it to completion until the day of Christ Jesus” (Phil 1:6). “Those whom he justified, he also glorified” (Rom 8:30). Hebrews itself insists on this: Christ “is able to save completely those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them” (7:25); “by a single offering he has perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (10:14); and the very next paragraph (6:13–20) grounds the believer’s hope in God’s unchangeable oath. These promises do not cancel the warnings; they work through them. The sheep that hear the Shepherd’s voice hear his warnings too, and they heed them. Paul told the Philippians to “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who is at work in you” (Phil 2:12–13). The warning of Hebrews 6 is one of the instruments by which God keeps his people from the very fall it describes. In that sense, Views 2 and 3 are not rivals but partners: the warning is a real means by which God preserves the elect (View 3), and those who finally fall away despite all such warnings show that, for all their privileges, they were never truly Christ’s (View 2).

6. The warning must not be blunted. It would be a serious misuse of this conclusion to say, “Since true believers cannot fall away, this passage does not apply to me.” No one in the congregation could say that, because no one could look into his own heart and declare himself the good land by inspection alone. The author addresses every hearer as one who must take this warning to heart. The test of the soil is the crop, and the crop is borne over time. The comfort of election is not given to those who are drifting carelessly toward the fire; it is given to those who are fleeing to Christ for refuge (6:18). The proper response to Hebrews 6:4–8 is neither despair nor complacency, but earnest, diligent faith that holds fast to Christ “until the end” (6:11).

7. The warning is not meant to torment the tender conscience. The apostasy described is a deliberate renunciation of the Son of God, a crucifying of him afresh and holding him up to contempt. The believer who fears he has committed it, who grieves over his sin and longs for Christ, is by that very grief and longing showing the marks of one in whom God is still at work. The apostate does not want Christ; he rejects him. To any who fear but still long for the Savior, the rest of Hebrews speaks clearly: “Let us draw near with confidence to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace for help in time of need” (4:16).

In short, the passage teaches that the experience of God’s blessings within the church, however real and rich, does not by itself guarantee final salvation; that those who, after such experience, deliberately and finally reject Christ have no other sacrifice and no path back, because they have spurned the only one; that true believers are recognized by the enduring fruit of faith and love; and that God, who is not unjust to forget that fruit, uses this very warning to bring his people safely to the end. The author holds both truths with full force. So should we.

For Reflection

  1. The author’s remedy for spiritual sluggishness is not to go back over the basics but to press on to a deeper grasp of Christ (6:1). Where in your own life might familiarity with the “elementary word” have become a resting place rather than a foundation to build on?
  2. The verb “let us be carried on” (6:1) is passive, and the author adds “if God permits” (6:3). How does the truth that spiritual growth is God’s work both humble you and encourage you to strive?
  3. The people of 6:4–5 had rich spiritual experiences, yet the author distinguishes these from “things that belong to salvation” (6:9). What does this teach about the difference between religious privilege or experience and saving faith that endures?
  4. In the parable of 6:7–8, the same rain falls on the land; the difference is the crop. What kind of “crop” is God looking for in a life that has received his word, and what does 6:10 suggest it looks like in practice?
  5. How do the warning of 6:4–8 and the reassurance of 6:9–12 work together in the author’s pastoral strategy? How might you speak to someone who is careless about their faith, and to someone who is terrified that they have fallen beyond God’s reach?
  6. The author calls his hearers to imitate “those who through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12). Whom has God placed in your life as an example of faith and patience, and what in their lives is worth imitating?
