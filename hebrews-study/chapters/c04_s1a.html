A Sabbath-Rest Remains for the People of God (4:1–13)

This section began at 3:7.

Translation

1Therefore let us fear lest, while a promise of entering his rest is still left open, any one of you should be found to have fallen short. 2For indeed we have had good news proclaimed to us, just as they also did; but the message they heard did not benefit them, because they were not united by faith with those who listened. 3For we who have believed are entering that rest, just as he has said,

“As I swore in my wrath,
‘They shall not enter my rest,’”

although his works were finished from the foundation of the world. 4For he has spoken somewhere concerning the seventh day in this way:

“And God rested on the seventh day from all his works.”

5And again in this passage:

“They shall not enter my rest.”

6Since therefore it remains for some to enter it, and those who formerly had the good news proclaimed to them did not enter because of disobedience, 7he again appoints a certain day, “Today,” saying through David after so long a time, just as has been said before,

“Today, if you hear his voice,
do not harden your hearts.”

8For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken afterward about another day. 9So then, a Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God; 10for the one who has entered his rest has himself also rested from his works, just as God did from his own. 11Let us therefore strive to enter that rest, so that no one may fall by following the same pattern of disobedience.

12For the word of God is living and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing as far as the division of soul and spirit, of joints and marrow, and able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart. 13And no creature is hidden before him, but all things are naked and laid bare to the eyes of him to whom we must give account.

Setting and Structure

These thirteen verses are the second half of a single sermon on Psalm 95:7–11 that began at 3:7. In 3:7–11 the preacher quoted the psalm in full; in 3:12–19 he drew its first application, warning against “an evil heart of unbelief” and showing from the wilderness story that the generation who came out of Egypt failed to enter God’s rest “because of unbelief” (3:19). Chapter 4 now takes up the positive side of the same psalm. The key move is in the first verse: the promise of entering God’s rest is “still left open.” The failure of the wilderness generation did not close the door; it only proved that the rest had not yet been entered. The burden of 4:1–11 is to prove that claim from Scripture and to press it on the hearers.

The argument proceeds in a way that would have been familiar to any Jewish hearer trained in the synagogue. The preacher links two passages that share a key word. Psalm 95:11 speaks of “my rest” (Greek κατάπαυσις katapausis); Genesis 2:2 says that God “rested” (κατέπαυσεν katepausen) on the seventh day. Later rabbis called this method gezerah shavah, “equal decree”: where the same word appears in two texts, each can shed light on the other. The preacher uses it not as a clever trick but because both texts speak of the same God and the same rest. By laying them side by side he shows that the rest God calls “my rest” is the rest God himself entered when his works of creation were complete. It is therefore older and greater than the land of Canaan.

The flow of thought can be traced in four steps:

  1. Exhortation (4:1–2). Let us fear falling short, since the promise still stands; we, like Israel, have heard good news, and hearing without faith did them no good.
  2. Exposition (4:3–10). Believers are entering the rest (4:3a). The rest is God’s own creation rest (4:3b–5). Since some must still enter it and the first hearers did not, God set another “Today” through David (4:6–7). Joshua therefore did not give the final rest (4:8). So a Sabbath-rest remains (4:9), a rest in which the one who enters ceases from his works as God did from his (4:10).
  3. Exhortation (4:11). Let us strive to enter that rest, so that no one falls as they fell.
  4. Warrant (4:12–13). The reason for urgency: the word of God that speaks “Today” is living and searching, and we must give account to God, before whom nothing is hidden.

Notice the inclusio (a literary “bookend”) that frames the exhortation: “let us fear … lest any one of you should be found to have fallen short” (4:1) and “let us strive … so that no one may fall” (4:11). Between them stands the scriptural proof. The verb “enter” occurs again and again, and the noun “rest” recurs like a drumbeat. The whole passage is about one question: will the hearers enter, or will they fall short?

The historical setting gives these words force. The hearers were second-generation believers (2:3) who had already endured public abuse, imprisonment of some members, and the confiscation of property (10:32–34). They had begun well. Now they were growing “sluggish” (5:11; 6:12), some were neglecting the assembly (10:25), and the pull of the old covenant’s visible securities—temple, priesthood, sacrifices, the respectability of the synagogue—was strong. The wilderness generation was an apt mirror. They too had been redeemed (by the Passover and the sea), had heard God’s voice, and had seen his works for forty years; yet at the very border of the promised land they shrank back in fear of the giants and refused to go in (Num 13–14). The preacher fears the same pattern: a redeemed people who, near the end of their journey, turn back. The parallel is all the sharper if, as seems likely, Hebrews was written in the 60s: by then a full generation had passed since the cross, as a generation had passed in the wilderness.

The idea of “rest” also had deep roots in Jewish hope. In Deuteronomy, “rest” meant secure settlement in the land with enemies subdued (Deut 12:9–10; 25:19). It was linked to the place where God would cause his name to dwell, and Psalm 132 calls Zion God’s own “resting place” (Ps 132:8, 14). The Sabbath itself was understood as a weekly participation in God’s creation rest (Exod 20:11; 31:17), and in later Jewish tradition the Sabbath became a picture of the age to come. Hebrews gathers all these strands—land, temple, Sabbath, and the age to come—and shows that they all point beyond themselves to God’s own rest, now opened to his people through Christ.

Finally, verses 12–13 are not an unrelated hymn to Scripture. They close the exposition of Psalm 95 by reminding the hearers that the psalm is not merely an old text. It is the living voice of God, still speaking “Today,” still searching the heart for the hardness and unbelief that kept Israel out. The God who speaks is the God to whom “we must give account.” With that sobering note the exposition ends, and the preacher turns at once to the provision God has made for weak and tempted people: a great high priest (4:14–16).

Verse by Verse

4:1

Therefore let us fear lest, while a promise of entering his rest is still left open, any one of you should be found to have fallen short.

The word “therefore” (οὖν oun) draws the conclusion from 3:16–19. The wilderness generation did not enter because of unbelief; therefore the present generation must take care not to repeat their failure. The verb is striking: Φοβηθῶμεν phobēthōmen, “let us fear.” It is a hortatory subjunctive, the grammatical form a speaker uses to say “let us …,” and it comes first in the Greek sentence for emphasis. The preacher includes himself. He does not say “you should be afraid,” but “let us fear.”

What kind of fear is meant? Not the panicked terror that makes a person flee from God, but the sober, reverent fear that takes God’s warnings seriously. Scripture often commends such fear. The writer later says that Noah, “moved by godly fear,” built the ark (11:7), and he calls his hearers to worship God “with reverence and awe” because “our God is a consuming fire” (12:28–29). In the same way Paul urges believers to work out their salvation “with fear and trembling,” because God is at work in them (Phil 2:12–13). Healthy fear is not opposed to faith; it is one of faith’s expressions. A person who truly believes that God has sworn to exclude the unbelieving will fear unbelief.

The fear has a reason: “while a promise of entering his rest is still left open.” The Greek participle καταλειπομένης kataleipomenēs (from καταλείπω kataleipō, “leave behind, leave remaining”) is in the present tense, describing a situation that continues: the promise “is being left,” still standing, not yet fulfilled and not withdrawn. This is the great discovery of the chapter. One might have thought that Psalm 95 closed the matter: God swore that the rebels would never enter, and they did not. But the very fact that they did not enter means the promise is still unclaimed. The door remains open. That is good news—and it is also the reason for fear, because an open door can still be refused.

The danger is that “any one of you should be found to have fallen short.” Two words call for comment. First, the preacher says “any one” (τις tis). He is concerned for each individual member of the congregation, just as in 3:12–13 (“lest there be in any of you … that none of you be hardened”) and 12:15 (“that no one fall short of the grace of God”). Pastoral care in Hebrews is personal; no one is to be lost in the crowd.

Second, the verb ὑστερηκέναι hysterēkenai, from ὑστερέω hystereō, means “to come too late, to lag behind, to fall short, to lack.” It pictures a traveler who fails to arrive, or a runner who does not reach the finish line. It is in the perfect tense, which in Greek describes a completed action with a lasting result: not merely stumbling for a moment, but ending up in the state of having fallen short. The same verb returns in 12:15: “see to it that no one fails to obtain [falls short of] the grace of God.”

Interpretive Options: “Should be found to have fallen short”

The verb δοκῇ dokē, from δοκέω dokeō, usually means “seem” or “think,” which raises a question about the exact sense.

  • “Lest anyone even seem to have fallen short.” On this reading the preacher softens his language out of courtesy, or he urges them to avoid even the appearance of falling short. Some older interpreters took it this way.
  • “Lest anyone think he has come too late.” Some modern interpreters suggest the danger is a false conclusion: that the promise expired with the wilderness generation, so it is too late to enter. The preacher would then be correcting discouragement.
  • “Lest anyone be found (judged) to have fallen short.” In Greek of this period dokeō can carry a semi-legal sense, “to be judged, to be reckoned,” describing how a person is assessed by a judge. The danger is that on the day of reckoning someone will be found to have failed to arrive.

The third view best fits the context. The passage ends with the God “to whom we must give account” (4:13), whose eyes see everything. The “seeming” in question is the verdict of the divine Judge. The second view has some appeal, since the whole point of 4:1–10 is that the promise is still open; but the main thrust of the verse is warning, not reassurance, and its parallel in 4:11 (“so that no one may fall”) speaks of real failure, not a mistaken opinion.

The phrase “his rest” refers back to Psalm 95:11, “my rest,” quoted in 3:11 and 3:18. Already the preacher hints at what he will prove in 4:3–5: this is not merely a rest that God gives, like land or freedom from enemies, but the rest that belongs to God himself.

4:2

For indeed we have had good news proclaimed to us, just as they also did; but the message they heard did not benefit them, because they were not united by faith with those who listened.

This verse explains why the fear of 4:1 is appropriate: our situation really is like Israel’s. The Greek καὶ γάρ ἐσμεν εὐηγγελισμένοι kai gar esmen euēngelismenoi is literally “for indeed we are people who have been evangelized.” The verb εὐαγγελίζω euangelizō means “to bring good news,” and it is the root of the English word “evangelize.” The form here is a perfect passive participle with the verb “to be,” a construction that stresses an abiding state: we stand as people to whom good news has been announced.

Remarkably, the preacher says that Israel in the wilderness also received good news: “just as they also did” (καθάπερ κἀκεῖνοι kathaper kakeinoi). What good news was that? It was the promise of the land, “a good land, a land flowing with milk and honey” (Exod 3:8; Num 14:7–8). When the spies returned, Joshua and Caleb announced that “the land is exceedingly good” and that the Lord would bring them in (Num 14:7–8). That was a gospel in the root sense: a message of God’s gracious promise, to be received by trust. The New Testament gospel is greater, for it announces Christ and the heavenly rest; but the structure is the same. God speaks a promise, and the promise must be believed.

The tragedy is stated plainly: “the message they heard did not benefit them.” The phrase ὁ λόγος τῆς ἀκοῆς ho logos tēs akoēs is literally “the word of hearing,” a Hebrew-style expression meaning “the message that was heard” or “the message proclaimed.” The noun ἀκοή akoē can mean both the act of hearing and the report that is heard. Paul uses it the same way in Romans 10:16–17, quoting Isaiah 53:1: “Lord, who has believed our report?” and concluding, “So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ.” In 1 Thessalonians 2:13 Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians received “the word of hearing” not as a human word but as the word of God, which is at work in those who believe. Hearing alone is not enough. A message can be heard with the ears and yet bring no benefit, because it is not received with faith.

Why did it not benefit them? Because, in the reading printed in the main text, “they were not united by faith with those who listened.” The participle συγκεκερασμένους synkekerasmenous comes from συγκεράννυμι synkerannymi, a verb meaning “to mix together, blend, unite,” used of mixing wine with water or of blending ingredients. Paul uses it of God “composing” or “blending together” the members of the body (1 Cor 12:24). The picture is of a people who should have been joined into one believing community but were not. The rebels did not join themselves by faith to “those who listened”—that is, to Joshua and Caleb, the two who heard God’s promise with faith and urged the people to go up (Num 14:6–9, 24, 30). The majority stood apart from the faithful few, and so the word they all heard did them no good.

Textual Note: “Not united” or “not mixed”?

This verse contains one of the most discussed textual variants in Hebrews. The difference is a single letter in Greek, but it changes the sense of the sentence.

  • συγκεκερασμένους synkekerasmenous (accusative plural). The participle describes “them” (ἐκείνους ekeinous), the wilderness generation: “the word did not benefit them, since they were not united by faith with those who heard.” This is the reading of the great majority of early witnesses, including the oldest papyrus of Paul’s letters and Hebrews (P46) and the major uncial manuscripts Alexandrinus, Vaticanus, and Ephraemi. It is the reading of the SBL Greek New Testament and the Nestle-Aland text.
  • συγκεκερασμένος synkekerasmenos (nominative singular). The participle then describes “the word”: “the word did not benefit them, since it was not mixed with faith in those who heard.” This reading appears in Codex Sinaiticus, some early versions, and the later Byzantine tradition, and it lies behind the familiar rendering of older English Bibles.

Bruce Metzger’s Textual Commentary explains that the editorial committee adopted the plural chiefly because of its strong and early attestation. The singular gives a smoother sense, and scribes were much more likely to change the difficult plural into the easier singular than the reverse. The plural reading also fits the context well: the wilderness story turns precisely on the contrast between the unbelieving majority and the faithful two, Joshua and Caleb, who “followed the Lord fully” (Num 14:24; 32:12). Either way, the theological point is the same and is beyond dispute: the word of God brings no benefit when it is not met by faith.

The lesson for the hearers is pointed. They too are a community. The way to be “united by faith” with those who listen is to belong, heart and life, to the believing people of God, encouraging one another daily (3:13) and not forsaking the assembly (10:25). Hebrews 11 will later present a long “cloud of witnesses” who heard God’s word with faith. The call is to join them, not the wilderness majority.
