Hebrews 1

God Has Spoken in His Son

Most New Testament letters begin by naming the writer and the readers and offering a greeting: “Paul, an apostle … to the church of God … grace to you and peace.” Hebrews does none of this. There is no name, no address, no thanksgiving, no prayer report. The first word is an adverb, Πολυμερῶς Polymerōs, “In many portions,” and the first subject is God. The listener is placed at once in the presence of the God who speaks. This is the opening of a sermon rather than of a letter, and it begins the way the best ancient orators began their weightiest speeches: with a long, carefully balanced “periodic” sentence (1:1–4), rich in sound and rhythm, in which subordinate clauses build toward and then unfold from a single main statement. In the Greek, verses 1–4 are one sentence, and its main clause is short and decisive: God … has spoken to us in a Son. Everything else in the chapter, and in a real sense everything else in the book, unpacks that claim.

Chapter 1 falls into two movements. The first (1:1–4) is the overture. It sets the earlier word of God “in the prophets” beside his final word “in a Son,” and then describes the Son in seven great strokes: he is heir of all things, agent of creation, radiance of God’s glory, exact imprint of God’s being, sustainer of the universe, the one who made purification for sins, and the one now seated at God’s right hand. It closes by stating that he has become “better than the angels” and has inherited a more excellent name. That last phrase introduces the second movement (1:5–14), a chain (Latin catena) of seven Old Testament quotations that proves the Son’s superiority to the angels from Israel’s own Scriptures. The chain is framed by the same rhetorical question at its beginning and its end: “To which of the angels did he ever say …?” (1:5, 13). The answer, never stated because it does not need to be, is: to none of them.

Three things in particular are worth watching for as you read.

First, God speaks. The verb λαλέω laleō, “speak,” opens the book (1:1–2) and returns at decisive moments (2:2–3; 12:25). In 1:5–13 the quotations are introduced, not as what “Scripture says” or what “David wrote,” but as what God himself “said” and “says” to and about his Son. For this author the Old Testament is the living voice of God, and the Son is both the subject of that voice and its final form. This prepares for the warning that follows immediately in 2:1–4: if God has spoken this way, we must not drift away from what we have heard.

Second, “Son.” The title υἱός huios, “Son,” dominates the chapter (1:2, 5, 8) and is the “name” of 1:4. Hebrews holds together two things that can seem to pull apart: the Son is eternal, the one through whom God made the ages and who bears all things (1:2–3), and he is also the one whom God, at a particular moment, appointed, enthroned, and declared to be Son (1:2, 5, 13). Both are essential to the book’s argument. Only one who is truly God can reveal God finally and save completely; only one who has truly entered human history, suffered, and been exalted can be our high priest. Chapter 1 stresses the first; chapter 2 will stress the second.

Third, the angels. To a modern reader it may seem odd that the first sustained argument of Hebrews is that Jesus is greater than the angels. To first-century Jews it would not. Angels were believed to have been present at Sinai and to have mediated the giving of the law (Deut 33:2; Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19), and Hebrews itself calls the law “the word spoken through angels” (2:2). Comparing the Son with the angels is therefore a way of comparing the new revelation with the old, and the new covenant with the Sinai covenant. That comparison will run through the whole book. Hebrews’ favorite word, κρείττων kreittōn, “better,” appears first in 1:4.

Finally, notice which psalm holds the chapter together. Psalm 110:1, “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet,” is echoed in 1:3 (“he sat down at the right hand”) and quoted in 1:13, forming a frame around the whole chapter. Psalm 110 is the backbone of Hebrews; its fourth verse, “You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek,” will become the center of the book’s teaching in chapters 5–7. The Son who is enthroned as King in chapter 1 will be revealed as the eternal Priest who made purification for sins (1:3) and now intercedes for his people.
