Final Greetings (13:22–25)

Translation

22Now I urge you, brothers, bear with the word of exhortation, for indeed I have written to you briefly. 23Know that our brother Timothy has been released; if he comes soon, I will see you with him. 24Greet all your leaders and all the saints. Those from Italy greet you. 25Grace be with all of you.

Setting and Structure

Hebrews began without any of the usual features of an ancient letter. There was no name of the sender, no named recipients, and no greeting, only the majestic opening sentence, “God, having spoken long ago …” (1:1). It ends, however, like a letter. Ancient letters commonly closed with personal requests, news of travel plans, greetings to and from various people, and a short farewell wish. Paul’s letters typically end with greetings and a grace-benediction (for example, Rom 16:1–24; 1 Cor 16:19–24; Col 4:7–18). Hebrews 13:22–25 follows the same pattern.

  • 13:22: A final request about the sermon itself: “bear with the word of exhortation.”
  • 13:23: News and travel plans: Timothy’s release and the author’s hope to visit.
  • 13:24: Greetings to and from others.
  • 13:25: The closing grace-wish.

This short ending tells us more about the situation of Hebrews than any other part of the book. We learn that the author knows the readers personally and hopes to visit them (compare 13:19). He belongs to the circle of Timothy, Paul’s close companion. The congregation has leaders, and the letter seems addressed to a group within a larger church, since the readers are asked to greet “all” the leaders and “all” the saints. People described as “those from Italy” send greetings. The author also describes the whole work in a phrase that names its genre: “the word of exhortation.”

Some older critics suggested that these verses were added by a later hand to make the anonymous sermon look like a letter of Paul. As the chapter introduction noted, the vocabulary and themes of chapter 13 are bound up with the rest of the book. These final verses in particular use the language of the sermon. “Exhortation” (13:22) recalls 3:13, 10:25, and 12:5. “Brothers” recalls 3:1, 12 and 10:19. “Leaders” recalls 13:7 and 17. “Grace” recalls 4:16, 12:15, 12:28, and 13:9. Moreover, a forger seeking to make the work look Pauline would surely have named Paul as the author. Instead, the author never names himself. The most natural explanation is the simplest: the preacher wrote his sermon to a congregation he knew, and added a short personal note at the end, as one would when sending a sermon by letter.

These verses also give the sermon a human face. After the high theology of the heavenly sanctuary, the readers hear about a friend just released, a hoped-for visit, and greetings from people far away. The author who spoke of the great cloud of witnesses (12:1) was himself part of a real network of believers who knew one another’s names, prayed for one another, and traveled at risk to strengthen one another. The church of the living God is not an abstraction. It is a fellowship of brothers and sisters bound by the grace of God.

Verse by Verse

13:22

Now I urge you, brothers, bear with the word of exhortation, for indeed I have written to you briefly.

Παρακαλῶ δὲ ὑμᾶς, ἀδελφοί Parakalō de hymas, adelphoi, “Now I urge you, brothers.” The verb parakalō was used in 13:19 (“I urge you … to do this”) and now introduces the author’s last request. The address “brothers” (ἀδελφοί adelphoi) is family language. It includes the women of the congregation as well as the men, as the plural regularly does in Greek. It reminds the readers that the author has spoken to them throughout, even in his sternest warnings, as a brother. He addressed them in the same way at two great turning points of the sermon (3:1, 12; 10:19).

The request is ἀνέχεσθε τοῦ λόγου τῆς παρακλήσεως anechesthe tou logou tēs paraklēseōs, “bear with the word of exhortation.” The verb ἀνέχομαι anechomai means “bear with, put up with, endure, accept patiently.” Paul uses it for patiently bearing with one another (Eph 4:2; Col 3:13). He uses it ironically when he asks the Corinthians to “bear with” a little of his foolishness (2 Cor 11:1). And he warns that a time will come when people “will not endure sound teaching” (2 Tim 4:3). The author knows that his sermon has contained hard words. He has called his readers “sluggish” (5:11), warned them of judgment (6:4–8; 10:26–31; 12:25–29), and told them that they need to be taught the basics again (5:12). He asks them not to resent his message, but to receive it in the love in which it was given. There is gentleness in this request. The preacher who spoke of God’s discipline as a sign of a father’s love (12:5–11) wants his own hard words to be received in the same spirit.

Word Study: λόγος τῆς παρακλήσεως logos tēs paraklēseōs, “word of exhortation”

παράκλησις paraklēsis comes from para, “beside,” and kaleō, “call.” It means “a calling alongside.” It can be exhortation, urging someone to act, or encouragement, comforting and strengthening someone. Often it is both. In Hebrews the word appears in 6:18, where believers have “strong encouragement” to hold fast to their hope, and in 12:5, where the readers are said to have forgotten “the exhortation that addresses you as sons” in Proverbs 3:11–12. The related verb appears in 3:13 (“exhort one another every day”) and 10:25 (“exhorting one another”). The whole sermon is a paraklēsis, a word that both warns and encourages.

The full phrase “word of exhortation” occurs in only one other place in the New Testament. In the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, after the reading of the Law and the Prophets, the synagogue rulers sent word to Paul and Barnabas: “Brothers, if you have any word of exhortation (λόγος παρακλήσεως logos paraklēseōs) for the people, say it” (Acts 13:15). Paul then rose and preached a sermon. It reviewed Israel’s history and the promises of God, quoted Psalms and Prophets, proclaimed Jesus’ death and resurrection, and ended with a warning from Habakkuk (Acts 13:16–41). That sermon resembles Hebrews in many ways. It uses Scripture to proclaim Christ and calls for a response, with warning. The phrase appears to have been a recognized term for a sermon based on Scripture, delivered in a synagogue or Christian assembly. When the author calls his work “the word of exhortation,” he is telling us what kind of work it is. It is a sermon. It was meant to be read aloud to the gathered congregation and to move them to faithful perseverance. This is how the whole of Hebrews should be read: not as a theological treatise only, but as preaching.

The reason is καὶ γὰρ διὰ βραχέων ἐπέστειλα ὑμῖν kai gar dia bracheōn epesteila hymin, “for indeed I have written to you briefly.” διὰ βραχέων dia bracheōn, literally “through few [words],” means “briefly, in a few words.” Peter ends his first letter in a similar way: “I have written to you briefly” (1 Pet 5:12, using a different expression). ἐπέστειλα epesteila, from epistellō, “send a letter, write,” is the verb from which “epistle” comes. The aorist is probably an “epistolary aorist,” in which the writer describes his writing from the viewpoint of the readers who will receive it: “I have written.”

To modern readers Hebrews may not seem brief. It is one of the longer letters in the New Testament. But the author is comparing what he wrote with what he could have said. Several times he has told his readers that he is holding back. Of the furnishings of the tabernacle he said, “of these things we cannot now speak in detail” (9:5). Of the heroes of faith he said, “time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson …” (11:32). Of Melchizedek he said, “about this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain” (5:11). The subject is inexhaustible. Read aloud, Hebrews takes something under an hour, which is not long for a sermon on such themes. The comment is also a courteous apology, common in ancient letters: “I have not taken too much of your time.” It strengthens the request to “bear with” him. The readers are asked to receive a short word on a great subject.

13:23

Know that our brother Timothy has been released; if he comes soon, I will see you with him.

γινώσκετε τὸν ἀδελφὸν ἡμῶν Τιμόθεον ἀπολελυμένον ginōskete ton adelphon hēmōn Timotheon apolelymenon, “Know that our brother Timothy has been released.” The verb ginōskete may be read as an indicative (“you know”) or an imperative (“know”). Most interpreters take it as an imperative introducing news: “I want you to know.” This is the only person besides Jesus and the Old Testament figures who is named in the whole book.

The description “our brother Timothy” is the same as Paul’s way of naming him in the openings of several letters (2 Cor 1:1; Col 1:1; Phlm 1; compare 1 Thess 3:2). Timothy was Paul’s closest companion. The son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, he joined Paul at Lystra (Acts 16:1–3) and was Paul’s “true child in the faith” (1 Tim 1:2). Paul sent him on many missions (1 Cor 4:17; Phil 2:19–23; 1 Thess 3:2, 6). Near the end of his life, Paul, imprisoned in Rome, urged Timothy to “come to me soon” (2 Tim 4:9, 21). The mention of Timothy places the author of Hebrews firmly within the circle of Paul’s associates. This is one reason the early church came to associate Hebrews with Paul. But it does not prove that Paul wrote it. The author’s style, his method of argument, and his statement that he received the gospel from those who heard the Lord (2:3) all point to someone other than Paul, someone who belonged to the same missionary network.

Word Study: ἀπολελυμένον apolelymenon, “released”

The verb ἀπολύω apolyō has a range of meanings: “release, set free, send away, dismiss.” In Acts it is often used of releasing prisoners. The council “released” Peter and John after threatening them (Acts 4:21, 23). The magistrates at Philippi sent word to “release” Paul and Silas (Acts 16:35–36). Pilate wished to “release” Jesus (Luke 23:16; John 19:10, 12). But the verb can also mean “send off” on a journey. Barnabas and Saul were “sent off” from Antioch (Acts 13:3), and the delegates were “sent off” to Antioch with the apostles’ letter (Acts 15:30). The form here is a perfect passive participle, “having been released,” describing a completed action whose result continues. Timothy is now free.

Most interpreters take the word to mean that Timothy had been in prison and has now been set free. This is the most natural meaning when a person’s release is reported as news of interest to a congregation that had been told to “remember the prisoners” (13:3). The New Testament does not record an imprisonment of Timothy elsewhere. If he answered Paul’s summons to Rome (2 Tim 4:9, 21), he may have been arrested there, but this is a reasonable guess, not a known fact. The alternative meaning, that Timothy had been “sent off” on a mission, is possible, but then we would expect a mention of where he was sent. Either way, the readers know Timothy and are glad to hear of him.

The author adds his plan: μεθ’ οὗ ἐὰν τάχιον ἔρχηται ὄψομαι ὑμᾶς meth’ hou ean tachion erchētai opsomai hymas, “with whom, if he comes soon, I will see you.” The author and Timothy are in different places. The author is waiting for Timothy to come to him, and then the two will travel together to the readers. If Timothy is delayed, the author may have to come alone. The word tachion, “soon” or “sooner,” is the same as in 13:19: the author hopes to be restored to them “the sooner.” This plan shows that the author himself was free to travel, so he was apparently not in prison when he wrote.

The verse shows the realism of early Christian life. Believers were imprisoned and released. Travel plans depended on events beyond their control. The author does not know exactly when he will come. He knows only that he hopes to come, and he has asked the readers to pray that he may come soon (13:19). As James says, “you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that’” (Jas 4:15).
