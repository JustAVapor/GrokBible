Appendix A

The Old Testament in Hebrews

No book of the New Testament is more saturated with the Old Testament than Hebrews. Its argument moves from text to text: it quotes the Scriptures of Israel more than thirty-five times (the exact number depends on how one counts repeated and combined citations), alludes to them on nearly every page, and builds whole chapters as sermons on a single passage. To read Hebrews well is to read it with the Old Testament open beside it. This appendix gathers every direct quotation in the order in which it appears in Hebrews, sets the author’s Greek beside the Masoretic Hebrew, and then lists the most important allusions.

God speaking now. The most striking feature of the way Hebrews cites Scripture is that it almost never says “it is written” and only rarely names a human author (“in David,” 4:7; “someone has testified somewhere,” 2:6). Instead, the Scriptures are the voice of God himself, and the verbs are frequently present tense: God “says” (λέγει legei, 1:6, 7; 5:6; 8:8); “the Holy Spirit says” (3:7) and “testifies to us” (10:15); the exhortation of Proverbs “addresses you as sons” (12:5). Some texts are heard as words spoken by the Father to the Son (1:5, 8–13; 5:5–6), others as the words of the Son himself, addressed to the Father (2:12–13; 10:5–7). For the author, Scripture is not a record of what God once said but the living address of the God who “has spoken” (1:1–2) and whose word is “living and active” (4:12). That is why the “today” of Psalm 95 can confront the congregation directly (3:7–4:11): the Spirit still speaks it.

The Septuagint. Hebrews was written in polished Greek to a Greek-speaking church, and its quotations come from the Septuagint (abbreviated LXX), the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made by Jewish translators in the last centuries before Christ and used throughout the Greek-speaking synagogue and the early church. Usually the LXX renders the Hebrew faithfully, and Hebrews follows it closely, sometimes word for word. In a few places the Greek differs from the Masoretic Hebrew (abbreviated MT, the standard Hebrew text preserved by medieval Jewish scribes): Psalm 8 has “angels” where the Hebrew has elohim (2:7); Psalm 40 has “a body you prepared for me” where the Hebrew has “ears you have dug for me” (10:5); Genesis 47:31 has “staff” where the Masoretes read “bed” (11:21); Habakkuk 2:3–4 is worded differently in Greek (10:37–38). These differences are explained in the notes below. In each case the reader can see that the Greek rendering, though not a mechanical reproduction of the Hebrew, expresses something genuinely present in the Hebrew passage, and that the author’s theological point is consistent with the Hebrew sense. Occasionally the author also adapts the LXX wording himself — abbreviating, changing a person or a word order, or adding a word for emphasis — as preachers in his day commonly did.

Versification. The Hebrew Psalter counts the superscription of many psalms as verse 1, so English Ps 40:6 is Hebrew 40:7; the LXX also numbers most psalms one lower than the Hebrew and English (English Ps 110 = LXX Ps 109). Jeremiah 31 in Hebrew and English is Jeremiah 38 in the LXX. The table gives English references first, with the Hebrew (“Heb.”) and LXX numbers where they differ.

The texts that structure the sermon. Several passages do more than support a point; they supply the backbone of the argument:

  • Psalm 110 is the controlling text of the whole book. Verse 1 (“Sit at my right hand”) is echoed in the opening sentence (1:3), quoted as the climax of the catena of chapter 1 (1:13), and recalled at each turning point (8:1; 10:12–13; 12:2). Verse 4 (“You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”) is introduced at 5:6 and expounded phrase by phrase in chapter 7.
  • Psalm 95:7–11 is quoted in 3:7–11 and expounded in 3:12–4:11: the wilderness generation, the “today” of God’s voice, the oath that excluded them, and the “rest” that still remains for the people of God.
  • Jeremiah 31:31–34, the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament (8:8–12), announces the new covenant; its re-quotation in 10:16–17 frames the central exposition of chapters 8–10 as a pair of bookends.
  • Psalm 40:6–8 (10:5–7) gives the Son’s own words on entering the world: the will of God is done not by animal sacrifice but by the obedient offering of his body.
  • Habakkuk 2:3–4 (10:37–38), “my righteous one will live by faith,” is the text on which the gallery of faith in chapter 11 is a sermon.
  • Proverbs 3:11–12 (12:5–6) interprets the congregation’s sufferings as the loving discipline of a Father.
  • Haggai 2:6 (12:26) announces the final shaking of heaven and earth, leaving only the unshakable kingdom (12:27–28).

Behind the author’s method lies a conviction he shares with the apostles: the Scriptures of Israel speak of Christ (Luke 24:27, 44), and the realities they foreshadowed — Sabbath rest, priesthood, covenant, sanctuary, sacrifice, Zion — find their fulfillment in him. Hebrews does not wrench texts from their settings; again and again its argument depends on reading a passage in its own context (for example, that Psalm 95 was spoken long after Joshua entered the land, 4:7–8, or that Psalm 110 promised a new priesthood long after the law of Moses, 7:11, 28).

Direct Quotations in Hebrews

Quotations are translated from the Greek of Hebrews. In the notes, “follows the LXX” means that Hebrews agrees with the Septuagint as commonly printed, allowing for minor differences; Hebrew is cited from the Masoretic Text (Westminster Leningrad Codex).

1:5a Ps 2:7 “You are my Son; today I have begotten you.” Verbatim LXX, which renders the Hebrew exactly: בְּנִי אַתָּה אֲנִי הַיּוֹם יְלִדְתִּיךָ beni attah ani hayyom yelidtikha, “You are my son; I today have begotten you.” A royal decree spoken at the enthronement of David’s son; quoted again at 5:5 (cf. Acts 13:33).
1:5b 2 Sam 7:14; 1 Chr 17:13 “I will be to him a father, and he will be to me a son.” Verbatim LXX. The Greek εἰς πατέρα … εἰς υἱόν eis patera … eis huion, literally “for a father … for a son,” reproduces the Hebrew idiom אֲנִי אֶהְיֶה־לּוֹ לְאָב וְהוּא יִהְיֶה־לִּי לְבֵן ani ehyeh-lo le’av vehu yihyeh-li leven. The promise of the Davidic covenant; Hebrews stops before 2 Samuel’s clause about chastening the king’s iniquity, which 1 Chr 17:13 also lacks.
1:6 Deut 32:43 LXX (cf. Ps 97:7) “And let all God’s angels worship him.” The line is not in the MT of Deut 32:43, which reads הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ harninu goyim ammo, “Rejoice, O nations, [with] his people.” The LXX Song of Moses has a longer text that includes it, and a Hebrew manuscript of Deuteronomy from Qumran (4QDeutq) also has a longer form calling on heavenly beings (elohim) to bow down to him — evidence that the line goes back to a Hebrew original rather than being a Greek addition. Hebrews’ exact wording matches the form of the Song preserved among the Odes appended to the Greek Psalter. Compare Ps 97:7 (LXX 96:7), where הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ־לוֹ כָּל־אֱלֹהִים hishtachavu-lo kol-elohim, “bow down to him, all gods,” is rendered “worship him, all his angels.”
1:7 Ps 104:4 (LXX 103:4) “He makes his angels winds, and his ministers a flame of fire.” Follows the LXX, except that Hebrews has “a flame of fire” (πυρὸς φλόγα pyros phloga) where most LXX witnesses read “flaming fire.” Hebrew: עֹשֶׂה מַלְאָכָיו רוּחוֹת מְשָׁרְתָיו אֵשׁ לֹהֵט oseh mal’akhav ruchot mesharetav esh lohet, “making his messengers winds, his ministers flaming fire.” Mal’akh means both “messenger” and “angel,” and ruach both “wind” and “spirit.” In the psalm’s context (104:3, “he makes the clouds his chariot”) the Hebrew is often read “he makes winds his messengers”; the Greek, by putting the article on “angels,” makes the angels the object. Either way the point Hebrews draws is sound: angels are creatures and servants, whereas the Son reigns on an eternal throne (1:8).
1:8–9 Ps 45:6–7 (Heb. 45:7–8; LXX 44:7–8) “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, and the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated lawlessness; therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your partners.” Follows the LXX with minor differences of article and word order. The LXX faithfully renders the Hebrew כִּסְאֲךָ אֱלֹהִים עוֹלָם וָעֶד kis’akha elohim olam va’ed, “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever,” in which elohim is most naturally a vocative address to the king, as the Greek ὁ θεός ho theos (nominative used for direct address) is in Hebrews. Hebrew רֶשַׁע resha, “wickedness,” appears as “lawlessness”; מֵחֲבֵרֶיךָ mechaverekha, “above your companions,” as “beyond your partners” (μετόχους metochous), a word Hebrews later uses of believers (3:1, 14).
1:10–12 Ps 102:25–27 (Heb. 102:26–28; LXX 101:26–28) “You, Lord, in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the works of your hands. They will perish, but you remain; they will all grow old like a garment, and like a cloak you will roll them up; like a garment they will also be changed. But you are the same, and your years will not come to an end.” Follows the LXX, which adds the address “Lord” (κύριε kyrie); the Hebrew psalm addresses Lord (YHWH) throughout (102:1, 12) but lacks the vocative here. Hebrew לְפָנִים lefanim, “of old,” is rendered “in the beginning,” recalling Gen 1:1. Hebrew כַּלְּבוּשׁ תַּחֲלִיפֵם kallevush tachalifem means “like clothing you will change them”; Hebrews reads “you will roll them up” (ἑλίξεις helixeis, as some LXX manuscripts do; cf. Isa 34:4) and repeats “like a garment.” Hebrew וְאַתָּה־הוּא ve’attah-hu, “but you are he,” becomes “you are the same.” Hebrews applies to the Son words addressed to YHWH as Creator.
1:13 Ps 110:1 (LXX 109:1) “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.” Verbatim LXX, faithfully rendering the Hebrew שֵׁב לִימִינִי עַד־אָשִׁית אֹיְבֶיךָ הֲדֹם לְרַגְלֶיךָ shev limini ad-ashit oyevekha hadom leraglekha. The psalm opens נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי ne’um YHWH ladoni, “the utterance of the Lord to my lord” — a word spoken by God to David’s Lord, which no angel ever received (1:13). Echoed at 1:3; 8:1; 10:12–13; 12:2.
2:6–8 Ps 8:4–6 (Heb. and LXX 8:5–7) “What is man, that you remember him, or the son of man, that you care for him? You made him for a little while lower than the angels; you crowned him with glory and honor; you put all things in subjection under his feet.” Follows the LXX. Hebrew וַתְּחַסְּרֵהוּ מְּעַט מֵאֱלֹהִים vattechasserehu me’at me’elohim, “you made him lack a little of God” (or “of divine beings”); the LXX renders elohim “angels,” as it does elsewhere when heavenly beings are in view. Hebrew me’at, “a little,” refers chiefly to degree; the Greek βραχύ τι brachy ti can mean “a little” in degree or “for a little while” in time, and Hebrews 2:9 draws out the temporal sense in Jesus’ humiliation. Hebrew תִפְקְדֶנּוּ tifqedennu, “you attend to him,” is “you care for (visit) him.” The best manuscripts of Hebrews omit Ps 8:6a, “you set him over the works of your hands.”
2:12 Ps 22:22 (Heb. 22:23; LXX 21:23) “I will proclaim your name to my brothers; in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise.” Follows the LXX except for “I will proclaim” (ἀπαγγελῶ apangelō) where the LXX has “I will recount” for Hebrew אֲסַפְּרָה asapperah. Hebrew קָהָל qahal, “assembly,” is ἐκκλησία ekklēsia, the word for “church.” The words of the forsaken sufferer of Ps 22:1, after his deliverance, are heard as the risen Christ’s words to his brothers.
2:13a Isa 8:17 (cf. 2 Sam 22:3; Isa 12:2) “I will put my trust in him.” Agrees with the LXX of Isa 8:17, “I will be trusting in him,” with an emphatic “I” added. The Hebrew is וְקִוֵּיתִי־לוֹ veqivviti-lo, “and I will wait (hope) for him,” parallel to “I will wait for the Lord.” The same Greek words occur in the LXX of 2 Sam 22:3 and Isa 12:2 (Hebrew אֶבְטַח evtach, “I will trust”), but the next quotation, from Isa 8:18, shows that Isaiah 8 is in view: the prophet who waits on God amid a faithless nation speaks for the Messiah.
2:13b Isa 8:18 “Behold, I and the children whom God has given me.” Verbatim LXX, stopping before “for signs and portents.” Hebrew הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי וְהַיְלָדִים אֲשֶׁר נָתַן־לִי יְהוָה hinneh anokhi vehayeladim asher natan-li YHWH, “Here am I and the children whom the Lord has given me” — Isaiah and his sons, whose names were signs to Israel. The LXX has “God” for YHWH. Hebrews hears Christ presenting the family God has given him (cf. John 17:6).
3:2, 5 Num 12:7 (allusion) “…faithful in all his house … as a servant…” A near quotation woven into the argument. LXX Num 12:7: “my servant Moses … he is faithful in all my house.” Hebrew עַבְדִּי מֹשֶׁה בְּכָל־בֵּיתִי נֶאֱמָן הוּא avdi Mosheh bekhol-beti ne’eman hu, “my servant Moses; in all my house he is trusted.” The LXX word for “servant,” θεράπων therapōn, denotes an honored attendant; Hebrew ne’eman means both “faithful” and “entrusted,” the background of Hebrews’ “faithful” (πιστός pistos). Moses is faithful in the house as servant; Christ is faithful over it as Son (3:6).
3:7–11 Ps 95:7b–11 (LXX 94:7b–11) “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, on the day of testing in the wilderness, where your fathers put me to the test by scrutinizing me, and saw my works for forty years. Therefore I was provoked with this generation, and I said, ‘They always go astray in their heart, and they have not known my ways.’ As I swore in my wrath, ‘They shall not enter my rest.’” Follows the LXX, which turns the Hebrew place names into common nouns: כִּמְרִיבָה כְּיוֹם מַסָּה kimrivah keyom massah, “as at Meribah [‘quarreling’], as on the day of Massah [‘testing’],” becomes “as in the rebellion (παραπικρασμός parapikrasmos), on the day of testing (πειρασμός peirasmos).” Hebrews makes small changes of its own: “by scrutiny” for the LXX verb “they tried”; “this generation” for “that generation”; and an inserted “therefore,” so that “forty years” goes with “saw my works” (Hebrew and LXX join it to “I loathed/was provoked”; Hebrews uses that link too, 3:17). “They shall not enter” renders a Hebrew oath formula, אִם־יְבֹאוּן אֶל־מְנוּחָתִי im-yevo’un el-menuchati, literally “If they enter my rest — !” (with a self-curse left unspoken), reproduced word for word in Greek (εἰ εἰσελεύσονται ei eiseleusontai). Hebrew menuchah, “resting place,” becomes κατάπαυσις katapausis, “rest.”
3:15 Ps 95:7b–8a “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion.” Repeated from 3:7–8 as the text for the questions of 3:16–18, which identify the rebels from Numbers 14.
4:3, 5 Ps 95:11 “As I swore in my wrath, ‘They shall not enter my rest.’” (4:5: “They shall not enter my rest.”) As at 3:11. The Hebrew menuchah was used of the land as Israel’s resting place (אֶל־הַמְּנוּחָה וְאֶל־הַנַּחֲלָה el-hammenuchah ve’el-hannachalah, “to the rest and the inheritance,” Deut 12:9). By the shared Greek word katapausis Hebrews links “my rest” to God’s own rest on the seventh day (Gen 2:2), a recognized Jewish method of interpreting one text by another that shares its key word.
4:4 Gen 2:2 “And God rested on the seventh day from all his works.” Follows the LXX, adding “God” as the explicit subject. Hebrew וַיִּשְׁבֹּת בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי מִכָּל־מְלַאכְתּוֹ vayyishbot bayyom hashshevi’i mikkol-melakhto, “and he ceased on the seventh day from all his work”; the verb shavat lies behind “Sabbath.” The LXX verb κατέπαυσεν katepausen, “rested,” supplies the verbal link to Ps 95, and its plural “works” (Hebrew singular) is followed by Hebrews (4:3, 10).
4:7 Ps 95:7b–8a “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” Introduced as spoken “in David.” The LXX title of Psalm 94 ascribes it to David (the Hebrew psalm has no title); “in David” may also simply mean “in the Psalter.” The argument turns on the timing: God set another “today” through David long after Joshua had brought Israel into the land (4:8).
5:5 Ps 2:7 “You are my Son; today I have begotten you.” As at 1:5. Here it is paired with Ps 110:4 (5:6): the one God declared his Son is the one he appointed priest. Christ did not glorify himself; he was called by God.
5:6 Ps 110:4 (LXX 109:4) “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” Verbatim LXX. Hebrew אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם עַל־דִּבְרָתִי מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק attah-khohen le’olam al-divrati Malki-tsedeq, “You are a priest forever after the manner of Melchizedek.” The rare Hebrew divrah (“manner, account”) is rendered τάξις taxis, “order, rank.” “Forever” (le’olam) becomes a load-bearing word in 7:3, 16–17, 24–25. Repeated or echoed at 5:10; 6:20; 7:11, 15, 17, 21.
6:13–14 Gen 22:16–17 “Surely I will greatly bless you and greatly multiply you.” (Literally, “blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”) Follows the LXX, except “you” for “your offspring,” keeping the focus on Abraham himself, who “obtained the promise” (6:15). The Greek participle-plus-verb reproduces the Hebrew infinitive absolute of emphasis: כִּי־בָרֵךְ אֲבָרֶכְךָ וְהַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה ki-varekh avarekhkha veharbah arbeh. The LXX’s εἰ μήν ei mēn, “surely,” is a Greek oath formula. The oath itself — בִּי נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי bi nishba’ti, “By myself I have sworn” (22:16) — is the basis of 6:13, 16–18.
7:1–2 Gen 14:17–20 “This Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of God Most High, met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings and blessed him, and Abraham apportioned to him a tenth of everything.” A summary of the narrative in LXX wording (“priest of God Most High” = Hebrew כֹהֵן לְאֵל עֶלְיוֹן kohen le’El Elyon; “a tenth of everything” = מַעֲשֵׂר מִכֹּל ma’aser mikkol). The interpretation of the names follows the Hebrew: מַלְכִּי־צֶדֶק Malki-tsedeq, “king of righteousness,” and שָׁלֵם Shalem, from the root of shalom, “peace.”
7:17, 21 Ps 110:4 7:17: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” 7:21: “The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: ‘You are a priest forever.’” Verbatim LXX. 7:21 quotes the opening of the verse, נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם nishba YHWH velo yinnachem, “The Lord has sworn and will not relent,” and stops (in the best manuscripts) at “forever.” Hebrews 7:20–22 builds on the divine oath, which the Levitical priests never received.
8:5 Exod 25:40 “See that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain.” Follows the LXX with two changes: Hebrews adds “everything” (compare Exod 25:9) and uses a simple past “was shown” for the LXX perfect “has been shown.” Hebrew וּרְאֵה וַעֲשֵׂה בְּתַבְנִיתָם אֲשֶׁר־אַתָּה מָרְאֶה בָּהָר ure’eh va’aseh betavnitam asher-attah mor’eh bahar, “See and make [them] according to their pattern which you are being shown on the mountain.” Hebrew tavnit, “pattern, model” (Exod 25:9), becomes τύπος typos; this text grounds Hebrews’ description of the earthly sanctuary as a “copy and shadow” of the heavenly.
8:8–12 Jer 31:31–34 (LXX 38:31–34) “Behold, days are coming, says the Lord, when I will bring to completion a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah, not like the covenant that I made with their fathers on the day when I took them by the hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt; because they did not remain in my covenant, and I disregarded them, says the Lord. For this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, says the Lord: putting my laws into their mind, I will also write them on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. And they shall not teach, each one his fellow citizen and each one his brother, saying, ‘Know the Lord,’ because all will know me, from the least to the greatest of them. For I will be merciful toward their iniquities, and their sins I will remember no more.” The longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. It follows the LXX closely, though Hebrews has “I will bring to completion” (συντελέσω syntelesō) and “I made” where the LXX uses forms of “covenant”; the Hebrew idiom is to “cut” a covenant (וְכָרַתִּי … בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה vekharatti … berit chadashah, “and I will cut … a new covenant”). Three LXX–MT differences: (1) Hebrew וְאָנֹכִי בָּעַלְתִּי בָם ve’anokhi ba’alti vam, “though I was a husband (master) to them,” where the Greek has “and I disregarded them”; both lay the breach on Israel, and Hebrews draws from the clause “they did not remain” the conclusion that God found fault with the first covenant (8:7–8). (2) Hebrew נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם natatti et-torati beqirbam, “I will put my law within them,” singular “law” and “inward parts,” where the Greek has plural “laws” and “mind.” (3) Hebrew כִּי אֶסְלַח לַעֲוֺנָם ki eslach la’avonam, “for I will forgive their iniquity,” where the Greek has “I will be merciful (ἵλεως hileōs) toward their iniquities,” a word of the same family as “make propitiation” (2:17). Hebrew רֵעֵהוּ re’ehu, “his neighbor,” is “his fellow citizen.”
9:20 Exod 24:8 “This is the blood of the covenant that God commanded for you.” Adapted from the LXX (“Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord covenanted with you”). Hebrews has “this” for “behold” — wording that recalls Jesus’ words over the cup, “This is my blood of the covenant” (Mark 14:24) — and “God commanded” for “the Lord covenanted.” Hebrew הִנֵּה דַם־הַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר כָּרַת יְהוָה עִמָּכֶם hinneh dam-habberit asher karat YHWH immakhem, “Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord has cut with you.”
10:5–7 Ps 40:6–8 (Heb. 40:7–9; LXX 39:7–9) “Sacrifice and offering you did not desire, but a body you prepared for me; in whole burnt offerings and sin offerings you took no pleasure. Then I said, ‘Behold, I have come — in the scroll of the book it is written of me — to do your will, O God.’” Follows the LXX, nearly all of whose manuscripts read “a body” (σῶμα sōma). The Hebrew is אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי oznayim karita li, “ears you have dug for me” — that is, you have opened my ears to hear and obey (cf. Isa 50:4–5). The Greek interprets the part by the whole: the ear that hears belongs to a body that obeys. Hebrews’ point — obedience offered in a body (10:10) rather than animal sacrifice — is precisely the Hebrew psalm’s point. Hebrews has “you took no pleasure” where the LXX, like the Hebrew לֹא שָׁאָלְתָּ lo sha’alta, has “you did not ask”; and it ends with “to do your will, O God,” leaving aside “I delight” and “your law is within my inward parts” (40:8 Eng.). Hebrew בִּמְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר bimgillat-sefer means “in the scroll of the book.”
10:16–17 Jer 31:33–34 “This is the covenant that I will make with them after those days, says the Lord: putting my laws on their hearts, I will also write them on their mind … and their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more.” An abbreviated re-quotation of 8:8–12, introduced as the Holy Spirit’s present testimony (10:15). Hebrews has “with them” for “with the house of Israel,” reverses “mind” and “hearts,” adds “and their lawless deeds,” and moves directly from the promise of the written law to the forgiveness at the end of Jer 31:34 (וּלְחַטָּאתָם לֹא אֶזְכָּר־עוֹד ulechatta’tam lo ezkor-od, “and their sin I will remember no more”). The conclusion follows in 10:18: where there is forgiveness, there is no longer an offering for sin.
10:30 Deut 32:35–36 “Vengeance is mine; I will repay.” And again, “The Lord will judge his people.” The first line differs from the LXX (“in a day of vengeance I will repay”) and is closer to the Hebrew לִי נָקָם וְשִׁלֵּם li naqam veshillem, “Vengeance and recompense are mine.” It is identical with Rom 12:19, suggesting a Greek form already current among Jews or early Christians. The second line agrees with the LXX and the Hebrew כִּי־יָדִין יְהוָה עַמּוֹ ki-yadin YHWH ammo. In Deut 32:36 “judge” means chiefly that God will vindicate his servants; but the same Song announces God’s judgment on his own faithless people (32:19–25), and Hebrews applies it to those within the covenant community who spurn the Son (10:29).
10:37–38 Isa 26:20; Hab 2:3–4 “For yet a very little while, and the coming one will come and will not delay; but my righteous one will live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him.” The opening words, “a very little while” (μικρὸν ὅσον ὅσον mikron hoson hoson), come from LXX Isa 26:20 (Hebrew כִמְעַט־רֶגַע khim’at-rega, “for a brief moment”). The rest follows LXX Hab 2:3–4 with two significant changes: Hebrews adds the article, “the coming one,” making a messianic title, and it reverses the clauses so that the warning against shrinking back applies to the righteous one himself. In the Hebrew of 2:3 the subject is the vision (חָזוֹן chazon): כִּי־בֹא יָבֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר ki-vo yavo lo ye’acher, “it will surely come; it will not delay”; the LXX’s masculine participle already allowed a personal reading. Hab 2:4 in Hebrew reads הִנֵּה עֻפְּלָה לֹא־יָשְׁרָה נַפְשׁוֹ בּוֹ וְצַדִּיק בֶּאֱמוּנָתוֹ יִחְיֶה hinneh upplah lo-yashrah nafsho bo vetsaddiq be’emunato yichyeh, “Behold, [the one] puffed up — his soul is not upright within him; but the righteous one by his faithfulness will live.” The LXX’s first line (“if he shrinks back…”) differs from the Hebrew, but the contrast is the same: the proud who will not wait versus the righteous who live by steadfast trust. Hebrew emunah, “steadfast faithfulness,” and Greek πίστις pistis, faith that holds on, meet in the persevering faith of chapter 11. Most LXX manuscripts read “by my faith(fulness)”; Hebrews has “my righteous one,” as some LXX witnesses do; Paul quotes the line without “my” (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11).
11:5 Gen 5:24 “…and he was not found, because God had taken him away.” “…he had pleased God.” Verbatim LXX. The LXX renders the Hebrew וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ חֲנוֹךְ אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים vayyithallekh Chanokh et-ha’elohim, “and Enoch walked with God,” as “Enoch pleased God,” and וְאֵינֶנּוּ כִּי־לָקַח אֹתוֹ אֱלֹהִים ve’enennu ki-laqach oto elohim, “and he was not, for God took him,” as “he was not found, because God took him away.” Hebrews reasons from “pleased” in 11:6; walking with God and pleasing God describe the same life of faith.
11:18 Gen 21:12 “Through Isaac offspring shall be named for you.” Verbatim LXX, an exact rendering of the Hebrew כִּי בְיִצְחָק יִקָּרֵא לְךָ זָרַע ki veYitschaq yiqqare lekha zara. Also quoted in Rom 9:7. The promise was bound to the very son Abraham was commanded to offer — hence his reasoning that God could raise the dead (11:19).
11:21 Gen 47:31 “…and he worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff.” Verbatim LXX (omitting the name “Israel”). The Hebrew reads עַל־רֹאשׁ הַמִּטָּה al-rosh hammittah, “at the head of the bed.” The consonants of the Hebrew word can be vocalized mittah, “bed” (the Masoretic reading), or matteh, “staff” (the reading behind the LXX). Both picture the dying patriarch bowing in worship; the staff suits Hebrews’ portrait of the patriarchs as pilgrims (cf. Gen 32:10). Hebrews joins this scene to Jacob’s blessing of Joseph’s sons (Gen 48).
12:5–6 Prov 3:11–12 “My son, do not think lightly of the discipline of the Lord, nor lose heart when you are reproved by him; for the Lord disciplines the one he loves, and he scourges every son whom he receives.” Follows the LXX, adding “my” to “son.” Hebrew 3:11: מוּסַר יְהוָה בְּנִי אַל־תִּמְאָס musar YHWH beni al-tim’as, “The discipline of the Lord, my son, do not reject.” In 3:12b the Hebrew reads וּכְאָב אֶת־בֵּן יִרְצֶה ukh’av et-ben yirtseh, “even as a father the son in whom he delights”; the LXX’s “and he scourges” probably reflects reading the same consonants as a form of the verb “to cause pain.” Both readings bind the Lord’s discipline to fatherly love, and Hebrews’ exposition (12:7–11) develops the father–son picture that the Hebrew makes explicit. Hebrew musar is rendered παιδεία paideia, “discipline, training.”
12:12–13 Isa 35:3; Prov 4:26 “Therefore lift up the drooping hands and the weakened knees, and make straight paths for your feet.” A free adaptation of two texts. Isa 35:3: חַזְּקוּ יָדַיִם רָפוֹת וּבִרְכַּיִם כֹּשְׁלוֹת אַמֵּצוּ chazzequ yadayim rafot uvirkayim koshlot ammetsu, “Strengthen slack hands, and make firm stumbling knees”; Hebrews’ wording is close to the Greek of Isa 35:3 (cf. Sir 25:23). Prov 4:26: פַּלֵּס מַעְגַּל רַגְלֶךָ palles ma’gal raglekha, “make level the track of your foot,” which the LXX renders “make straight paths for your feet”; Hebrews makes the command plural, addressed to the whole church. Isaiah’s words belong to the vision of the redeemed returning to Zion on the highway of holiness (35:8–10), fitting Hebrews’ picture of the journey to Mount Zion (12:22).
12:15 Deut 29:18 (Heb. 29:17) “…that no root of bitterness, springing up, causes trouble…” Close to the LXX, “lest there be among you a root springing up in gall and bitterness.” Hebrews’ “causes trouble” (ἐνοχλῇ enochlē) closely resembles the LXX’s “in gall” (en cholē), and some LXX manuscripts themselves read “causes trouble.” Hebrew שֹׁרֶשׁ פֹּרֶה רֹאשׁ וְלַעֲנָה shoresh poreh rosh vela’anah, “a root bearing poison and wormwood” — in context, any person “whose heart turns away today from the Lord our God” to serve other gods. Hebrews applies it to apostasy within the church, which can defile many.
12:20 Exod 19:12–13 “If even an animal touches the mountain, it shall be stoned.” A free summary. Hebrews mentions only the animal, which heightens the dread: not even a beast could approach unharmed. Hebrew כִּי־סָקוֹל יִסָּקֵל ki-saqol yissaqel, “he shall surely be stoned,” and אִם־בְּהֵמָה אִם־אִישׁ לֹא יִחְיֶה im-behemah im-ish lo yichyeh, “whether animal or man, he shall not live.”
12:21 Deut 9:19 “I am terrified and trembling.” The LXX of Deut 9:19 has “I am terrified”; Hebrews adds “and trembling.” Hebrew כִּי יָגֹרְתִּי מִפְּנֵי הָאַף וְהַחֵמָה ki yagorti mippene ha’af vehachemah, “for I was in dread of the anger and the wrath.” In Deuteronomy Moses speaks of the aftermath of the golden calf at Horeb (Sinai); Hebrews gathers the whole Sinai experience into one portrait of terror.
12:26 Hag 2:6 “Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also heaven.” Adapted from the LXX (“Yet once more I will shake the heaven and the earth and the sea and the dry land”): Hebrews abbreviates and adds “not only … but also,” putting the weight on heaven. Hebrew עוֹד אַחַת מְעַט הִיא וַאֲנִי מַרְעִישׁ אֶת־הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֶת־הָאָרֶץ od achat me’at hi va’ani mar’ish et-hashshamayim ve’et-ha’arets, “Yet once — it is a little while — and I will shake the heavens and the earth.” The LXX “yet once more” (ἔτι ἅπαξ eti hapax) is the phrase Hebrews expounds in 12:27; the Hebrew “once” supports the same point — a single, final shaking.
12:29 Deut 4:24 (cf. 9:3) “For our God is a consuming fire.” Close to the LXX (“the Lord your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God”), with “our God” for “the Lord your God.” Hebrew כִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה הוּא אֵל קַנָּא ki YHWH elohekha esh okhlah hu el qanna, “for the Lord your God is a devouring fire, a jealous God.” Hebrews uses it without an introductory formula, as a statement of the same God who spoke at Sinai and now speaks from heaven.
13:5 Deut 31:6, 8; Josh 1:5 (cf. Gen 28:15; 1 Chr 28:20) “I will never leave you, nor will I ever forsake you.” Not identical to any single LXX text. Deut 31:6, 8 speak in the third person (לֹא יַרְפְּךָ וְלֹא יַעַזְבֶךָּ lo yarpekha velo ya’azvekka, “he will not let you go, and he will not abandon you”); Josh 1:5 has the first person: לֹא אַרְפְּךָ וְלֹא אֶעֶזְבֶךָּ lo arpekha velo e’ezvekka, “I will not let you go, and I will not abandon you.” Hebrews piles up Greek negatives for emphasis (οὐ μή … οὐδ’ οὐ μή ou mē … oud’ ou mē, “never … nor ever”). The same form of the saying appears in Philo (On the Confusion of Tongues 166), which suggests it circulated in this form among Greek-speaking Jews.
13:6 Ps 118:6 (LXX 117:6) “The Lord is my helper; I will not fear. What will man do to me?” Verbatim LXX. Hebrew יְהוָה לִי לֹא אִירָא מַה־יַּעֲשֶׂה לִי אָדָם YHWH li lo ira mah-ya’aseh li adam, “The Lord is for me; I will not fear; what can man do to me?” The LXX’s “helper” anticipates the next verse of the psalm (“the Lord is for me among my helpers,” 118:7). The congregation that has lost property (10:34) answers God’s promise with the psalmist’s confession.

Major Allusions

Beyond its direct quotations Hebrews alludes constantly to the Old Testament. The following are the most important; the commentary discusses each in place.

  • 1:2 “heir of all things” — Ps 2:8, “I will make the nations your inheritance.”
  • 1:3 “sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high” — Ps 110:1, the first echo of the book’s controlling text.
  • 2:16 “he takes hold of the offspring of Abraham” — Isa 41:8–9, where God addresses Israel as זֶרַע אַבְרָהָם zera Avraham, “offspring of Abraham,” and says, “I took hold of you (הֶחֱזַקְתִּיךָ hechezaqtikha) from the ends of the earth.”
  • 3:16–18 the rebels who fell in the wilderness — Num 14 (esp. 14:22–23, 29–35), the refusal at Kadesh and God’s oath that that generation would not see the land.
  • 4:8 Joshua (Greek Iēsous) and the conquest — Josh 21:44; 22:4.
  • 6:7–8 land bearing thorns and thistles — Gen 3:17–18.
  • 6:19–20 the hope that enters “within the curtain,” where Jesus has entered as forerunner — Lev 16:2, 12, 15, the high priest’s entry “inside the curtain” (מִבֵּית לַפָּרֹכֶת mibbet lapparokhet) on the Day of Atonement.
  • Chapter 7 Melchizedek — Gen 14:17–20 read through Ps 110:4; the Levitical tithe — Num 18:21–26; the tribe of Judah — Gen 49:10.
  • 8:1–5; 9:1–5 the tabernacle and its furnishings — Exod 25–26; 30:1–10; 37; the jar of manna — Exod 16:33; Aaron’s rod — Num 17:8–10; the tablets — Exod 25:16; Deut 10:1–5; the mercy seat — Exod 25:17–22.
  • 9:6–14, 25 the Day of Atonement ritual — Lev 16; the ashes of the heifer — Num 19.
  • 9:18–22 the covenant inauguration at Sinai — Exod 24:3–8; cleansing with blood, hyssop, scarlet wool — Lev 14:4–7; Num 19:6, 18; Lev 17:11.
  • 9:28 “to bear the sins of many” — Isa 53:12.
  • 10:11–13 priests standing daily; Christ seated — Ps 110:1.
  • 10:27 “a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries” — Isa 26:11.
  • 10:28 death “on the testimony of two or three witnesses” — Deut 17:6; 19:15.
  • Chapter 11 the whole narrative of the Old Testament: creation (Gen 1); Abel (Gen 4); Enoch (Gen 5); Noah (Gen 6–9); Abraham and Sarah (Gen 12; 15; 17–18; 21–23); Isaac, Jacob, Joseph (Gen 27; 47–50); Moses (Exod 2; 12; 14); Jericho and Rahab (Josh 2; 6); the judges, David, Samuel, and the prophets (Judges; 1–2 Samuel; 1–2 Kings; Daniel); the martyrs of the Maccabean era (cf. 2 Macc 6–7).
  • 11:12 “as the stars of heaven … as the sand by the seashore” — Gen 22:17; 32:12.
  • 11:13 “strangers and exiles on the earth” — Gen 23:4; 47:9; Ps 39:12.
  • 12:2 “seated at the right hand of the throne of God” — Ps 110:1.
  • 12:16–17 Esau’s birthright and lost blessing — Gen 25:29–34; 27:30–40.
  • 12:18–21 Sinai: fire, darkness, gloom, storm, trumpet, voice — Exod 19:16–19; 20:18–21; Deut 4:11–12; 5:22–27.
  • 12:22 Mount Zion, the city of the living God — Ps 2:6; 48:1–2; Isa 2:2–3.
  • 12:24 “the sprinkled blood that speaks better than the blood of Abel” — Gen 4:10, Abel’s blood crying from the ground.
  • 13:2 entertaining angels unawares — Gen 18:1–8; 19:1–3.
  • 13:11–13 bodies of the sin offerings burned “outside the camp” — Lev 16:27; cf. Exod 29:14; Lev 4:12, 21; the tent of meeting outside the camp — Exod 33:7.
  • 13:15 “the fruit of lips” — Hos 14:2 (Heb. 14:3). The Hebrew speaks of offering “bulls of our lips” (praise in place of sacrificial bulls); the LXX reads “the fruit of our lips.” The sense is the same: praise and confession are the sacrifice God now desires.
  • 13:20 “who brought up from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep” — Isa 63:11, הַמַּעֲלֵם מִיָּם אֵת רֹעֵי צֹאנוֹ hamma’alem miyyam et ro’e tso’no, “who brought them up from the sea with the shepherds of his flock”; “by the blood of the eternal covenant” — Zech 9:11, בְּדַם־בְּרִיתֵךְ bedam-beritekh, “by the blood of your covenant” (cf. Isa 55:3; Jer 32:40; Ezek 37:26).
