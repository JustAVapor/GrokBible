God Has Spoken in His Son (1:1–4)

Translation

1Long ago, in many portions and in many ways, God, having spoken to the fathers in the prophets, 2in these last days has spoken to us in a Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom he also made the ages. 3He, being the radiance of his glory and the exact imprint of his essential being, and bearing all things by the word of his power, after making purification for sins through himself, sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high, 4having become as much better than the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs.

Setting and Structure

These four verses are a single sentence in Greek, and one of the most carefully crafted sentences in the New Testament. Ancient teachers of rhetoric called such a sentence a “period” (περίοδος periodos, literally “a way around”): a long, rounded construction in which several clauses circle around one main statement and the whole is not complete until the last word. Hebrews opens with such a period because it is announcing the theme of the entire sermon, and it does so with the solemnity the theme deserves. English translations usually break it into two or three sentences for readability; the translation above keeps it as one, so that the reader can feel how everything hangs on a single main verb.

The structure can be laid out as follows.

  • The main clause (1:1–2a). The grammatical heart of the sentence is “God … has spoken to us in a Son.” Before it stands a participle phrase describing God’s earlier speaking: “having spoken long ago to the fathers in the prophets.” The two halves are deliberately parallel, and the parallel highlights four contrasts: long ago / in these last days; to the fathers / to us; in the prophets / in a Son; and, implicitly, in many portions and many ways / in one final and complete way. The subject, God, is the same; the act, speaking, is the same. The contrast is not between a false word and a true one, but between a partial word and a final one.
  • Two relative clauses about the Son’s relation to all things (1:2b). “Whom he appointed heir of all things” (the end of history) and “through whom he also made the ages” (the beginning of history). The Son stands at both ends of the story of creation.
  • A third relative clause about who the Son is and what he did (1:3). It begins “who” (ὃς hos) and has its own main verb, “sat down” (ἐκάθισεν ekathisen). Before that verb come three participles. Two are present tense and describe what the Son continually is and does: “being” the radiance and imprint of God, and “bearing” all things. One is aorist and describes a completed act: “having made” purification for sins. Then comes the climax: he “sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high.”
  • A concluding participle (1:4). “Having become as much better than the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs.” This final phrase is the hinge to the rest of the chapter. It introduces the angels and “the name,” both of which the catena of quotations in 1:5–14 will explain.

Within this structure many interpreters have counted seven descriptions of the Son: (1) heir of all things, (2) agent of creation, (3) radiance of God’s glory, (4) imprint of God’s being, (5) sustainer of all things, (6) maker of purification for sins, (7) seated at God’s right hand. Whether or not the author intended the number to be noticed, the sequence moves in a great arc: from the Son’s eternal relation to God, through creation and providence, down into the work of redemption, and back up to the throne. It is the same pattern found in other early Christian confessions about Christ, such as Philippians 2:6–11 and Colossians 1:15–20, and some scholars think the author is drawing on, or composing in the style of, a hymn or confession already known to his hearers. That is possible, but the passage is so thoroughly woven into the vocabulary and argument of Hebrews that it is best read as the author’s own composition.

Several features of the sentence anticipate the rest of the book. The note of God’s speaking will return in the warning that we must listen to him (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 12:25). “Heir” and “inherited” (1:2, 4) look ahead to “those who are about to inherit salvation” (1:14) and to the inheritance of the promises (6:12, 17; 9:15). “Purification for sins” compresses into three words the high-priestly argument of chapters 7–10. And “sat down at the right hand” introduces Psalm 110:1, the text that frames this chapter (1:3, 13) and returns at turning points in the book (8:1; 10:12; 12:2).

Hebrews also opens with sound. The first verse is marked by alliteration on the letter p: Πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως πάλαι … πατράσιν … προφήταις Polymerōs kai polytropōs palai … patrasin … prophētais. A sermon was heard before it was read, and the author, who writes the most polished Greek in the New Testament, wants his hearers to feel from the first syllables that something weighty is being said.

The historical setting is worth keeping in mind. The congregation addressed was made up largely of Jewish believers who had grown up revering the law, the prophets, the temple, the priesthood, and the angels who were thought to have mediated the law at Sinai. They were now under social pressure and tempted to drift back toward the securities of the old order (2:1; 10:32–39). The opening sentence does not belittle what they had received; it honors it as genuinely God’s word. But it insists that all of that earlier speaking was preparation, and that God’s final, complete word has now been spoken in his Son. To turn from the Son back to the earlier word would be to turn from the fulfillment back to the promise.

Verse by Verse

1:1

Long ago, in many portions and in many ways, God, having spoken to the fathers in the prophets,

The sermon begins not with the preacher, nor with the congregation, nor even with Christ, but with God, and with God as one who speaks. The participle λαλήσας lalēsas, “having spoken,” is an aorist participle of λαλέω laleō, “speak.” It looks back on the whole Old Testament period as a completed stage of revelation. The same verb will be the main verb of the sentence in 1:2: God “has spoken” in a Son. The author’s first theological conviction, on which everything else depends, is that God has not remained silent. The God of Israel is not an unknown force to be guessed at; he has made himself known by speech. Hebrews will close on the same note: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking” (12:25).

Two rare and sonorous adverbs stand at the very front of the sentence for emphasis: Πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως Polymerōs kai polytropōs. Neither occurs anywhere else in the New Testament. Together they describe the character of the earlier revelation: it came in many portions and in many ways. It was fragmentary (given piece by piece over many centuries, to many people, each prophet receiving a part) and it was varied (given through many different means). The Old Testament itself shows that variety: God spoke through the words of the covenant at Sinai, through visions and dreams, through angelic messengers, through the Urim and Thummim, through symbolic actions such as Jeremiah’s broken jar or Ezekiel’s siege model, through psalms and proverbs, through history and law. In Numbers 12:6–8 the Lord himself draws a distinction between the ways he speaks: “If there is a prophet among you, I the Lord make myself known to him in a vision; in a dream I speak with him. Not so my servant Moses … mouth to mouth I speak with him.” Even within the old covenant, then, revelation came in different measures and modes.

The point of the two adverbs is not to criticize that earlier revelation. Everything the author says about it is positive: it was God who spoke, and he spoke truly. The point is rather that it was partial and preparatory. Calvin rightly observes that the author is not setting the law and the prophets against Christ, as though they were in conflict, but showing that God measured out his revelation gradually until he brought it to completion in his Son. A mosaic laid one tile at a time is not a false picture, but the full image only appears when the last piece is in place. The phrase therefore sets up the contrast with 1:2: the many portions and many ways stand over against the one, final, complete word spoken “in a Son.”

Word Study: πολυμερῶς polymerōs and πολυτρόπως polytropōs

Πολυμερῶς Polymerōs is built from poly-, “many,” and μέρος meros, “part, portion, share.” It means “in many parts” or “bit by bit,” and so it can also carry the idea “at many times.” Πολυτρόπως Polytropōs is built from poly- and τρόπος tropos, “manner, way,” and means “in many ways, in various manners.” The first adverb stresses the quantity and distribution of the earlier revelation (spread out over many speakers and centuries), the second its diversity of form. Both words are emphatic by position, and together with the alliteration of the opening verse they give the sermon a grand and deliberate beginning. They also quietly prepare for a contrast: the Son is not one more portion and one more way; he is the whole and final word.

The word πάλαι palai, “long ago,” sets the time. It contrasts with “in these last days” in 1:2. The phrase “to the fathers” (τοῖς πατράσιν tois patrasin) means the ancestors of Israel in general, not only the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. It is the language of a family: the author speaks of Israel’s forebears as the spiritual ancestors of the Christian congregation. Hebrews never regards the people of the old covenant as strangers to the church. The heroes of faith in chapter 11 are “our” forerunners, and they and we will be “made perfect” together (11:39–40). There is one people of God, addressed by one God, across both stages of his speaking.

God spoke “in the prophets” (ἐν τοῖς προφήταις en tois prophētais). The preposition ἐν en usually means “in,” but here it has an instrumental sense, “by means of,” and it may also suggest that God’s word was truly present in the prophets as they spoke (compare 2 Pet 1:21, “men carried along by the Holy Spirit spoke from God”). The translation keeps “in” because the same preposition is used in 1:2 of the Son, and the parallel is part of the argument: God spoke in the prophets; he has now spoken in a Son. “The prophets” here includes more than the writing prophets from Isaiah to Malachi. Moses was the prophet par excellence (Deut 18:15; 34:10), and David is called a prophet in the New Testament (Acts 2:30). The phrase covers all the human instruments through whom God spoke before Christ, which is to say, the whole Old Testament. That is confirmed by the way Hebrews goes on to quote Moses, the Psalms, and the Prophets indiscriminately as the voice of God.

For the original hearers this verse was both affirming and unsettling. It affirmed the Scriptures they loved: they had not been misled. But by describing all of that revelation as “long ago” and “in many portions,” it quietly announced that something had now arrived that the earlier word could only anticipate. Pastorally, the verse also corrects two opposite errors that still trouble the church: the error of treating the Old Testament as obsolete or as the word of a different God, and the error of treating it as complete in itself, without its fulfillment in Christ. The same God spoke both, and the first speaking leads to the second.

1:2

in these last days has spoken to us in a Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom he also made the ages.

Here is the main clause of the whole opening period: God “has spoken to us in a Son.” The verb ἐλάλησεν elalēsen is an aorist indicative, which in Greek presents an action as a complete whole. The author does not say that God “is speaking” in a Son, as though the revelation were still in progress and awaiting further installments. He views the entire event of the Son, his coming, his life, his death, his exaltation, as one decisive act of divine speech. That is why the revelation in the Son is final: there is nothing beyond it to be added, only its meaning to be grasped, believed, and obeyed. Hebrews will later warn that this word was “declared at first through the Lord” and “confirmed to us by those who heard” (2:3).

“In these last days.” The phrase ἐπ’ ἐσχάτου τῶν ἡμερῶν τούτων ep’ eschatou tōn hēmerōn toutōn, literally “at the end of these days,” is borrowed from the language of the Greek Old Testament, where “at the end of the days” translates the Hebrew בְּאַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים be’acharit hayyamim. That Hebrew phrase is used by the prophets of the time when God would bring his purposes to their climax: “It shall come to pass in the latter days that the mountain of the house of the Lord shall be established as the highest of the mountains … and all the nations shall stream to it” (Isa 2:2); Balaam announces what Israel will do to Moab “in the latter days” before he prophesies the star from Jacob and the scepter from Israel (Num 24:14–17). By adding “these,” Hebrews says that this long-awaited final era is not merely future; it has begun. The coming of the Son marks the turn of the ages. Later the author will say that Christ has appeared “at the consummation of the ages” to put away sin (9:26), and Peter at Pentecost interprets the outpouring of the Spirit as the fulfillment of what God promised “in the last days” (Acts 2:17). The church lives in the last days, between the Son’s first appearing and his second (9:28).

“To us.” The contrast is with “the fathers.” The pronoun includes the author and his hearers. They are not spectators of a revelation given to someone else; the final word has been addressed to them, which is exactly why they cannot afford to neglect it (2:1–3).

Word Study: ἐν υἱῷ en huiō, “in a Son”

Greek has a definite article (“the”) but no indefinite article (“a”). When a noun lacks the article, as υἱῷ huiō does here, the writer is often stressing the kind or quality of the thing rather than pointing to a particular individual already known. The author could have written “in his Son” or “in the Son”; instead he writes, literally, “in Son,” that is, in one who has the character of a Son. Westcott brought out the force by paraphrasing the contrast as between messengers who were prophets and one whose very nature is that of a Son. The point is not to make the Son anonymous (the next clauses identify him unmistakably), but to set the kind of messenger in contrast with the prophets. The prophets were servants who carried a word; the Son is related to the Father in his very being, so that what he speaks and is is the word of God. English cannot reproduce this neatly: “in a Son” sounds indefinite, while “in his Son” loses the emphasis. The reader should hear “in a Son” as “in one who is Son.” Later the author will draw the same contrast between Moses, faithful in God’s house “as a servant,” and Christ, faithful over God’s house “as a Son” (3:5–6).

“Whom he appointed heir of all things.” The first thing said about the Son concerns the end of history. The verb ἔθηκεν ethēken (from τίθημι tithēmi, “put, place, appoint”) is used of a formal appointment. A κληρονόμος klēronomos, “heir,” is one who receives an inheritance as his rightful possession, and in the ancient world the heir was ordinarily the son. Jesus’ own parable of the wicked tenants made the same connection: the owner of the vineyard “had still one, a beloved son,” and the tenants said, “This is the heir” (Mark 12:6–7). The background in Israel’s Scriptures is Psalm 2, the psalm the author will quote in 1:5. There God says to his anointed king, “You are my son,” and immediately adds:

Old Testament Text: Psalm 2:8

Hebrew: שְׁאַל מִמֶּנִּי וְאֶתְּנָה גוֹיִם נַחֲלָתֶךָ וַאֲחֻזָּתְךָ אַפְסֵי־אָרֶץ — she’al mimmenni ve’ettenah goyim nachalatekha va’achuzzatekha afse-arets, “Ask of me, and I will give the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession.”

The Hebrew נַחֲלָה nachalah, “inheritance,” is the word used of Israel’s allotted land. In Psalm 2 the inheritance of the Davidic son is extended to all nations and to the ends of the earth. Hebrews widens it still further: the Son is heir not merely of the nations but of “all things” (πάντων pantōn), the whole created order. Hebrews 2:5–9 will develop this, applying Psalm 8 (“you put all things in subjection under his feet”) to Jesus.

When was the Son “appointed” heir? The aorist verb could refer to God’s eternal purpose, decreed before creation, or to the moment when that purpose was publicly realized at the Son’s exaltation (1:3–4, 13). Both belong together. The Son did not become heir by accident or by achievement alone; he was appointed by the Father’s sovereign decision. But the inheritance was entered upon when, having made purification for sins, he sat down at God’s right hand. This is why Hebrews can say both that the Son is eternal (1:2b–3, 8, 10–12) and that he “has inherited” the name (1:4). The heir has also become the one through whom others inherit: believers are “those who are about to inherit salvation” (1:14), and Paul calls them “heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ” (Rom 8:17).

“Through whom he also made the ages.” Having spoken of the end of history, the author turns to its beginning. The Son who will receive all things as his inheritance is the one through whom God made all things in the first place. The word καὶ kai, “also,” links the two thoughts: it is fitting that the one who is the goal of creation should also be its agent. The preposition διά dia, “through,” with the genitive marks the Son as the agent through whom the Father acted. The same conviction appears across the New Testament: “all things came into being through him” (John 1:3); “one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things” (1 Cor 8:6); “in him all things were created … all things were created through him and for him” (Col 1:16). Notice the shape of Colossians 1:16, “through him and for him,” which corresponds to Hebrews’ “through whom” and “heir of all things.” Hebrews thus places the Son on the Creator’s side of the line that divides Creator from creature, a line the Old Testament guards fiercely: “I am the Lord, who made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens” (Isa 44:24). The angels, by contrast, are things made (1:7).

Word Study: τοὺς αἰῶνας tous aiōnas, “the ages”

The noun αἰών aiōn (here plural, aiōnas) basically means “age,” a long period of time, and in the phrase “forever” it means unending time (1:8, “forever and ever,” literally “to the age of the age”). In biblical Greek it often translates the Hebrew עוֹלָם olam, a word for remote or unending time that in later Hebrew also came to mean “world.” So when Hebrews says that God “made the ages” through the Son, the phrase includes both the created universe and the successive eras of its history. Some translations render it “the worlds” or “the universe,” which captures the spatial side; the translation above keeps “the ages” to preserve the temporal side as well, since Hebrews thinks in terms of “this age” and “the age to come” (6:5). The Son is Lord not only of space but of time, of the whole sweep of history from creation to consummation. Hebrews 11:3 uses the same phrase: “By faith we understand that the ages were fashioned by the word of God.”

Historical Background: Wisdom, the Word, and the Son

Jewish thinkers before the New Testament had already reflected on the means by which God created. The Old Testament speaks of creation by God’s word (“By the word of the Lord the heavens were made,” Ps 33:6) and by his wisdom: in Proverbs 8:22–31 Wisdom speaks as one who was beside God “like a master workman” when he established the heavens. The Wisdom of Solomon, written in Greek probably in Alexandria a century or so before Christ, prays to God “who made all things by your word, and by your wisdom formed humankind” (Wis 9:1–2), and describes Wisdom in language that Hebrews 1:3 echoes closely. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish contemporary of Jesus, spoke of the divine Logos (“Word”) as the instrument through whom God fashioned the world. Hebrews uses this familiar vocabulary, but transforms it. For the wisdom writers, Wisdom is a personified attribute of God; for Philo the Logos is an intermediary of uncertain status. For Hebrews, the agent of creation is a Son, a person who would take flesh and blood (2:14), die, and be exalted. What Israel had glimpsed in poetic personification is revealed as the eternal Son of God.

The verse, then, moves from the fact of revelation (God has spoken) to the identity of the revealer (a Son) to his stature (heir of all things and agent of creation). The order matters. The author mentions the Son’s inheritance before his role in creation because the end illuminates the beginning: the world was made through the Son for the Son. For a congregation tempted to think that their future lay with the visible and familiar institutions of the old order, this was a needed reminder. All things belong to the Son. To hold fast to him is to hold to the one who owns the future.

1:3

He, being the radiance of his glory and the exact imprint of his essential being, and bearing all things by the word of his power, after making purification for sins through himself, sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high,

This verse is the summit of the opening sentence and one of the richest statements about Christ in all of Scripture. It is introduced by a relative pronoun, ὃς hos, “who” (rendered “He” above), and its single main verb comes near the end: ἐκάθισεν ekathisen, “he sat down.” Everything before that verb is expressed in participles, and their tenses are carefully chosen. The first two, ὤν ōn, “being,” and φέρων pherōn, “bearing,” are present participles: they describe what the Son always is and continually does, not something that began at a point in time. The third, ποιησάμενος poiēsamenos, “having made,” is an aorist participle: it describes a completed act that took place before he sat down. So the verse moves from the Son’s eternal being (radiance and imprint), to his ongoing work in the world (sustaining all things), to his once-for-all work in history (purification for sins), to his present position (seated at God’s right hand).

The contrast between “being” here and “having become” in 1:4 is important. The present participle ὤν ōn asserts what the Son is, without beginning; the aorist participle γενόμενος genomenos in 1:4 describes what he became through his exaltation. The Gospel of John makes a similar contrast: “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1), but “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14). Hebrews does not say that the Son became the radiance of God’s glory. He is that, always.

“The radiance of his glory.” The “glory” (δόξα doxa) of God in the Greek Old Testament translates the Hebrew כָּבוֹד kavod, literally “weight, heaviness,” and so “importance, splendor.” In Israel’s history the glory of the Lord was the visible, blazing manifestation of God’s presence: it appeared on Sinai “like a devouring fire” (Exod 24:17), and when the tabernacle was completed, “the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle” (Exod 40:34, Hebrew וּכְבוֹד יְהוָה מָלֵא אֶת־הַמִּשְׁכָּן ukhevod YHWH male et-hammishkan). Ezekiel saw “the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the Lord” as a brilliance like a rainbow (Ezek 1:28). For readers steeped in this tradition, to say that the Son is the ἀπαύγασμα apaugasma of God’s glory is to say that in him the very shining of God’s presence has come among us. John says the same: “we beheld his glory” (John 1:14); Paul speaks of “the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ” (2 Cor 4:6).

Word Study: ἀπαύγασμα apaugasma, “radiance”

The noun occurs only here in the New Testament. It comes from the verb ἀπαυγάζω apaugazō, “to shine forth” or “to reflect,” built on αὐγή augē, “brightness, ray of light.” Because the prefix apo- (“from”) can suggest either light streaming out from a source or light thrown back from a surface, the word can mean either “radiance, effulgence” (active: the brightness that shines out from a light) or “reflection” (passive: light reflected from a mirror). The closest parallel in Jewish literature is in the Wisdom of Solomon, which says of divine Wisdom: “she is a radiance (apaugasma) of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness” (Wis 7:26). There the companion word “mirror” could tilt the meaning toward “reflection.” Philo also uses the word in describing how the human mind is related to the divine Logos. In Hebrews, however, the active meaning “radiance” fits best. The Son is not a separate surface off which God’s light happens to bounce; he is the shining-forth of that light itself. A ray of sunlight is not the sun, yet it is of the same nature as the sun and is never separated from it; where the sun is, its radiance is; and it is by the radiance that we see the sun at all. That analogy, much used by early Christian teachers, does justice both to the Son’s distinction from the Father and to his oneness with him.

Interpretive Options: “Radiance” or “Reflection”?

  • Radiance (effulgence). The Son is the outshining of God’s glory, light from light. This was the understanding of the Greek-speaking church fathers, and it shaped the phrase “Light from Light” in the Nicene Creed. Chrysostom, preaching on this verse, used it to show that the Son is of the same nature as the Father and was never without him, as light is never without its brightness. Most modern commentators also prefer this sense.
  • Reflection. Some interpreters, appealing to the “mirror” language of Wisdom 7:26, take the word passively: the Son reflects God’s glory as a mirror reflects light.

Evaluation: The context of Hebrews 1:3 favors “radiance.” The present participle “being” points to what the Son is intrinsically, and the parallel phrase “exact imprint of his essential being” stresses identity of nature rather than mere resemblance. A reflection can exist apart from its source and may be distorted; the Son is the perfect and inseparable expression of God. Yet even the “reflection” view, rightly understood, affirms that to see the Son is to see God’s glory (John 14:9). The two senses differ in nuance, not in their exalted view of Christ.

“The exact imprint of his essential being.” The second phrase sharpens the first. A χαρακτήρ charaktēr (from which English gets “character”) was originally a tool for engraving or stamping, and then the mark or impression made by it. The word was used for the image stamped on a coin by a die and for the impression left in wax by a signet ring. The impression corresponds exactly, point for point, to the seal that made it. If you have the impression, you know precisely what the seal looks like. So the Son is the exact representation of the being of God: not an approximation, not a likeness drawn by an observer, but the perfect, one-to-one expression of what God is. Jesus said, “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (John 14:9), and Paul calls Christ “the image (εἰκών eikōn) of the invisible God” (Col 1:15). The word χαρακτήρ charaktēr is, if anything, even stronger than “image,” since it stresses exact correspondence. It occurs nowhere else in the New Testament.

Word Study: ὑπόστασις hypostasis, “essential being”

The noun ὑπόστασις hypostasis is formed from hypo, “under,” and stasis, “standing,” and literally means “that which stands under,” hence “foundation, substance, underlying reality.” In ordinary Greek it could mean the sediment at the bottom of a liquid, the foundation of a building, a plan or undertaking, or the real substance of a thing as opposed to its appearance. Here it means the essential being or real nature of God: what God truly is. Hebrews uses the word three times, with different nuances that all grow from this root idea: here (1:3), God’s “essential being”; in 3:14, “the beginning of our confidence,” the firm footing on which a believer stands; and in 11:1, “faith is the assurance of things hoped for,” that which gives substance and solid reality to what is not yet seen.

A caution is in order. In the theological debates of the fourth century, Greek-speaking theologians came to use hypostasis in a technical sense for each of the three “persons” of the Trinity, distinguishing it from ousia, the one divine “being” or “substance.” If one read that later usage back into Hebrews, the verse would seem to say that the Son is the imprint of the Father’s person, as if distinct from his nature. But that technical distinction did not yet exist when Hebrews was written. In 1:3 hypostasis means God’s very nature and reality. The verse thus teaches that the Son perfectly expresses what God is, which is fully consistent with, and indeed one of the scriptural foundations for, the church’s later confession that the Son is of one being with the Father.

“Bearing all things by the word of his power.” The Son is not only the agent through whom the universe was made (1:2); he also upholds and carries it now. The participle φέρων pherōn, from φέρω pherō, “carry, bear, bring,” is present tense: he is continually bearing “all things” (τὰ πάντα ta panta, the totality of creation). The picture is not of Atlas in Greek mythology, straining under the static weight of the sky, but of one who carries the universe forward, sustaining it in existence and bearing it along toward its appointed goal. The same verb is used of the Spirit “carrying along” the prophets (2 Pet 1:21) and of Hebrews’ own call, “let us be carried on to maturity” (6:1). Paul says the same of the Son: “in him all things hold together” (Col 1:17). Nothing in creation, from the movement of galaxies to the next breath of a persecuted believer, is outside the Son’s sustaining care.

He does this “by the word of his power” (τῷ ῥήματι τῆς δυνάμεως tō rhēmati tēs dynameōs). The phrase is a Hebrew-style expression in which the genitive (“of his power”) functions like an adjective: “by his powerful word.” As God created by speaking (“And God said, Let there be light,” Gen 1:3; compare Heb 11:3), so the Son sustains creation by his word. In the context of this opening sentence, it is striking that the theme of God’s speaking returns: the one through whom God has spoken his final word to us is the one whose word holds the universe in being. The “his” most naturally refers to the Son, who is the subject of the participle; the power by which the cosmos is upheld is his own.

“After making purification for sins through himself.” Here the sentence turns from cosmic grandeur to the work of redemption, and the change is startling. The one who bears all things has also borne our sins (9:28). The noun καθαρισμός katharismos, “cleansing, purification,” belongs to the vocabulary of Israel’s worship. Under the law, sin was not only guilt to be pardoned but defilement that made a person unfit to come near the holy God, and it also defiled the sanctuary itself. The great Day of Atonement existed to deal with this: “For on this day atonement shall be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins you shall be clean before the Lord” (Lev 16:30). The Hebrew reads יְכַפֵּר עֲלֵיכֶם לְטַהֵר אֶתְכֶם מִכֹּל חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם yekhapper alekhem letaher etkhem mikkol chattotekhem, “he shall make atonement for you, to purify you from all your sins.” Hebrews will argue in chapters 9–10 that those annual rites could cleanse only outwardly and only for a time, whereas the blood of Christ “will purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14), and that the heavenly sanctuary itself was purified by his sacrifice (9:23). This brief phrase in 1:3 is the seed from which that whole argument will grow.

The participle ποιησάμενος poiēsamenos, “having made,” is in the aorist tense, describing a completed act. It is also in the middle voice, which in Greek often indicates that the subject acts with personal involvement or for himself. The Son did not commission someone else to make purification, nor did he offer an animal outside himself; he accomplished it in his own person, by offering himself (7:27; 9:14, 26). The phrase δι’ αὑτοῦ di’ hautou, “through himself,” makes that explicit. The Levitical high priest entered the sanctuary “with blood not his own” (9:25); this priest is both the one who offers and the offering.

Textual Note

The manuscripts vary slightly in this clause. The words “through himself” are present in the text followed here (the SBL Greek New Testament), with early support, but are absent from some important manuscripts, and the Nestle-Aland edition omits them. Many later manuscripts also add “our” (“purification of our sins”), which is the reading behind older English versions such as “by himself purged our sins.” The addition of “our” is almost certainly secondary. The difference over “through himself” does not affect the meaning: even without the phrase, the middle-voice participle already expresses that the Son himself made the purification.

“Sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high.” Now comes the main verb: ἐκάθισεν ekathisen, “he sat down.” The words echo Psalm 110:1, which will be quoted in full at 1:13: “The utterance of the Lord to my lord: Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.” In the ancient world the right hand of a king was the place of highest honor and delegated authority (compare 1 Kgs 2:19, where Solomon seats his mother at his right hand). To sit at God’s right hand is to share God’s throne and rule. No angel is ever invited to sit in God’s presence; in the visions of Scripture the heavenly beings stand before God (Isa 6:2; Dan 7:10; Luke 1:19).

The act of sitting has a second significance in Hebrews, which the author will draw out later: “Every priest stands day after day ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But this one, having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, sat down at the right hand of God” (10:11–12). There were no chairs for the priests in the tabernacle; their work was never done. Christ sat down because his work of purification was finished. The order of the participle and the main verb in 1:3 expresses exactly this: having made purification, he sat down.

“The Majesty” (ἡ μεγαλωσύνη hē megalōsynē, “greatness, majesty”) is a reverent way of referring to God without naming him, in keeping with Jewish sensitivity about speaking God’s name directly. The same expression returns at 8:1: “we have such a high priest, who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens.” “On high” (ἐν ὑψηλοῖς en hypsēlois, literally “in the high places”) means in heaven, God’s dwelling, above all creation.

Read as a whole, this verse presents Christ in the three roles by which God’s people were led under the old covenant, an observation made by Calvin and many after him. He is the prophet in whom God has spoken his final word (1:1–2), the priest who has made purification for sins, and the king enthroned at God’s right hand. The rest of Hebrews will show that he fulfills and surpasses every prophet, priest, and king who came before him. For the struggling hearers, this verse also answers the question of where their security lies. The universe is held by his word; their sins have been cleansed by his self-offering; and the one who did this now reigns. They need not look anywhere else.

1:4

having become as much better than the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs.

The great opening sentence ends with a participle phrase that serves as a bridge to the rest of the chapter. It introduces two subjects that 1:5–14 will develop: the angels, and the “name” the Son has inherited.

The construction is a balanced comparison: τοσούτῳ … ὅσῳ tosoutō … hosō, “by so much … as,” or “as much … as.” The Son’s superiority to the angels corresponds exactly to the superiority of his name over theirs. The point of the comparison is that the measure of his greatness can be read off from the name he bears: if his name is incomparably greater, so is he.

Word Study: κρείττων kreittōn, “better”

This comparative adjective, “better, superior, more excellent,” appears here for the first time in Hebrews, and it is one of the book’s signature words. It occurs thirteen times in Hebrews, more than in the rest of the New Testament combined. The author speaks of “better things” belonging to salvation (6:9), a “better hope” (7:19), a “better covenant” (7:22; 8:6) enacted on “better promises” (8:6), “better sacrifices” (9:23), a “better possession” (10:34), a “better country” (11:16), a “better resurrection” (11:35), “something better” for us (11:40), and blood that speaks “better” than the blood of Abel (12:24). The word does not imply that the old order was bad. It implies that the old was good but provisional, and that the new is superior because it is the reality to which the old pointed. Here at its first use, “better” is applied to the Son himself, which is the root of every other “better” in the book: the covenant, the sacrifice, the hope are better because he is better.

“Having become.” The participle γενόμενος genomenos, from γίνομαι ginomai, “become,” is aorist and describes something that happened. This raises a natural question. If the Son is eternally the radiance of God’s glory and the agent of creation (1:2–3), how can he have “become” better than the angels? Was he not always superior to them?

The answer lies in the movement of the sentence and in what the author will say in chapter 2. The participle follows directly on “he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high.” It refers to the Son’s exaltation after he had made purification for sins. In order to make that purification he had to share our flesh and blood (2:14); in that state he was “made for a little while lower than the angels” (2:9, quoting Ps 8:5). Now, having accomplished his work, he has been exalted as the incarnate, crucified, and risen one to the place of supreme authority. What he always was by nature, he has now become in his public, historical status as the God-man and Messiah. Paul expresses the same pattern: the one who was “in the form of God” emptied himself and became obedient to death, and “therefore God highly exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name” (Phil 2:6–9). Hebrews is not teaching that Jesus was a mere man promoted to divine status, a view flatly excluded by 1:2–3 and 1:10–12; it is teaching that the eternal Son, having humbled himself, was exalted in his humanity to the throne.

“The angels.” The Greek ἄγγελος angelos, like the Hebrew מַלְאָךְ mal’akh, basically means “messenger.” Both words can be used of human messengers, but in this chapter they refer to the heavenly beings who serve God. The word appears eleven times in Hebrews 1–2, and the comparison between the Son and the angels runs through both chapters. The reason will become clear in 2:2: the law given at Sinai was “the word spoken through angels.” The angels were regarded as the mediators of the old covenant revelation. By showing that the Son is far superior to them, the author shows that the word spoken through the Son is far weightier than the word spoken through them, and so the consequences of neglecting it are far graver (2:1–4).

“The name he has inherited.” The verb κεκληρονόμηκεν keklēronomēken, “he has inherited,” is in the perfect tense, which in Greek describes a past action with continuing results: he received the name, and he holds it still. It picks up the word “heir” from 1:2. In the biblical world, a name was not a mere label; it expressed a person’s identity, status, and character. To inherit a name was to enter into the rank and rights that the name conferred. The adjective διαφορώτερον diaphorōteron, “more excellent,” is the comparative of διάφορος diaphoros, which means “different” and so “outstanding, superior.” Hebrews will use it again of Christ’s “more excellent ministry” (8:6). The phrase παρ’ αὐτούς par’ autous, “than they,” uses a preposition that Hebrews often employs for comparisons: literally “beside them,” and so “in comparison with them.”

Interpretive Options: What Is “the Name”?

  • “Son.” The very next verse asks, “To which of the angels did he ever say, ‘You are my Son’?” (1:5). The word “for” (γάρ gar) at the start of 1:5 shows that the quotations are meant to explain the superiority of the name. Most commentators therefore conclude that the name is “Son.”
  • “Lord” or the divine name. Some, noting Philippians 2:9–11 (“the name above every name … that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord”), suggest that the name is “Lord,” the Greek equivalent of the divine name YHWH, which the Son is given in 1:10.

Evaluation: The immediate context strongly favors “Son.” But “Son” here should be heard in its full weight. The catena that follows does not stop with “Son”; it goes on to address the Son as “God” (1:8) and as “Lord,” the Creator (1:10). Angels are sometimes called “sons of God” collectively in the Old Testament (Job 1:6; 38:7), but no angel is ever singled out and addressed by God as “my Son,” enthroned at God’s right hand, and given the name and honor of God himself. The name the Son has inherited is “Son” in that unique, royal, and divine sense.

With this verse the preacher has stated his theme and laid the foundation for everything that follows. The God who spoke in the prophets has spoken finally in a Son, who is heir, creator, the radiance and imprint of God, the sustainer of all, the purifier of sins, the enthroned king, and the bearer of a name above the angels. The hearers are now ready for the proof from Scripture.

For Reflection

  1. Hebrews begins not with human questions about God but with the fact that God has spoken. How might your approach to Scripture change if you consistently received it as God himself speaking to you?
  2. The earlier revelation came “in many portions and in many ways,” but the final word has come in a Son. In what ways do you see the Old Testament pointing forward to Christ, and how does knowing Christ help you read the Old Testament?
  3. The Son is “heir of all things” and “bears all things by the word of his power.” What anxieties in your life look different when you remember who holds the world and its future?
  4. The one who is the radiance of God’s glory “made purification for sins through himself.” What does it mean to you that the cleansing of your conscience was accomplished by God’s own Son, personally and completely?
  5. Christ “sat down” because his work of purification was finished. Are there ways in which you still live as though your standing before God depended on work that remains to be done?
