8:7

For if that first covenant had been faultless, no place would have been sought for a second.

The preacher now explains (γάρ gar, “for”) why the covenant Christ mediates is better. His argument takes a familiar shape. Once again he uses a contrary-to-fact condition: Εἰ γὰρ ἡ πρώτη ἐκείνη ἦν ἄμεμπτος, οὐκ ἂν δευτέρας ἐζητεῖτο τόπος Ei gar hē prōtē ekeinē ēn amemptos, ouk an deuteras ezēteito topos, “for if that first had been faultless, no place would have been sought for a second.” As in 8:4, the grammar signals that the condition is untrue: the first covenant was not faultless, and a place was sought for a second.

The noun “covenant” is not expressed in the Greek; the feminine adjectives “first” (πρώτη prōtē) and “second” (δευτέρα deutera) agree with the feminine noun διαθήκη diathēkē from 8:6, so the translation supplies it. This is the first time Hebrews calls the Sinai covenant “the first”; the label recurs in 8:13, 9:1, 9:15, and 9:18. The word “that” (ἐκείνη ekeinē) adds a note of distance: “that first covenant,” the one belonging to an earlier stage of God’s dealings.

The key word is ἄμεμπτος amemptos, “faultless, blameless,” formed from the negative prefix a- and the verb μέμφομαι memphomai, “to find fault, to blame,” which appears in the very next verse. The same adjective is used of Zechariah and Elizabeth, who walked “blameless in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord” (Luke 1:6), and by Paul of his former life, “as to righteousness under the law, blameless” (Phil 3:6). To say the first covenant was not “faultless” does not mean that it was sinful, or that God made a mistake in giving it. Paul insists that “the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good” (Rom 7:12). Hebrews itself treats the old covenant as God’s own word, spoken through Moses and the prophets (1:1; 2:2; 3:5). The “fault” lay in its insufficiency to accomplish the final purpose of God: to bring sinners to perfection, cleanse their consciences, and give them permanent access to his presence. The preacher has already said it plainly: “a former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness, for the law made nothing perfect, but on the other hand a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God” (7:18–19). Paul locates the weakness in the same place: “what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending his own Son” (Rom 8:3). The law could command, but not change the heart; it could provide sacrifices that pointed to forgiveness, but not sacrifices that could “take away sins” (10:4).

The phrase “no place would have been sought” (οὐκ ἂν… ἐζητεῖτο τόπος ouk an… ezēteito topos) is idiomatic: there would have been no room, no occasion, for a second covenant. The passive “would have been sought” implicitly has God as its subject: it was God who “sought a place” for a second covenant when he spoke through Jeremiah.

This verse uses the same kind of reasoning the preacher has used twice already, and will use again. In 4:8 he argued, “if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day after these things.” In 7:11 he argued, “if perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood… what further need would there have been for another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek?” In 10:1–2 he will argue that if the sacrifices could perfect the worshipers, they would have ceased to be offered. In each case the logic is the same: when God himself, at a later point in redemptive history, announces something new, he thereby shows that what came before was not final. The Old Testament itself bears witness to its own provisional character. This is not an argument imposed on the Hebrew Scriptures from outside; it is drawn from the forward-looking promises within them. The preacher’s hearers did not need to choose between Moses and Jesus as if they were rivals. They needed to see that Moses and the prophets pointed to Jesus.

8:8

For, finding fault with them, he says: “Behold, days are coming, says the Lord, when I will accomplish a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah,

The preacher now introduces Jeremiah’s oracle with a participle that picks up the key idea of 8:7: μεμφόμενος γὰρ αὐτοὺς λέγει memphomenos gar autous legei, “for, finding fault with them, he says.” The subject of “he says” is God. Throughout Hebrews, Scripture is quoted as the living voice of God speaking in the present (“he says,” present tense), not merely as words written long ago (compare 1:6–7; 3:7; 10:5). Jeremiah was the human messenger, but the preacher hears God himself speaking.

Who is it that God finds fault with? According to the Greek text printed here, the object is αὐτούς autous, “them,” that is, the people of Israel. This is important. Verse 7 might have left the impression that the fault lay in the covenant as such; verse 8 clarifies that the fault God found was with the people who lived under it. The quotation itself confirms this immediately: “because they did not continue in my covenant” (8:9). The problem with the first covenant was not that its commandments were wrong, but that it was made with a people whose hearts were not changed, and it did not itself provide the power to change them. As Moses lamented at the end of the wilderness years, “the Lord has not given you a heart to know, or eyes to see, or ears to hear, to this day” (Deut 29:4 [Hebrew 29:3]).

Textual Note: “with them” or “to them”?

The Greek manuscripts are divided between αὐτούς autous (accusative, “them”) and αὐτοῖς autois (dative, “to them”). With the accusative, the natural sense is “finding fault with them, he says.” With the dative, one could read either “finding fault with them” (since the verb can also take a dative object) or “finding fault [with it, the first covenant], he says to them.” Both readings have strong support, and modern editions (including the text used in this study) generally print the accusative. Whichever is original, the meaning is not greatly affected, because the quotation that follows explicitly places the blame on the people: “they did not continue in my covenant.” The weakness of the first covenant and the failure of the people belong together: the covenant was weak precisely because it could not overcome the people’s unfaithfulness.

Then the quotation begins. Jeremiah 31:31–34 (numbered 38:31–34 in the Greek Old Testament, which arranges Jeremiah’s chapters differently) is the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. The preacher quotes it in full because every line will matter for his argument in chapters 9 and 10.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:31

Hebrew: הִנֵּה יָמִים בָּאִים נְאֻם־יְהוָה וְכָרַתִּי אֶת־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶת־בֵּית יְהוּדָה בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה׃ — hinneh yamim ba’im ne’um-YHWH vekharatti et-beit yisra’el ve’et-beit yehudah berit chadashah, “Behold, days are coming, declares the Lord, when I will cut with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah a new covenant.”

The Hebrew idiom is to “cut” (כָּרַת karat) a covenant, probably reflecting the ancient ceremony in which animals were cut in two and the covenant parties passed between the pieces (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18–20). The phrase “new covenant” (בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה berit chadashah) appears only here in the Hebrew Bible, though the idea is present elsewhere: an “everlasting covenant” (Jer 32:40; Ezek 37:26), a “covenant of peace” (Isa 54:10; Ezek 34:25), and the gift of a new heart and a new spirit (Ezek 36:26–27). The Hebrew adjective chadash can mean either “brand new” or “renewed” (it is related to chodesh, the “new moon,” which renews each month). But Jeremiah himself rules out a mere renewal of the old: this covenant will be “not like the covenant I made with their fathers” (31:32).

The Greek of Hebrews follows the Septuagint closely, with a few small differences of wording. Where the Hebrew says “I will cut” and the Septuagint, as generally printed, says “I will make” (diathēsomai, the verb related to “covenant”), Hebrews has συντελέσω syntelesō, “I will accomplish, bring to completion.” Hebrews also says “with” using the preposition epi with the accusative (“upon, for, with the house of Israel”). The Greek “says the Lord” (λέγει κύριος legei kyrios) renders the solemn Hebrew prophetic formula ne’um YHWH, “utterance of the Lord.” Here “Lord” (kyrios) stands for the divine name YHWH.

“Behold, days are coming” (Ἰδοὺ ἡμέραι ἔρχονται Idou hēmerai erchontai) is a characteristic formula in Jeremiah for God’s future intervention, sometimes in judgment (Jer 7:32; 19:6) and often in salvation: the days when God will raise up for David “a righteous Branch” (23:5; 33:14–15), when he will restore the fortunes of his people (30:3), when Jerusalem will be rebuilt (31:38). In the context of Hebrews, these coming days have arrived: God has spoken “in these last days” by his Son (1:2), and Christ has appeared “at the end of the ages” (9:26).

The verb συντελέσω syntelesō, “I will accomplish,” is worth pausing over. It means to bring something to its end, to complete or fulfill it. Whether or not the preacher chose it deliberately, it fits his theology beautifully. The new covenant is not one more attempt alongside the old, but the accomplishment of what God had purposed all along. The related noun appears in 9:26: Christ appeared “at the consummation (συντελείᾳ synteleia) of the ages.” The new covenant belongs to the consummation.

The “new” covenant is καινήν kainēn. The adjective kainos often carries the sense of “new in kind or quality,” as in a “new creation” (2 Cor 5:17) or a “new heaven and new earth” (Rev 21:1), in contrast to another Greek word for “new,” neos, which more often means “new in time, recent, young.” The distinction should not be pressed too hard, because the two words overlap, and Hebrews uses neos of the same covenant in 12:24 (“the mediator of a new covenant”). But in Jeremiah’s oracle, the covenant is new above all because it is different in character from the old: “not like” it.

The covenant is made “with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah.” In Jeremiah’s day the people had long been divided: the northern kingdom (Israel) had been carried into exile by Assyria in 722 BC, and the southern kingdom (Judah) was now going into exile in Babylon. The promise of a new covenant with both houses is a promise that God will reunite and restore his whole people (compare Jer 3:18; 30:3; Ezek 37:15–28). The new covenant is first of all God’s promise to Israel. The New Testament never forgets this. Jesus, the Messiah of Israel, inaugurated it at a Passover meal with twelve Jewish apostles, representing the twelve tribes (Luke 22:14–20). Through him, Gentiles who were once “strangers to the covenants of promise” (Eph 2:12) have been brought near and share in the blessings of Abraham (Gal 3:14, 29), grafted into Israel’s olive tree (Rom 11:17–24). The new covenant does not create a different people of God from the one God called in Abraham; it fulfills God’s promises to Israel in Israel’s Messiah and opens them to the nations, as God promised Abraham from the beginning (Gen 12:3). Paul, quoting Isaiah alongside the language of Jeremiah’s new covenant, still anticipates a day when “all Israel will be saved,” for “this is my covenant with them, when I take away their sins” (Rom 11:26–27).

8:9

not like the covenant that I made with their fathers on the day when I took them by the hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt; because they did not continue in my covenant, and I disregarded them, says the Lord.

The new covenant is defined first by what it is not: οὐ κατὰ τὴν διαθήκην ἣν ἐποίησα τοῖς πατράσιν αὐτῶν ou kata tēn diathēkēn hēn epoiēsa tois patrasin autōn, “not like the covenant that I made with their fathers.” The covenant in view is the one made at Sinai after the exodus (Exod 19–24), with its law, priesthood, tabernacle, and sacrifices. It is identified by its occasion: ἐν ἡμέρᾳ ἐπιλαβομένου μου τῆς χειρὸς αὐτῶν ἐξαγαγεῖν αὐτοὺς ἐκ γῆς Αἰγύπτου en hēmera epilabomenou mou tēs cheiros autōn exagagein autous ek gēs Aigyptou, literally “in the day of my taking hold of their hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt.”

The image is tender. God took Israel by the hand, as a parent takes a small child’s hand, and led them out of slavery. Hosea uses the same picture: “When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son… I taught Ephraim to walk; I took them up by their arms” (Hos 11:1, 3). The first covenant was not a cold legal arrangement; it was the covenant of a redeeming God who had already saved his people before he gave them his law (Exod 20:2, “I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery”). The verb ἐπιλαμβάνομαι epilambanomai, “to take hold of,” is the same verb used in Hebrews 2:16: “for surely it is not angels that he takes hold of, but he takes hold of the offspring of Abraham.” The God who took Israel by the hand at the exodus has taken hold of Abraham’s children again, in the incarnation of his Son, to lead them out of a greater bondage (2:14–15).

Then comes the reason the new covenant must be different: ὅτι αὐτοὶ οὐκ ἐνέμειναν ἐν τῇ διαθήκῃ μου hoti autoi ouk enemeinan en tē diathēkē mou, “because they did not continue in my covenant.” The pronoun “they” (αὐτοί autoi) is emphatic in Greek: they, the people, were the ones who failed. The verb ἐμμένω emmenō, “to remain in, to abide by, to persevere in,” is the same verb the Greek Old Testament uses in the covenant curse of Deuteronomy 27:26, which Paul quotes: “cursed is everyone who does not continue in all the things written in the book of the law, to do them” (Gal 3:10). Israel’s history from the golden calf at the foot of Sinai (Exod 32) onward was a history of failure to continue. Jeremiah himself charged that “the house of Israel and the house of Judah have broken my covenant that I made with their fathers” (Jer 11:10).

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:32

Hebrew: לֹא כַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר כָּרַתִּי אֶת־אֲבוֹתָם בְּיוֹם הֶחֱזִיקִי בְיָדָם לְהוֹצִיאָם מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם אֲשֶׁר־הֵמָּה הֵפֵרוּ אֶת־בְּרִיתִי וְאָנֹכִי בָּעַלְתִּי בָם נְאֻם־יְהוָה׃ — lo khabberit asher karatti et-avotam beyom hecheziqi veyadam lehotsi’am me’erets mitsrayim asher-hemmah heferu et-beriti ve’anokhi ba’alti vam ne’um-YHWH, “not like the covenant that I cut with their fathers on the day I took hold of their hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt—my covenant which they broke, although I was a husband to them, declares the Lord.”

Two differences between the Hebrew and the Greek (which Hebrews follows) are worth noting.

“They broke” and “they did not continue.” The Hebrew says the fathers “broke” (הֵפֵרוּ heferu, from parar, “to break, annul, frustrate”) God’s covenant. The Greek says they “did not continue in” it. The meaning is essentially the same: the people abandoned the covenant obligations. The Hebrew is more forceful (they shattered it), while the Greek describes the same reality as a failure to persevere—a word that resonates with Hebrews’ constant call to endure and hold fast (3:6, 14; 10:23, 36).

“I was a husband to them” and “I disregarded them.” This is the more significant difference. The Hebrew reads וְאָנֹכִי בָּעַלְתִּי בָם ve’anokhi ba’alti vam, “although I was a husband to them” or “although I was master over them.” The verb ba’al means to be lord or owner, and especially to be a husband (the noun ba’al can mean “husband”). Jeremiah uses the same expression in 3:14: “Return, O faithless children, declares the Lord, for I am a husband to you (בָּעַלְתִּי בָכֶם ba’alti vakhem).” The Hebrew therefore stresses the contrast between Israel’s unfaithfulness and the Lord’s faithfulness: Israel broke the covenant like an adulterous wife, though the Lord had been a faithful husband. This marriage imagery runs throughout the prophets (Jer 2:2; 3:1–20; Hos 2; Ezek 16).

The Greek translation reads κἀγὼ ἠμέλησα αὐτῶν kagō ēmelēsa autōn, “and I disregarded them” (or “I ceased to care for them, I neglected them”). How the Greek translators arrived at this is uncertain. They may have read a Hebrew text with a similar-looking verb (such as ga’alti, “I loathed,” since the Hebrew letters for b and g are similar in shape), or they may have interpreted the rare expression in light of its consequence. Either way, the Greek describes God’s response to the broken covenant: he withdrew his protective care and handed them over to exile. This, too, is thoroughly biblical: “the Lord has rejected and forsaken the generation of his wrath” (Jer 7:29); “you are not my people, and I am not your God” (Hos 1:9).

The two readings are not contradictory; they are two sides of the same history. Israel broke the covenant although the Lord was a faithful husband (Hebrew), and therefore the Lord gave them over to the consequences of their unfaithfulness (Greek). The preacher’s point stands securely on either text: the first covenant ended in the people’s failure and in God’s judgment, and so a new covenant was needed that would not end that way.

The verb in the Greek, ἀμελέω ameleō, “to neglect, to disregard,” has a sharp echo elsewhere in Hebrews that the first hearers would not have missed. In 2:3 the preacher warned them, “how shall we escape if we neglect (ἀμελήσαντες amelēsantes) so great a salvation?” The fathers did not continue in God’s covenant, and God “neglected” them. The readers are now in danger of neglecting a far greater salvation. The quotation from Jeremiah is therefore not only an argument about covenants; it is a mirror held up to the congregation. The generation of the exodus was redeemed by God’s mighty hand and yet failed to continue (as chapters 3–4 have already shown from Psalm 95). The question for the readers is whether they will continue.

This verse also guards against misunderstanding. The new covenant is not needed because God failed in the old. The Hebrew text especially emphasizes that the Lord was faithful as a husband. The new covenant is needed because the people failed, and because the first covenant, for all its glory, did not provide the inward remedy for that failure. The solution, as the next verse shows, will come from God’s side.

8:10

For this is the covenant that I will covenant with the house of Israel after those days, says the Lord: putting my laws into their mind, I will also write them upon their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.

Having said what the new covenant is not, God now says what it is: ὅτι αὕτη ἡ διαθήκη ἣν διαθήσομαι τῷ οἴκῳ Ἰσραὴλ μετὰ τὰς ἡμέρας ἐκείνας hoti hautē hē diathēkē hēn diathēsomai tō oikō Israēl meta tas hēmeras ekeinas, “for this is the covenant that I will covenant with the house of Israel after those days.” The Greek repeats the root (“the covenant that I will covenant”), as the Hebrew does in its own idiom (“the covenant that I will cut”). Here only “the house of Israel” is named; it stands for the whole reunited people promised in 8:8. “After those days” refers to the time following the judgment and exile; in the flow of Hebrews, it points to the “last days” in which God has spoken by his Son (1:2).

Now come the “better promises” (8:6), and every verb has God as its subject. In the Sinai covenant the people said, “All that the Lord has spoken we will do” (Exod 19:8; 24:3, 7). In the new covenant, God says, “I will.”

The first promise: the law written on the heart. διδοὺς νόμους μου εἰς τὴν διάνοιαν αὐτῶν, καὶ ἐπὶ καρδίας αὐτῶν ἐπιγράψω αὐτούς didous nomous mou eis tēn dianoian autōn, kai epi kardias autōn epigrapsō autous, “putting my laws into their mind, I will also write them upon their hearts.” The participle “putting” (literally “giving”) and the finite verb “I will write” describe one action from two angles. The “mind” (διάνοια dianoia, “understanding, thinking”) and the “hearts” (καρδίαι kardiai) together describe the whole inner person. In biblical language the heart is not merely the seat of emotion but the center of thought, will, and devotion (Prov 4:23). When Hebrews quotes this verse again in 10:16, it reverses the order (“putting my laws upon their hearts, and upon their minds I will write them”), showing that the two terms are parallel.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:33

Hebrew: כִּי זֹאת הַבְּרִית אֲשֶׁר אֶכְרֹת אֶת־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל אַחֲרֵי הַיָּמִים הָהֵם נְאֻם־יְהוָה נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם וְעַל־לִבָּם אֶכְתֲּבֶנָּה וְהָיִיתִי לָהֶם לֵאלֹהִים וְהֵמָּה יִהְיוּ־לִי לְעָם׃ — ki zot habberit asher ekhrot et-beit yisra’el acharei hayyamim hahem ne’um-YHWH natatti et-torati beqirbam ve’al-libbam ekhtevennah vehayiti lahem lelohim vehemmah yihyu-li le’am, “For this is the covenant that I will cut with the house of Israel after those days, declares the Lord: I will put my Torah within them, and upon their heart I will write it; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.”

The Hebrew has the singular “my Torah” (תּוֹרָתִי torati, “my instruction, my law”), while the Greek has the plural “my laws” (nomous). The Hebrew “within them” (בְּקִרְבָּם beqirbam, “in their inward part”) is rendered in the Greek as “into their mind.” Neither difference changes the meaning: in both, it is God’s own law—not a different law—that is written inwardly.

The background is the giving of the law at Sinai, when God gave Moses “two tablets of the testimony, tablets of stone, written by the finger of God” (Exod 31:18; compare 32:15–16; 34:1). The law was holy, but it stood outside the people, written on stone, while their hearts remained unchanged. Jeremiah had described the tragic result with a stunning image: “The sin of Judah is written with a pen of iron; with a point of diamond it is engraved on the tablet of their heart” (Jer 17:1). In the new covenant, what is engraved on the heart will no longer be sin but God’s law.

It is crucial to notice what is new here and what is not. The content of God’s will is not new; it is “my laws,” the same holy will of God revealed in the Torah. What is new is the location and the power: the law will be written not on stone tablets but on hearts, so that God’s people will love and do his will from within. Even this was anticipated in the old covenant itself. Moses commanded, “these words that I command you today shall be on your heart” (Deut 6:6), and called Israel to “circumcise the foreskin of your heart” (Deut 10:16). But he also foresaw that after their failure and exile, God himself would do what they had not done: “the Lord your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your offspring, to love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul, so that you may live” (Deut 30:6; Hebrew וּמָל יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ אֶת־לְבָבְךָ umal YHWH elohekha et-levavkha). The command of Deuteronomy 10:16 becomes the promise of Deuteronomy 30:6, and that promise is the heart of Jeremiah’s new covenant. Ezekiel adds that God will accomplish it by his Spirit: “I will give you a new heart… I will put my Spirit within you and cause you to walk in my statutes” (Ezek 36:26–27). The New Testament announces the fulfillment: the Spirit writes on “tablets of human hearts” (2 Cor 3:3), so that “the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit” (Rom 8:4).

There is a beautiful Christ-centered dimension here as well. In Psalm 40, which Hebrews will quote in 10:5–7 as the words of Christ coming into the world, the speaker continues: “I delight to do your will, O my God; your Torah is within my heart” (Ps 40:8 [Hebrew 40:9], וְתוֹרָתְךָ בְּתוֹךְ מֵעָי vetoratekha betokh me’ay, literally “within my inward parts”). The one perfectly obedient Son, in whom God’s law was wholly inward, is the one who by his sacrifice writes that law on the hearts of his people.

The second promise: the covenant relationship. καὶ ἔσομαι αὐτοῖς εἰς θεόν καὶ αὐτοὶ ἔσονταί μοι εἰς λαόν kai esomai autois eis theon kai autoi esontai moi eis laon, “and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.” This is the “covenant formula” that runs through the whole Bible. God promised it to Abraham: “to be God to you and to your offspring after you” (Gen 17:7). He declared it at the exodus: “I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God” (Exod 6:7). He attached it to his dwelling among them: “I will walk among you and will be your God, and you shall be my people” (Lev 26:12). The prophets repeated it as the goal of restoration (Jer 24:7; 30:22; 32:38; Ezek 37:27). And the Bible ends with its complete fulfillment: “Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man… they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God” (Rev 21:3). This element of the new covenant is, therefore, not new at all in its goal. It is the one great purpose of God from Abraham to the new creation. What is new is that it will be realized: a people whose hearts are made new will actually be the people of God in truth.

This should shape how we speak of “old” and “new.” The new covenant does not replace God’s purpose; it fulfills it. The God of the new covenant is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and his purpose is the same: a people for himself, in whose midst he dwells.

8:11

And they shall by no means teach, each one his fellow citizen and each one his brother, saying, ‘Know the Lord,’ because all will know me, from the least to the greatest of them.

The third promise: universal, personal knowledge of God. The verse begins with a strong negation: καὶ οὐ μὴ διδάξωσιν kai ou mē didaxōsin, “and they shall by no means teach.” The Greek combination of two negatives, ou mē, with the aorist subjunctive is the most emphatic way of denying a future possibility: “they shall certainly not.” The occasion that is ruled out is a situation in which members of the covenant people must urge one another, Γνῶθι τὸν κύριον Gnōthi ton kyrion, “Know the Lord!”—because some of them do not know him.

Those who will no longer need such exhortation are described as ἕκαστος τὸν πολίτην αὐτοῦ καὶ ἕκαστος τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ hekastos ton politēn autou kai hekastos ton adelphon autou, “each one his fellow citizen and each one his brother.” The Greek πολίτης politēs, “fellow citizen,” renders the Hebrew רֵעֵהוּ re’ehu, “his neighbor, his companion.” (Many later Greek manuscripts substitute the more common word plēsion, “neighbor,” which is why some older English versions read “neighbor” here.) The reason given is ὅτι πάντες εἰδήσουσίν με ἀπὸ μικροῦ ἕως μεγάλου αὐτῶν hoti pantes eidēsousin me apo mikrou heōs megalou autōn, “because all will know me, from the least to the greatest of them.”

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:34a

Hebrew: וְלֹא יְלַמְּדוּ עוֹד אִישׁ אֶת־רֵעֵהוּ וְאִישׁ אֶת־אָחִיו לֵאמֹר דְּעוּ אֶת־יְהוָה כִּי־כוּלָּם יֵדְעוּ אוֹתִי לְמִקְטַנָּם וְעַד־גְּדוֹלָם נְאֻם־יְהוָה — velo yelammedu od ish et-re’ehu ve’ish et-achiv lemor de’u et-YHWH ki-khullam yed’u oti lemiqtannam ve’ad-gedolam ne’um-YHWH, “And they shall no longer teach, each man his neighbor and each man his brother, saying, ‘Know the Lord,’ for all of them shall know me, from the least of them to the greatest of them, declares the Lord.”

The Greek follows the Hebrew closely. In the Hebrew, “Know the Lord” uses the divine name YHWH, rendered in Greek by kyrios, “Lord.” The phrase “from the least to the greatest” deliberately reverses an earlier oracle of judgment in Jeremiah: “from the least of them to the greatest of them, everyone is greedy for unjust gain; from prophet to priest, everyone deals falsely” (Jer 6:13; compare 8:10). Where once corruption ran through every level of society, in the new covenant the knowledge of God will run through every level.

In the Bible, to “know” God (Hebrew יָדַע yada’) is far more than knowing facts about him. It is personal, relational, covenantal knowledge that issues in trust, love, and obedience. Hosea lamented, “there is no faithfulness or steadfast love, and no knowledge of God in the land” (Hos 4:1), and “my people are destroyed for lack of knowledge” (Hos 4:6). Jeremiah himself defined the knowledge of God in practical terms when he said of King Josiah, “He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the Lord” (Jer 22:16). The knowledge promised in the new covenant is the heart-knowledge that the written-on-the-heart law (8:10) produces.

Under the first covenant, the people of Israel as a whole were in covenant with God, but many among them did not truly know him. Knowledge of God’s will was mediated through priests (Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7) and prophets, and the covenant community was a mixture of the faithful and the faithless; the generation in the wilderness was a sobering example (Heb 3:16–19). The prophets repeatedly called the people back to a knowledge they lacked. In the new covenant, by contrast, every member will know the Lord personally. Isaiah foresaw it: “all your children shall be taught by the Lord” (Isa 54:13; Hebrew וְכָל־בָּנַיִךְ לִמּוּדֵי יְהוָה vekhol-banayikh limmudei YHWH), a verse Jesus applied to those who come to him (John 6:45). Joel promised that God would pour out his Spirit on all flesh, sons and daughters, young and old, servants and handmaids (Joel 2:28–29; Acts 2:16–18). John writes to ordinary believers, “you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you all know” (1 John 2:20).

Does this verse mean that the new covenant has no place for teachers? Certainly not. Hebrews itself rebukes its readers because “by this time you ought to be teachers” (5:12), urges them to remember their leaders “who spoke to you the word of God” (13:7), and commands obedience to those who watch over their souls (13:17). Christ gave teachers to his church (Eph 4:11), and the apostles commanded that the faith be taught and passed on (Matt 28:20; 2 Tim 2:2). What the promise excludes is not teaching about the Lord, but the situation in which a covenant member does not know the Lord at all and must be urged to begin. It also excludes any order in which knowledge of God belongs to a privileged class—priest, scribe, or elite—while the rest of the people stand at a distance. In Christ, every believer, “from the least to the greatest,” has direct access to God (4:16; 10:19–22).

Readers of Hebrews may ask how this promise relates to the preacher’s solemn warnings (6:4–8; 10:26–31), which assume that some within the visible congregation may fall away. The promise describes the covenant as God will finally and perfectly realize it: a people who truly know him. The warnings are addressed to a visible community in which the genuineness of each person’s participation is proven by perseverance (“we are his house, if we hold fast,” 3:6; “we have come to share in Christ, if we hold our original confidence firm to the end,” 3:14). Both belong to the preacher’s message: the new covenant guarantees that God’s people will know him, and the evidence that one belongs to that people is continuing in faith.

8:12

For I will be merciful toward their unrighteous deeds, and their sins I will by no means remember any longer.”

The fourth promise: definitive forgiveness. The final promise is introduced by ὅτι hoti, “for, because.” This small word is very important. It indicates that the forgiveness of sins is not simply the last item in a list but the ground of all the others. God will write his law on their hearts, be their God, and be known by all of them, because he will forgive their sins. Forgiveness is the foundation on which the whole new covenant stands. That is why, when the preacher returns to this oracle at the end of the section, he quotes only the promise of the inward law and this promise of forgiveness, and then draws the conclusion: “where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin” (10:16–18).

The first clause is ἵλεως ἔσομαι ταῖς ἀδικίαις αὐτῶν hileōs esomai tais adikiais autōn, “I will be merciful toward their unrighteous deeds.” The adjective ἵλεως hileōs, “merciful, gracious, propitious,” belongs to the same word family as ἱλάσκομαι hilaskomai, “to make propitiation” (2:17), and ἱλαστήριον hilastērion, “the mercy seat” (9:5), the lid of the ark where the blood of atonement was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement. The tax collector in Jesus’ parable prays with the related verb: “God, be merciful (propitious) to me, the sinner” (Luke 18:13). God’s mercy toward sin is not indifference to sin; it is a mercy that comes through atonement. In Hebrews, that atonement is the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ, the high priest who made propitiation for the sins of the people (2:17). “Unrighteous deeds” (ἀδικίαι adikiai, plural) describes sins as violations of what is right.

The second clause is even stronger: καὶ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν αὐτῶν οὐ μὴ μνησθῶ ἔτι kai tōn hamartiōn autōn ou mē mnēsthō eti, “and their sins I will by no means remember any longer.” Again we meet the emphatic double negative ou mē (as in 8:11): God will certainly not remember their sins any more. The word “sins” is placed first in the Greek clause for emphasis.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:34b

Hebrew: כִּי אֶסְלַח לַעֲוֺנָם וּלְחַטָּאתָם לֹא אֶזְכָּר־עוֹד׃ — ki eslach la’avonam ulechatta’tam lo ezkar-od, “for I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more.”

The Hebrew verb סָלַח salach, “to forgive, to pardon,” is used in the Old Testament only with God as its subject; only God forgives in this sense (compare Mark 2:7). The Hebrew has the singular nouns “iniquity” (avon) and “sin” (chatta’t), used collectively; the Greek uses plurals (“unrighteous deeds,” “sins”). The Greek “I will be merciful” interprets “I will forgive” in terms of God’s gracious, propitious disposition toward sinners. Both say the same thing: God will pardon, fully and finally.

Textual Note

Many later manuscripts add “and their lawless deeds” (καὶ τῶν ἀνομιῶν αὐτῶν kai tōn anomiōn autōn) after “their sins,” which is why the phrase appears in some older English versions. The addition almost certainly comes from Hebrews 10:17, where the preacher himself includes it when he quotes this verse again. The earlier manuscripts and the Greek of Jeremiah lack it here.

What does it mean for God not to “remember” sins? It cannot mean that the all-knowing God suffers a lapse of memory. In Scripture, “remembering” is often active: to remember someone is to act toward them in light of what is remembered. “God remembered Noah” and caused the waters to subside (Gen 8:1); “God remembered his covenant” and acted to deliver Israel from Egypt (Exod 2:24). When God “remembers” sin, he acts in judgment (Hos 8:13; 9:9; Rev 16:19). So when he promises not to remember sins, he means he will never again hold them against his people or act toward them as sinners deserving judgment. David prayed, “Remember not the sins of my youth or my transgressions; according to your steadfast love remember me” (Ps 25:7). Isaiah heard God declare, “I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins” (Isa 43:25).

The promise acquires special depth in Hebrews because of the contrast the preacher will draw in chapter 10. The repeated sacrifices of the old covenant, especially the annual Day of Atonement, carried within them “a reminder of sins year by year” (ἀνάμνησις ἁμαρτιῶν anamnēsis hamartiōn, 10:3). Every year the ritual itself declared that sin had not yet been finally dealt with. In the new covenant, God promises the opposite: not an annual reminder, but a permanent forgetting. This is possible only because Christ “by a single offering has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14).

Here too we must speak carefully about what is new. Forgiveness was not unknown under the old covenant. God revealed himself to Moses as “the Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious… forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin” (Exod 34:6–7). The law repeatedly promised that when the priest made atonement, “it shall be forgiven them” (Lev 4:20, 26, 31, 35). David knew the blessedness of the one “whose transgression is forgiven” (Ps 32:1–2; compare Rom 4:6–8), and Micah rejoiced in the God who casts all our sins into the depths of the sea (Mic 7:18–19). Believers before Christ were truly forgiven. What is new in the new covenant is the ground and the finality of forgiveness. The animal sacrifices could not in themselves “take away sins” (10:4); they pointed forward to the sacrifice that could. Hebrews explains that Christ’s death brought “redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant” (9:15). Paul says the same: God had “passed over former sins” in his forbearance, and in the cross he showed his righteousness in doing so (Rom 3:25–26). The saints of the old covenant were forgiven on credit, so to speak, on the basis of a sacrifice yet to come; in the new covenant the price has been paid, the sacrifice offered once for all, and the conscience can be cleansed (9:14; 10:22).

8:13

In saying “new,” he has made the first old; and what is being made old and is growing aged is near to vanishing.

The quotation ends, and the preacher adds a brief, piercing comment. He seizes on a single word in Jeremiah’s oracle: “new.” ἐν τῷ λέγειν Καινὴν πεπαλαίωκεν τὴν πρώτην en tō legein Kainēn pepalaiōken tēn prōtēn, “in saying ‘new,’ he has made the first old.” The construction en tō legein, “in the saying,” means “by the very act of saying.” The mere use of the word “new” implies that there is an “old.” A covenant cannot be called new unless another is thereby marked as former. The preacher argues here, as he often does, from the precise wording of Scripture (compare his argument from the word “today” in 3:13–4:7, and from the silence of Genesis about Melchizedek’s genealogy in 7:3).

The subject of “he has made old” is God, the one who spoke through Jeremiah (“he says,” 8:8). The verb πεπαλαίωκεν pepalaiōken, from παλαιόω palaioō, “to make old, to declare old, to treat as obsolete,” is in the perfect tense. This is striking. The preacher does not say that Christ’s death made the first covenant old (although it did bring it to its end), nor that it will become old in the future. He says that God, in speaking through Jeremiah some six centuries earlier, already “has made” it old. From the moment the promise of a new covenant was spoken, the Sinai covenant was officially on notice. It continued to function, and faithful Israelites rightly continued to live under it, offering its sacrifices and keeping its feasts, until the new covenant came. But by God’s own declaration it was a covenant with a horizon, a covenant awaiting its successor.

The second half of the verse states a general principle and applies it: τὸ δὲ παλαιούμενον καὶ γηράσκον ἐγγὺς ἀφανισμοῦ to de palaioumenon kai gēraskon engys aphanismou, “and what is being made old and is growing aged is near to vanishing.” Two present participles describe an ongoing process. παλαιούμενον palaioumenon, “being made old, becoming obsolete,” uses the same verb as the first half of the verse. γηράσκον gēraskon, “growing aged,” is a word used especially of people growing old (as in John 21:18, “when you are old”). The image is of something nearing the end of its natural life. The same verb palaioō appeared in Hebrews 1:11, quoting Psalm 102 about the heavens and the earth: “they will all grow old like a garment” (Hebrew Ps 102:27, וְכֻלָּם כַּבֶּגֶד יִבְלוּ vekhullam kabbeged yivlu). There, the created order grows old while the Son remains; here, the first covenant grows old while the new covenant, mediated by the Son, endures. Everything except the Son and what he establishes is subject to aging.

Word Study: ἀφανισμός aphanismos

The noun ἀφανισμός aphanismos comes from the verb aphanizō, “to make unseen, to cause to disappear, to destroy.” It can mean simply “disappearance,” but it is also used in the Greek Old Testament for the ruin and desolation that come upon a land or city under judgment. The word occurs only here in the New Testament. The related verb is used by James for life itself, “a vapor that appears for a little while and then vanishes” (Jas 4:14). The preacher says that the first covenant is ἐγγύς engys, “near,” to this vanishing. He uses the same adverb in 6:8 of land that bears thorns and thistles, which is “near to a curse.”

What is “near to vanishing”? Strictly, it is the first covenant as an administration: its priesthood, sacrifices, sanctuary, and the regulations attached to them. The preacher will describe these as “regulations for the body imposed until the time of reformation” (9:10). With the coming of Christ, the time of reformation has arrived. The Levitical priesthood has been superseded by the priesthood of Melchizedek’s order (7:11–12, 18); the sacrifices have been fulfilled and ended by the one sacrifice (10:8–10, 18); the earthly sanctuary has given way to the heavenly (9:8–12, 24). “He takes away the first in order to establish the second” (10:9).

Historical Background: “Near to Vanishing” Before AD 70

If, as the evidence suggests, Hebrews was written in the mid-60s AD, then the temple in Jerusalem was still standing and its sacrifices were still being offered daily. Priests in their linen garments still stood at the altar; the high priest still entered the Most Holy Place once a year. To a Jewish Christian under pressure, these were impressive realities, sanctioned by centuries of history and by the law of God. The preacher’s words would have sounded bold: this whole system, though still visibly operating, has been declared old by God and is near to vanishing.

Within a few years, the words proved true in a way no one could miss. In AD 70, after a terrible siege, the Roman army under Titus captured Jerusalem and burned the temple (Josephus describes the catastrophe at length in Jewish War, book 6). The sacrifices ceased and have never resumed. Jesus himself had foretold it: “There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down” (Mark 13:2), and he had said that these things would come upon “this generation” (Mark 13:30; Matt 23:36; 24:34). Hebrews does not mention the temple’s destruction, which is one strong reason for dating it before AD 70; a writer arguing that the old system was obsolete could hardly have passed over its actual end in silence.

It would be a mistake, however, to make the preacher’s point depend on a political event. The first covenant became obsolete because God declared a new one and because Christ fulfilled it, not because Roman soldiers burned a building. The destruction of the temple was the outward, historical confirmation of what had already happened in heaven when Christ sat down at God’s right hand. The torn curtain at the moment of Jesus’ death (Mark 15:38) had already announced that the way into God’s presence was open by another route (Heb 10:19–20).

The pastoral implication is plain. The readers were tempted to return to, or to rest in, what was aging and about to vanish. The preacher says to them in effect: Why would you anchor your soul to something that God himself declared old six hundred years ago and that is now in its last days? Anchor yourself instead to the hope that enters within the curtain, where Jesus has gone as forerunner (6:19–20).

What Is New, and What Continues?

Verse 13 is sometimes misused to suggest that the Old Testament as a whole, or God’s dealings with Israel, or God’s moral law, has been discarded. The whole of Scripture, including Hebrews itself, shows that this is not what the preacher means. It is important to be precise.

What has become “old” and passes away:

  • The Sinai covenant as the governing administration of God’s people, with its specific terms and mediating institutions. This includes the Levitical priesthood (7:11–12, 18–19), the sacrifices of animals (10:1–10, 18), the earthly tabernacle and its rituals of access (9:1–10), and the purity and food regulations that served as “regulations for the body imposed until the time of reformation” (9:10; compare 13:9; Mark 7:19; Col 2:16–17). These were a “shadow of the good things to come” (10:1), and the shadow passes when the reality arrives.
  • The arrangement in which God’s law stood outside the people on tablets of stone, demanding obedience without itself supplying the power to obey (8:9–10; 2 Cor 3:3–11).

What continues, and is brought to fulfillment:

  • The God who speaks. The same God who “spoke to the fathers by the prophets” has now spoken in his Son (1:1–2). The new covenant is announced by the God of Sinai, and its promises are his promises.
  • The Scriptures of the old covenant. Hebrews builds its entire argument on the Old Testament, which it treats as the living voice of God (3:7; 4:12; 10:15). The old covenant is obsolete as an administration, but its Scriptures remain God’s word, “profitable for teaching” (2 Tim 3:16), and they testify to Christ (Luke 24:27, 44; John 5:39).
  • God’s moral will. Jeremiah says that what God writes on the heart is “my laws” (8:10). God’s holy will is not abolished but internalized. Jesus said he came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them (Matt 5:17); Paul says faith does not overthrow the law but upholds it (Rom 3:31), and that love fulfills its commands (Rom 13:8–10), so that believers are “not without the law of God but under the law of Christ” (1 Cor 9:21). Hebrews itself closes with concrete moral commands rooted in the Old Testament: brotherly love, hospitality, sexual purity, contentment, care for prisoners (13:1–6).
  • The promises to Abraham. God’s promise to Abraham, confirmed by an oath, is “unchangeable” (6:13–18). Paul argues that the law, which came 430 years later, could not annul that covenant (Gal 3:15–18). The new covenant does not replace the Abrahamic promise; it is the means by which the blessing of Abraham reaches all who belong to Christ, Abraham’s offspring (Gal 3:14, 16, 29). The covenant formula promised to Abraham (“to be God to you and to your offspring,” Gen 17:7) is the very promise of the new covenant (8:10).
  • The promises to David. God’s covenant with David (2 Sam 7:12–16), quoted in Hebrews 1:5, is fulfilled in Jesus, the Son enthroned forever (1:8–13).
  • The one people of God and the one way of salvation by faith. The faithful of the old covenant are “a great cloud of witnesses” (12:1) whose faith is held up as the model for believers (11:1–40), and God’s purpose is that “apart from us they should not be made perfect” (11:40). There is one household of God, in which Moses was a faithful servant and Christ is the Son (3:1–6).
  • God’s faithfulness to Israel. The new covenant was promised to “the house of Israel and the house of Judah” (8:8). Immediately after this oracle, Jeremiah records God’s pledge that Israel’s offspring will not cease to be a nation before him as long as the sun, moon, and stars endure (Jer 31:35–37). Paul affirms that “God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew” (Rom 11:2) and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Rom 11:29).

The new covenant, then, is new in its mediator (the Son, not Moses), its priesthood (Melchizedek’s order, not Aaron’s), its sacrifice (the once-for-all offering of Christ, not repeated animal sacrifices), its sanctuary (heaven itself, not the earthly copy), its power (the law written on the heart by God, not merely on stone), its scope (all who know the Lord, from least to greatest, and through Israel’s Messiah the nations as well), and its forgiveness (final and complete, so that sins are remembered no more). It is not new in its God, its goal, or its moral character. The first covenant was not a mistake that God corrected, but a stage that God fulfilled. As Augustine famously put it in substance, the new is concealed in the old, and the old is revealed in the new.

The chapter that began by pointing to the heavenly throne ends by pointing to the fading of the earthly order. Between them stands the mediator of a better covenant. The preacher has now laid the foundation for the next movement of his sermon: having shown that the first covenant is old, he will describe its sanctuary and rituals in detail (9:1–10) in order to show how Christ, entering the true sanctuary with his own blood, has accomplished what they could only foreshadow (9:11–10:18).

For Reflection

  1. The preacher says that the “main point” of his message is that “we have such a high priest” seated at God’s right hand (8:1). If someone asked you to state the main point of your faith, what would you say? How does Hebrews’ emphasis on a living, present, ministering Christ correct a faith centered on systems, rituals, or ideas?
  2. The tabernacle was a God-given “copy and shadow” of the heavenly reality (8:5). In what ways are believers today still tempted to cling to visible, tangible securities rather than to the unseen reality of Christ in heaven? What are the “shadows” that can compete with the substance in your own life?
  3. Jeremiah says the first covenant failed because “they did not continue in my covenant” (8:9), and Hebrews earlier warned, “how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?” (2:3). How does the history of Israel serve as both a warning and an encouragement to you? Where are you tempted to neglect what God has given?
  4. In the new covenant God promises to write his law on the heart (8:10). How does this change the way you think about obedience? What is the difference between obeying God out of external pressure and obeying him because his will has become your own desire? How does the Holy Spirit accomplish this (Ezek 36:26–27; Rom 8:4; 2 Cor 3:3)?
  5. God promises, “their sins I will by no means remember any longer” (8:12). Are there sins you keep “remembering” against yourself, even though God has promised not to remember them? How should the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ shape the way you deal with guilt and with the past?
  6. Verse 13 says that the first covenant is “near to vanishing.” How would you explain to a Jewish friend, or to a fellow Christian, both what is new in the new covenant and what continues from the old (God’s moral will, the promises to Abraham, the Scriptures, God’s faithfulness to Israel)? Why is it important to hold both together?
