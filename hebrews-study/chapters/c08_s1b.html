8:7

For if that first covenant had been faultless, no place would have been sought for a second.

The preacher now explains (γάρ gar, “for”) why the covenant Christ mediates is better. His argument takes a familiar shape. Once again he uses a contrary-to-fact condition: Εἰ γὰρ ἡ πρώτη ἐκείνη ἦν ἄμεμπτος, οὐκ ἂν δευτέρας ἐζητεῖτο τόπος Ei gar hē prōtē ekeinē ēn amemptos, ouk an deuteras ezēteito topos, “for if that first had been faultless, no place would have been sought for a second.” As in 8:4, the grammar signals that the condition is untrue: the first covenant was not faultless, and a place was sought for a second.

The noun “covenant” is not expressed in the Greek; the feminine adjectives “first” (πρώτη prōtē) and “second” (δευτέρα deutera) agree with the feminine noun διαθήκη diathēkē from 8:6, so the translation supplies it. This is the first time Hebrews calls the Sinai covenant “the first”; the label recurs in 8:13, 9:1, 9:15, and 9:18. The word “that” (ἐκείνη ekeinē) adds a note of distance: “that first covenant,” the one belonging to an earlier stage of God’s dealings.

The key word is ἄμεμπτος amemptos, “faultless, blameless,” formed from the negative prefix a- and the verb μέμφομαι memphomai, “to find fault, to blame,” which appears in the very next verse. The same adjective is used of Zechariah and Elizabeth, who walked “blameless in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord” (Luke 1:6), and by Paul of his former life, “as to righteousness under the law, blameless” (Phil 3:6). To say the first covenant was not “faultless” does not mean that it was sinful, or that God made a mistake in giving it. Paul insists that “the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good” (Rom 7:12). Hebrews itself treats the old covenant as God’s own word, spoken through Moses and the prophets (1:1; 2:2; 3:5). The “fault” lay in its insufficiency to accomplish the final purpose of God: to bring sinners to perfection, cleanse their consciences, and give them permanent access to his presence. The preacher has already said it plainly: “a former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness, for the law made nothing perfect, but on the other hand a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God” (7:18–19). Paul locates the weakness in the same place: “what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending his own Son” (Rom 8:3). The law could command, but not change the heart; it could provide sacrifices that pointed to forgiveness, but not sacrifices that could “take away sins” (10:4).

The phrase “no place would have been sought” (οὐκ ἂν… ἐζητεῖτο τόπος ouk an… ezēteito topos) is idiomatic: there would have been no room, no occasion, for a second covenant. The passive “would have been sought” implicitly has God as its subject: it was God who “sought a place” for a second covenant when he spoke through Jeremiah.

This verse uses the same kind of reasoning the preacher has used twice already, and will use again. In 4:8 he argued, “if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day after these things.” In 7:11 he argued, “if perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood… what further need would there have been for another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek?” In 10:1–2 he will argue that if the sacrifices could perfect the worshipers, they would have ceased to be offered. In each case the logic is the same: when God himself, at a later point in redemptive history, announces something new, he thereby shows that what came before was not final. The Old Testament itself bears witness to its own provisional character. This is not an argument imposed on the Hebrew Scriptures from outside; it is drawn from the forward-looking promises within them. The preacher’s hearers did not need to choose between Moses and Jesus as if they were rivals. They needed to see that Moses and the prophets pointed to Jesus.

8:8

For, finding fault with them, he says: “Behold, days are coming, says the Lord, when I will accomplish a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah,

The preacher now introduces Jeremiah’s oracle with a participle that picks up the key idea of 8:7: μεμφόμενος γὰρ αὐτοὺς λέγει memphomenos gar autous legei, “for, finding fault with them, he says.” The subject of “he says” is God. Throughout Hebrews, Scripture is quoted as the living voice of God speaking in the present (“he says,” present tense), not merely as words written long ago (compare 1:6–7; 3:7; 10:5). Jeremiah was the human messenger, but the preacher hears God himself speaking.

Who is it that God finds fault with? According to the Greek text printed here, the object is αὐτούς autous, “them,” that is, the people of Israel. This is important. Verse 7 might have left the impression that the fault lay in the covenant as such; verse 8 clarifies that the fault God found was with the people who lived under it. The quotation itself confirms this immediately: “because they did not continue in my covenant” (8:9). The problem with the first covenant was not that its commandments were wrong, but that it was made with a people whose hearts were not changed, and it did not itself provide the power to change them. As Moses lamented at the end of the wilderness years, “the Lord has not given you a heart to know, or eyes to see, or ears to hear, to this day” (Deut 29:4 [Hebrew 29:3]).

Textual Note: “with them” or “to them”?

The Greek manuscripts are divided between αὐτούς autous (accusative, “them”) and αὐτοῖς autois (dative, “to them”). With the accusative, the natural sense is “finding fault with them, he says.” With the dative, one could read either “finding fault with them” (since the verb can also take a dative object) or “finding fault [with it, the first covenant], he says to them.” Both readings have strong support, and modern editions (including the text used in this study) generally print the accusative. Whichever is original, the meaning is not greatly affected, because the quotation that follows explicitly places the blame on the people: “they did not continue in my covenant.” The weakness of the first covenant and the failure of the people belong together: the covenant was weak precisely because it could not overcome the people’s unfaithfulness.

Then the quotation begins. Jeremiah 31:31–34 (numbered 38:31–34 in the Greek Old Testament, which arranges Jeremiah’s chapters differently) is the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. The preacher quotes it in full because every line will matter for his argument in chapters 9 and 10.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 31:31

Hebrew: הִנֵּה יָמִים בָּאִים נְאֻם־יְהוָה וְכָרַתִּי אֶת־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל וְאֶת־בֵּית יְהוּדָה בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה׃ — hinneh yamim ba’im ne’um-YHWH vekharatti et-beit yisra’el ve’et-beit yehudah berit chadashah, “Behold, days are coming, declares the Lord, when I will cut with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah a new covenant.”

The Hebrew idiom is to “cut” (כָּרַת karat) a covenant, probably reflecting the ancient ceremony in which animals were cut in two and the covenant parties passed between the pieces (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18–20). The phrase “new covenant” (בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה berit chadashah) appears only here in the Hebrew Bible, though the idea is present elsewhere: an “everlasting covenant” (Jer 32:40; Ezek 37:26), a “covenant of peace” (Isa 54:10; Ezek 34:25), and the gift of a new heart and a new spirit (Ezek 36:26–27). The Hebrew adjective chadash can mean either “brand new” or “renewed” (it is related to chodesh, the “new moon,” which renews each month). But Jeremiah himself rules out a mere renewal of the old: this covenant will be “not like the covenant I made with their fathers” (31:32).

The Greek of Hebrews follows the Septuagint closely, with a few small differences of wording. Where the Hebrew says “I will cut” and the Septuagint, as generally printed, says “I will make” (diathēsomai, the verb related to “covenant”), Hebrews has συντελέσω syntelesō, “I will accomplish, bring to completion.” Hebrews also says “with” using the preposition epi with the accusative (“upon, for, with the house of Israel”). The Greek “says the Lord” (λέγει κύριος legei kyrios) renders the solemn Hebrew prophetic formula ne’um YHWH, “utterance of the Lord.” Here “Lord” (kyrios) stands for the divine name YHWH.

“Behold, days are coming” (Ἰδοὺ ἡμέραι ἔρχονται Idou hēmerai erchontai) is a characteristic formula in Jeremiah for God’s future intervention, sometimes in judgment (Jer 7:32; 19:6) and often in salvation: the days when God will raise up for David “a righteous Branch” (23:5; 33:14–15), when he will restore the fortunes of his people (30:3), when Jerusalem will be rebuilt (31:38). In the context of Hebrews, these coming days have arrived: God has spoken “in these last days” by his Son (1:2), and Christ has appeared “at the end of the ages” (9:26).

The verb συντελέσω syntelesō, “I will accomplish,” is worth pausing over. It means to bring something to its end, to complete or fulfill it. Whether or not the preacher chose it deliberately, it fits his theology beautifully. The new covenant is not one more attempt alongside the old, but the accomplishment of what God had purposed all along. The related noun appears in 9:26: Christ appeared “at the consummation (συντελείᾳ synteleia) of the ages.” The new covenant belongs to the consummation.

The “new” covenant is καινήν kainēn. The adjective kainos often carries the sense of “new in kind or quality,” as in a “new creation” (2 Cor 5:17) or a “new heaven and new earth” (Rev 21:1), in contrast to another Greek word for “new,” neos, which more often means “new in time, recent, young.” The distinction should not be pressed too hard, because the two words overlap, and Hebrews uses neos of the same covenant in 12:24 (“the mediator of a new covenant”). But in Jeremiah’s oracle, the covenant is new above all because it is different in character from the old: “not like” it.

The covenant is made “with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah.” In Jeremiah’s day the people had long been divided: the northern kingdom (Israel) had been carried into exile by Assyria in 722 BC, and the southern kingdom (Judah) was now going into exile in Babylon. The promise of a new covenant with both houses is a promise that God will reunite and restore his whole people (compare Jer 3:18; 30:3; Ezek 37:15–28). The new covenant is first of all God’s promise to Israel. The New Testament never forgets this. Jesus, the Messiah of Israel, inaugurated it at a Passover meal with twelve Jewish apostles, representing the twelve tribes (Luke 22:14–20). Through him, Gentiles who were once “strangers to the covenants of promise” (Eph 2:12) have been brought near and share in the blessings of Abraham (Gal 3:14, 29), grafted into Israel’s olive tree (Rom 11:17–24). The new covenant does not create a different people of God from the one God called in Abraham; it fulfills God’s promises to Israel in Israel’s Messiah and opens them to the nations, as God promised Abraham from the beginning (Gen 12:3). Paul, quoting Isaiah alongside the language of Jeremiah’s new covenant, still anticipates a day when “all Israel will be saved,” for “this is my covenant with them, when I take away their sins” (Rom 11:26–27).
