Minister of a Better Covenant (8:1–13)

Translation

1Now the main point in what is being said is this: we have such a high priest, one who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens, 2a minister of the sanctuary, that is, of the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched, not man. 3For every high priest is appointed to offer both gifts and sacrifices; hence it was necessary for this one also to have something that he might offer. 4Now if he were on earth, he would not even be a priest, since there are those who offer the gifts according to the law— 5men who serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things, just as Moses was divinely instructed when he was about to complete the tabernacle; for “See,” he says, “that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown to you on the mountain.” 6But as it is, he has obtained a more excellent ministry, to the same degree that he is also the mediator of a better covenant, which has been enacted on the basis of better promises.

7For if that first covenant had been faultless, no place would have been sought for a second. 8For, finding fault with them, he says:

“Behold, days are coming, says the Lord,
when I will accomplish a new covenant
with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah,

9not like the covenant that I made with their fathers
on the day when I took them by the hand
to lead them out of the land of Egypt;
because they did not continue in my covenant,
and I disregarded them, says the Lord.

10For this is the covenant that I will covenant with the house of Israel
after those days, says the Lord:
putting my laws into their mind,
I will also write them upon their hearts;
and I will be their God,
and they shall be my people.

11And they shall by no means teach,
each one his fellow citizen and each one his brother,
saying, ‘Know the Lord,’
because all will know me,
from the least to the greatest of them.

12For I will be merciful toward their unrighteous deeds,
and their sins I will by no means remember any longer.”

13In saying “new,” he has made the first old; and what is being made old and is growing aged is near to vanishing.

Setting and Structure

With chapter 8 the sermon crosses a threshold. The long exposition of Psalm 110:4 in chapter 7 has shown that Jesus is a priest of a different and better order than Aaron’s: appointed not by a law of physical descent but by “the power of an indestructible life” (7:16), confirmed by God’s oath (7:20–22), holding his priesthood permanently (7:24), and “able to save completely those who draw near to God through him” (7:25). Chapter 7 closed by describing him as “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and become higher than the heavens” (7:26), who offered himself “once for all” (7:27). Now the preacher gathers this up and presses forward: “we have such a high priest” (8:1). The question is no longer only who he is, but where he ministers, what he offers, and under what covenant he serves.

The section has two movements, each controlled by an Old Testament text.

  • 8:1–6: The better ministry in the true sanctuary. Psalm 110:1 (“Sit at my right hand”) stands behind 8:1, and Exodus 25:40 (“make everything according to the pattern”) is quoted in 8:5. The preacher contrasts two sanctuaries (the true tabernacle in heaven and the earthly copy), two sets of ministers (the exalted Son and the Levitical priests who “serve a copy and shadow”), and two covenants (the first and the better). Verse 6 is the hinge: the superiority of Christ’s ministry is measured by the superiority of the covenant he mediates.
  • 8:7–13: The better covenant promised by God. Jeremiah 31:31–34 (in the Greek Old Testament, Jer 38:31–34) is quoted in full (8:8–12), framed by the preacher’s own brief comments before (8:7–8a) and after (8:13). The frame states the logic: if God promised a “new” covenant, the first was not faultless and was already, by God’s own word, made old.

The two movements are tightly joined. Verse 6 names three “better” things in quick succession—a more excellent ministry, a better covenant, better promises—and each of them is then unfolded. The “better promises” are spelled out by Jeremiah’s oracle itself (8:10–12): the law written on the heart, the covenant relationship (“I will be their God”), universal personal knowledge of God, and decisive forgiveness of sins. Chapters 9 and 10 will then show how the sacrifice of Christ secures these promises. The section 8:1–10:18 is bracketed by Jeremiah’s prophecy: quoted at length here, it returns at the end in abbreviated form, focused on its two climactic promises, the law written on the heart and sins remembered no more (10:16–17). Everything between is commentary on how the new covenant came into force.

Three strands of background help the reader.

First, the tabernacle. The preacher consistently speaks of the wilderness tabernacle (σκηνή skēnē, “tent, tabernacle”), not of Herod’s temple in Jerusalem. He argues from Scripture, not from the architecture of his own day. The Pentateuch presents the tabernacle as built according to a “pattern” God showed Moses on the mountain (Exod 25:9, 40; 26:30; Num 8:4), and the Old Testament elsewhere speaks of God’s true dwelling as being in heaven (1 Kgs 8:27, 30; Ps 11:4; Isa 66:1). Hebrews takes these biblical threads and draws the conclusion that the earthly sanctuary was a God-given copy of a heavenly reality.

Second, Jewish reflection on a heavenly sanctuary. Many Jewish writings of the Second Temple period also spoke of a heavenly temple. The Wisdom of Solomon has Solomon say that the temple he built was “a copy of the holy tent which you prepared from the beginning” (Wis 9:8). The apocalypse known as 2 Baruch (late first century AD) says that God showed Moses on Sinai “the likeness of the tabernacle and all its vessels” (2 Bar 4:5). Philo of Alexandria, the Jewish philosopher who was an older contemporary of the apostles, interpreted the same verse (Exod 25:40) through the lens of Plato’s philosophy, as if Moses had glimpsed the eternal, incorporeal “ideas” of which the material tabernacle was an imitation. The relationship of Hebrews to these different backgrounds is discussed under 8:5.

Third, the hope of a new covenant. Jeremiah spoke his oracle in the darkest years of Judah’s history, as the Babylonians were destroying Jerusalem and taking the people into exile (early sixth century BC). The Sinai covenant had been broken (Jer 11:10), and the covenant curses of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28 had fallen. Into that ruin God promised not a mere restoration of the old arrangement but a new covenant. Later Jewish groups cherished this hope; the community associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls described its members as those who had entered “the new covenant in the land of Damascus” (CD 6:19). Most decisively, Jesus himself, on the night he was betrayed, took the cup and said, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25). When Hebrews quotes Jeremiah, it is expounding a covenant that the Lord Jesus had already declared to be inaugurated in his own death.

Finally, the section has a pastoral edge. The readers were second-generation believers, many of them Jewish, who had suffered for their faith (10:32–34) and were now tempted to drift (2:1), to grow sluggish (5:11; 6:12), and to look back with longing toward the visible priesthood, sacrifices, and sanctuary of the old covenant. To such people the preacher says: what you are tempted to return to was only a copy and a shadow; the reality is in heaven, and Jesus is ministering there now on your behalf. And God himself, centuries ago, declared the old arrangement “old.” To go back would be to clutch a shadow while turning from the substance.

Verse by Verse

8:1

Now the main point in what is being said is this: we have such a high priest, one who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens,

The preacher pauses to tell his hearers where his argument has arrived. The opening word, Κεφάλαιον kephalaion, is related to κεφαλή kephalē, “head.” It can mean a “summary” (the heading under which things are summed up), or the “main point, the chief thing.” In commercial language it could also mean the principal, a capital sum of money; the only other New Testament occurrence has this sense, when the Roman commander tells Paul he acquired his citizenship “for a large sum” (Acts 22:28). Here the context favors “main point.” The preacher is not simply recapping chapter 7; he is about to say something new (the heavenly sanctuary, the covenant), and he is identifying the crowning point toward which the whole discussion has been moving. Many commentators, including Westcott and Bruce, take it in this way, and the flow of the argument supports them.

The phrase ἐπὶ τοῖς λεγομένοις epi tois legomenois, “in what is being said,” uses a present participle (“the things being said”), not a past one (“the things that have been said”). This suggests that the preacher has in view his whole discourse as it is now unfolding, including what he is about to say, not only what lies behind. The “main point” is the heart of the entire priestly argument.

That main point is stated simply: τοιοῦτον ἔχομεν ἀρχιερέα toiouton echomen archierea, “we have such a high priest.” Two words deserve attention. τοιοῦτον toiouton, “such,” points both backward and forward: backward to the high priest described in 7:26 (“for it was fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, undefiled…”), and forward to the relative clause that follows (“one who sat down…”). And ἔχομεν echomen, “we have,” is present tense. The preacher does not say “we had” or “we shall have,” but “we have.” This is one of the great present possessions of the believer in Hebrews: “we have a great high priest” (4:14); “we have this hope as an anchor of the soul” (6:19); “having a great priest over the house of God, let us draw near” (10:21–22); “we have an altar” (13:10). The readers were tempted to think that, having left the temple and its priests, they had nothing tangible. The preacher answers: you have a high priest, and he is in the heavens.

The relative clause describes his position: ὃς ἐκάθισεν ἐν δεξιᾷ τοῦ θρόνου τῆς μεγαλωσύνης ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς hos ekathisen en dexia tou thronou tēs megalōsynēs en tois ouranois, “who sat down at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens.” This is the language of Psalm 110:1, the verse that runs through the whole sermon (1:3, 13; 10:12–13; 12:2). In the Hebrew, “The Lord declares to my lord: ‘Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet’ ” (נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי שֵׁב לִימִינִי ne’um YHWH la’doni shev limini). In chapter 7 the preacher expounded Psalm 110:4 (“You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek”); here he brings 110:1 alongside it. The one who is priest forever is also the one enthroned at God’s right hand. In him kingship and priesthood are united, as the prophet Zechariah foresaw of the coming “Branch”: “he shall sit and rule upon his throne, and he shall be a priest upon his throne” (Zech 6:13; Hebrew וְיָשַׁב וּמָשַׁל עַל־כִּסְאוֹ וְהָיָה כֹהֵן עַל־כִּסְאוֹ veyashav umashal al-kis’o vehayah khohen al-kis’o).

The verb ἐκάθισεν ekathisen, “he sat down,” is in the aorist tense, which in Greek presents an action as a whole, here a definite event: at his exaltation, Christ took his seat. The posture matters. The preacher will later draw the contrast explicitly: “every priest stands daily ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins; but this one, having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, sat down at the right hand of God” (10:11–12). There was no chair in the tabernacle’s holy place for the priests; their work was never done. Christ’s sitting signals a finished sacrifice. Yet, as 8:2 will immediately add, his sitting does not mean inactivity: he is enthroned as a minister, and he “always lives to intercede” for his people (7:25).

“The Majesty” (μεγαλωσύνη megalōsynē, “greatness, majesty”) is a reverent way of referring to God himself without naming him, as in 1:3 (“at the right hand of the Majesty on high”). Jews of the period often used such expressions out of reverence for the divine name (compare “the Power” in Mark 14:62). Adding “the throne” here heightens the royal imagery: Christ is seated not merely beside God but at the right hand of the throne from which God rules all things. The throne of God is also, for believers, “the throne of grace” to which they may draw near with boldness because of this high priest (4:16).

Finally, “in the heavens.” The earthly high priest ministered in a tent on earth; this high priest ministers in heaven. The preacher has already said that Jesus “has passed through the heavens” (4:14) and has “become higher than the heavens” (7:26). The location of his ministry is the main point. If Christ’s priesthood were located on earth, it would be one more priesthood alongside the others. Because it is located in the very presence of God, it is the reality to which every earthly priesthood pointed.

The pastoral force of the verse should not be missed. The center of the Christian faith, according to Hebrews, is not a system or an idea but a person, alive and active, who sits at the place of supreme authority and who is “for us.” Calvin remarks, in substance, that the author directs the eyes of the readers to heaven, so that they may not be held down by earthly ceremonies. The congregation’s great temptation was to measure reality by what could be seen. The preacher calls them to measure it by where Jesus is.

8:2

a minister of the sanctuary, that is, of the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched, not man.

The enthroned Son is also a servant. The word λειτουργός leitourgos, “minister,” is a remarkable one to place beside the throne. In the Greek city-states a leitourgia was a public service that a wealthy citizen performed at his own expense for the good of the community, such as funding a festival or equipping a warship. In the Greek Old Testament, however, the word family was used especially for the priestly service of the sanctuary (for example, Exod 28:35, 43; Num 18:2–7), and Isaiah promised that God’s restored people would be called “ministers of our God” (Isa 61:6, where the Greek uses leitourgoi). Hebrews uses the word family consistently in this priestly sense: Christ has obtained a “ministry” (λειτουργία leitourgia, 8:6); Moses sprinkled “all the vessels of the ministry” (9:21); “every priest stands daily ministering” (10:11).

There is a quiet echo of chapter 1 here. There the angels were called God’s “ministers” (leitourgoi, 1:7, quoting Ps 104:4) and “ministering spirits sent out to serve” (1:14), while the Son was addressed as God enthroned (1:8–13). Now the Son himself is called “minister,” but of a unique kind: the angels minister by being sent; the Son ministers seated at God’s right hand, as the high priest of the heavenly sanctuary. He combines in himself the dignity of the throne and the humility of service, as he did on earth when he said that he came “not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45).

He is minister τῶν ἁγίων tōn hagiōn, literally “of the holy [places/things].” Grammatically the form could be masculine (“of the holy ones, the saints”) or neuter (“of the holy things” or “of the holy place”). Hebrews regularly uses the neuter plural ta hagia for the sanctuary, especially the inner sanctuary, the Most Holy Place, into which the high priest entered on the Day of Atonement and into which Christ has entered “once for all” (9:8, 12, 24, 25; 10:19; 13:11). So the best rendering is “minister of the sanctuary.” The suggestion that it means “minister of the saints” is grammatically possible but foreign to the context, which is entirely about sanctuaries.

The next phrase is joined by “and”: καὶ τῆς σκηνῆς τῆς ἀληθινῆς kai tēs skēnēs tēs alēthinēs, “and of the true tabernacle.” Does the preacher mean two distinct things—an inner sanctuary (“the holy places”) and an outer tent (“the tabernacle”)? Or is “and” explanatory, so that the second phrase restates the first (“the sanctuary, that is, the true tabernacle”)? Since the whole verse is concerned to contrast the heavenly sanctuary as a whole with its earthly copy, and since 9:11–12 speaks of Christ entering “the sanctuary” through “the greater and more perfect tabernacle,” some interpreters see a distinction here as well. Most, however, judge that 8:2 is not yet making that finer distinction; it simply identifies the sphere of Christ’s ministry as the real, heavenly dwelling of God. The translation above reflects this explanatory reading, while recognizing that the preacher will develop the inner structure of the heavenly sanctuary in chapter 9.

Word Study: ἀληθινός alēthinos

The adjective ἀληθινός alēthinos means “true” in the sense of “real, genuine,” as opposed to what is a mere copy, imitation, or provisional representation. It does not mean that the Mosaic tabernacle was false in the sense of being a lie or a pagan counterfeit; God himself commanded it. Rather, the earthly tent was a real but derivative representation, while the heavenly one is the original reality. Hebrews makes this explicit in 9:24: “Christ did not enter a sanctuary made with hands, a copy (ἀντίτυπα antitypa) of the true things, but into heaven itself.” The same use appears in John’s Gospel, where Jesus is “the true light” (John 1:9), “the true bread from heaven” given by the Father in contrast to the manna Moses gave (John 6:32), and “the true vine” (John 15:1). In each case the Old Testament gift was genuine and good, but it was a sign pointing to the greater reality now present in Christ.

The true tabernacle is the one ἣν ἔπηξεν ὁ κύριος, οὐκ ἄνθρωπος hēn epēxen ho kyrios, ouk anthrōpos, “which the Lord pitched, not man.” The verb πήγνυμι pēgnymi means “to fix, fasten,” and is used for driving in tent pegs and so for setting up a tent. The wilderness tabernacle was pitched by human hands—by Moses, Bezalel, Oholiab, and the skilled workers of Israel (Exod 35:30–36:8; 40:17–33). The heavenly tabernacle was pitched by God himself. Hebrews will later describe it as “not made with hands, that is, not of this creation” (9:11), and say that Christ entered “not into a sanctuary made with hands” but “into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24). Here “the Lord” most naturally refers to God the Father, the one who also builds the city Abraham looked for, “whose architect and builder is God” (11:10).

Old Testament Echo: Numbers 24:6

The phrase “which the Lord pitched” appears to echo the Greek Old Testament of Balaam’s blessing on Israel’s encampment in Numbers 24:6, where the Septuagint describes Israel’s dwellings as “like tents which the Lord pitched,” using the same verb pēgnymi. The Hebrew of that verse reads differently: כַּאֲהָלִים נָטַע יְהוָה כַּאֲרָזִים עֲלֵי־מָיִם ka’ahalim nata YHWH ka’arazim ale-mayim, “like aloes that the Lord planted, like cedars beside the waters.” The Hebrew word ahalim (“aloes,” an aromatic tree) is spelled with the same consonants as ohalim (“tents”), and the Greek translators read it as “tents,” which suits the opening of Balaam’s oracle (“How lovely are your tents, O Jacob,” Num 24:5). The echo is verbal rather than an argument; the preacher does not build his case on Numbers 24. But the phrase reminds a reader steeped in the Greek Bible that what God himself sets up is beautiful and lasting, in contrast to what human hands erect.

The contrast “the Lord, not man” is the decisive one. Throughout Scripture, God’s greatest works are those in which human hands contribute nothing: creation itself, the stone “cut out without hands” that becomes a mountain filling the earth (Dan 2:34, 45), the “house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens” (2 Cor 5:1). Stephen, quoting Isaiah 66:1–2, insisted that “the Most High does not dwell in houses made with hands” (Acts 7:48), and Paul told the Athenians the same (Acts 17:24). The point is not that the Mosaic tabernacle was illegitimate, but that it was never the final dwelling of God. Christ ministers in the dwelling that God himself has made, and therefore his ministry has the permanence and reality of God’s own work.

8:3

For every high priest is appointed to offer both gifts and sacrifices; hence it was necessary for this one also to have something that he might offer.

The preacher now gives the reason (γάρ gar, “for”) why it is important to establish where Christ ministers. He restates a principle he laid down in 5:1: “every high priest taken from among men is appointed on behalf of men in the things pertaining to God, so that he may offer both gifts and sacrifices for sins.” Here the wording is almost identical: πᾶς γὰρ ἀρχιερεὺς εἰς τὸ προσφέρειν δῶρά τε καὶ θυσίας καθίσταται pas gar archiereus eis to prospherein dōra te kai thysias kathistatai, “for every high priest is appointed to offer both gifts and sacrifices.” Offering is not one of many incidental duties of a priest; it is the defining purpose of the office. The verb καθίσταται kathistatai, “is appointed,” is the same verb used in 5:1 and 7:28; it denotes official installation in an office. A high priest who offers nothing is not a high priest.

“Gifts and sacrifices” (δῶρά τε καὶ θυσίας dōra te kai thysias) is a comprehensive expression for the various offerings of the Levitical system—grain offerings, peace offerings, burnt offerings, sin and guilt offerings (Lev 1–7). The preacher is not distinguishing sharply between bloodless “gifts” and blood “sacrifices”; the pair simply covers the whole range of what priests brought to God on the people’s behalf.

From this general principle he draws a necessary inference: ὅθεν ἀναγκαῖον ἔχειν τι καὶ τοῦτον ὃ προσενέγκῃ hothen anankaion echein ti kai touton ho prosenenkē, “hence it was necessary for this one also to have something that he might offer.” Since Jesus is a high priest, he too must offer. The Greek sentence has no verb “is” or “was” with “necessary”; the reader must supply one. If we supply “is,” the sense would be that Christ is continually offering something in heaven. If we supply “was,” the sense is that he had to have an offering to bring. The grammar of the clause points to the second option. The verb “he might offer,” προσενέγκῃ prosenenkē, is an aorist subjunctive, and the aorist in this kind of construction presents the offering as a single, complete act, not a continuous process. This contrasts with the present infinitive προσφέρειν prospherein, “to offer,” used earlier in the verse of high priests in general, whose offering was repeated again and again. Hebrews consistently describes Christ’s offering in this way: “he did this once for all when he offered up himself” (7:27); “he has been manifested once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself” (9:26); “Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many” (9:28); “we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10).

What is this “something”? The preacher leaves it unnamed here, building suspense, but he has already answered in 7:27—“he offered up himself”—and he will answer again in full in 9:12–14: not the blood of goats and calves, but “his own blood,” the offering of “himself without blemish to God” through the eternal Spirit. The high priest is also the sacrifice.

Interpretive Options: When and Where Did Christ Offer?

Verse 3, set right after the description of Christ’s heavenly ministry, has raised the question of whether Christ’s priestly offering took place on the cross, in heaven, or both.

  • View 1: The offering took place only in heaven, after the ascension. Some (notably the Socinians of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, against whom John Owen wrote at length) argued that Christ became a priest only after his ascension and that his death on the cross was merely the preparation for his priestly work, not itself a priestly sacrifice. This view cannot stand against the text of Hebrews. The preacher says that Christ “offered up himself” (7:27), that he appeared “to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself” (9:26), that he “offered one sacrifice for sins” and then “sat down” (10:12), that believers are sanctified “through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ” (10:10), and that Jesus “suffered outside the gate, in order to sanctify the people through his own blood” (13:12). The sacrifice was his bodily death on the cross.
  • View 2: The offering was the death on the cross, and its presentation was completed in his entrance into the heavenly sanctuary. This view, held by most interpreters from the Reformers (Calvin, Owen) to modern commentators (for example, Bruce, Lane, Cockerill), follows the pattern of the Day of Atonement, which Hebrews explicitly uses in chapter 9. On that day the high priest slaughtered the sacrificial animals outside the Most Holy Place and then carried their blood inside the curtain to sprinkle it before the mercy seat (Lev 16:11–16). Both actions were parts of one atoning ministry. In the same way, Christ offered himself on the cross and then, by means of his own blood, entered once for all into the heavenly sanctuary (9:12, 24), where he now appears before God for us. His present intercession (7:25) is not a fresh or repeated offering but the abiding effectiveness of a sacrifice completed once.

The second view does justice both to the aorist “he might offer” (one completed act) and to Hebrews’ emphasis on the heavenly location of Christ’s ministry. Christ does not continually sacrifice himself in heaven; he “sat down” because the offering was finished (10:12–14). But the finished offering has been brought into the true sanctuary, and its power is permanent.

The pastoral point is profound. A priest without an offering is no help to sinners. The preacher’s hearers may have felt that, apart from the temple’s altar, they had no sacrifice to cling to. He tells them that their high priest has something to offer, and that what he offered is infinitely more precious than all the animals ever slain at the bronze altar: he offered himself.

8:4

Now if he were on earth, he would not even be a priest, since there are those who offer the gifts according to the law—

The preacher now explains why Christ’s ministry must be located in heaven. The sentence is a “contrary-to-fact” condition: εἰ μὲν οὖν ἦν ἐπὶ γῆς, οὐδ’ ἂν ἦν ἱερεύς ei men oun ēn epi gēs, oud’ an ēn hiereus, “now if he were on earth, he would not even be a priest.” In Greek, the combination of a past-tense verb in the “if” clause with the particle ἄν an in the “then” clause signals that the speaker regards the condition as untrue. The preacher is saying: he is not on earth, and that is precisely why he can be a priest. The word οὐδέ oude, “not even,” is emphatic: on earth he would not merely be a lesser priest; he would not be a priest at all.

Why not? Because the earthly priesthood was already fully occupied by those whom the law appointed: ὄντων τῶν προσφερόντων κατὰ νόμον τὰ δῶρα ontōn tōn prospherontōn kata nomon ta dōra, “since there are those who offer the gifts according to the law.” The law of Moses restricted the priesthood to the descendants of Aaron within the tribe of Levi. God told Aaron, “You and your sons with you shall guard your priesthood for everything concerning the altar and what is inside the curtain… and the outsider who comes near shall be put to death” (Num 18:7; compare Num 3:10; 16:40). The preacher has already pointed out that Jesus “has arisen from Judah, a tribe concerning which Moses said nothing about priests” (7:14). When King Uzziah of Judah presumed to burn incense in the temple, he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). Under the law, a man of Judah had no right to serve at the altar. So if Jesus’ priesthood were of the earthly kind, it would be illegitimate. His priesthood belongs to another order (Melchizedek’s) and is exercised in another sphere (heaven).

Does this mean that Jesus performed no priestly act on earth? That conclusion would contradict the rest of the letter, which insists that he offered himself in his death (7:27; 9:14, 26; 10:10, 12; 13:12; see the note on 8:3). The point of 8:4 is that Christ’s priesthood is not one more earthly, Levitical ministry competing with Aaron’s sons at the Jerusalem altar. His sacrifice was not offered in the temple courts, “according to the law,” but outside the city gate (13:12), and his priestly ministry is carried out in the true sanctuary in heaven. As on the Day of Atonement, the slaying took place outside the inner sanctuary and the decisive presentation inside it; but the sanctuary he entered was heaven itself.

Historical Background: The Present Tense and the Date of Hebrews

The preacher speaks of the Levitical priests in the present tense: “there are those who offer the gifts according to the law” (8:4), who “serve a copy and shadow” (8:5). The same present tenses recur in 9:6–9, 10:1–3, and 10:11 (“every priest stands daily ministering”). Many interpreters take this as an indication that the temple in Jerusalem was still standing and its sacrifices still being offered when Hebrews was written, that is, before the Romans destroyed the temple in AD 70.

Honesty requires two qualifications. First, the present tense by itself is not conclusive. Writers after AD 70 sometimes described the temple ritual in the present tense: Josephus does so in places in his Antiquities, written in the 90s; the letter known as 1 Clement (usually dated around AD 96) says that the daily sacrifices are offered not everywhere but only in Jerusalem (1 Clem 41:2); and the Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, describes the temple service in vivid present detail. Second, Hebrews argues from the tabernacle described in Scripture rather than from Herod’s temple, so some of its present tenses may simply describe what the law prescribes.

Still, the cumulative evidence points toward a date before AD 70. The preacher’s argument is that the old priesthood and sacrifices have been superseded and that the first covenant is “near to vanishing” (8:13). He even asks whether the sacrifices “would not have ceased to be offered” if they had been able to perfect the worshipers (10:2). Had the temple already been burned and its sacrifices brought to an end, that event would have been an overwhelming confirmation of his case, and it is hard to believe he would not have mentioned it. The present tenses in 8:4–5 fit most naturally with a situation in which the altar in Jerusalem was still smoking and the readers could still feel its pull.

There is an irony here that the first hearers would have felt. The Levitical priests were legitimate “according to the law,” and on earth they had every right to the altar that Jesus did not. Yet the very legitimacy that law conferred on them confined them to the earthly copy. Jesus, who had no claim to the earthly altar, has been appointed by God’s oath to the heavenly reality. The phrase “according to the law” (κατὰ νόμον kata nomon) recalls 7:16, where Jesus is said to have become a priest “not according to the law of a fleshly commandment, but according to the power of an indestructible life.”

8:5

men who serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things, just as Moses was divinely instructed when he was about to complete the tabernacle; for “See,” he says, “that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown to you on the mountain.”

This verse, which the Greek text sets off as a parenthesis, describes the nature of the earthly priests’ service and grounds it in the Torah itself. It is one of the most important verses in Hebrews for understanding how the author sees the relationship between the old covenant’s institutions and the reality in Christ.

The priests are described as οἵτινες ὑποδείγματι καὶ σκιᾷ λατρεύουσιν τῶν ἐπουρανίων hoitines hypodeigmati kai skia latreuousin tōn epouraniōn, “men who serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things.” The verb λατρεύω latreuō, “to serve, to worship,” is a word for religious service rendered to God. Hebrews uses it for the worship of the old covenant (9:9; 10:2; 13:10, “those who serve the tabernacle”) and also for the worship of the new (9:14, “to serve the living God”; 12:28, “let us serve God acceptably with reverence and awe”). The striking thing here is the object of their service: they serve “a copy and shadow.” Their ministry, however sincere and divinely commanded, was rendered within a structure that was itself a representation of something else.

Word Study: ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma, σκιά skia, and τύπος typos

ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma ordinarily means an “example” or “model” to be imitated, and Hebrews uses it that way in 4:11 (“so that no one may fall by the same example of disobedience”). Here, and again in 9:23 (“the copies of the things in the heavens”), it means a copy or outline sketch that represents a reality greater than itself. The earthly tabernacle was a kind of three-dimensional sketch of God’s heavenly dwelling.

σκιά skia, “shadow,” is a shape cast by a real object. A shadow is genuinely connected to what casts it; it has the same outline, and by it one can know something of the object. But it has no substance of its own. Hebrews uses the word again in 10:1: “the law, having a shadow of the good things to come, not the very form of the things.” Paul uses the same image: food laws, festivals, new moons, and Sabbaths “are a shadow of the things to come, but the body belongs to Christ” (Col 2:17). In both passages, notice, the shadow is cast forward in time: it anticipates “things to come.”

τύπος typos, “pattern,” originally meant the mark left by a blow (as in the “mark” of the nails, John 20:25), then an impression, image, model, or pattern. In Exodus 25:40 (quoted here) it renders the Hebrew תַּבְנִית tavnit, “pattern, model, plan,” from the verb banah, “to build.” From this word comes the term “typology,” the study of how persons, events, and institutions in the Old Testament prefigure their fulfillment in Christ.

The preacher grounds this description in Scripture: καθὼς κεχρημάτισται Μωϋσῆς μέλλων ἐπιτελεῖν τὴν σκηνήν kathōs kechrēmatistai Mōusēs mellōn epitelein tēn skēnēn, “just as Moses was divinely instructed when he was about to complete the tabernacle.” The verb χρηματίζω chrēmatizō is used in the New Testament for a divine communication—a revelation, warning, or instruction from God (Matt 2:12, 22; Luke 2:26; Acts 10:22). Hebrews uses it of Noah, “being divinely warned about things not yet seen” (11:7), and of God “who warns from heaven” (12:25). It is in the perfect tense here (“has been divinely instructed”), which suggests that the instruction given to Moses stands on record in Scripture with continuing authority. The phrase “about to complete” (ἐπιτελεῖν epitelein, “to finish, to carry through”) refers to the construction of the tabernacle as a whole.

Then comes the quotation itself, introduced by “he says” (φησίν phēsin), with God as the speaker: Ὅρα γάρ, φησίν, ποιήσεις πάντα κατὰ τὸν τύπον τὸν δειχθέντα σοι ἐν τῷ ὄρει Hora gar, phēsin, poiēseis panta kata ton typon ton deichthenta soi en tō orei, “for ‘See,’ he says, ‘that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown to you on the mountain.’ ” In the Greek, “you will make” is a future indicative used with the force of a command, as often in biblical law (“you shall make”).

Old Testament Text: Exodus 25:40

Hebrew: וּרְאֵה וַעֲשֵׂה בְּתַבְנִיתָם אֲשֶׁר־אַתָּה מָרְאֶה בָּהָר׃ — ure’eh va’aseh betavnitam asher-attah mor’eh bahar, “And see, and make [them] according to their pattern, which you are being shown on the mountain.”

The verse concludes God’s instructions for the lampstand (Exod 25:31–40), but it echoes the general command of 25:9: “According to all that I am showing you, the pattern (תַּבְנִית tavnit) of the tabernacle and the pattern of all its furnishings, so you shall make it.” The same idea recurs in Exodus 26:30 (“you shall erect the tabernacle according to its plan which you were shown on the mountain”) and Numbers 8:4. Notice the Hebrew participle mor’eh, “being shown, being made to see”: Moses on Sinai was being given a vision.

Hebrews follows the Greek Old Testament, which renders tavnit with typos, “pattern.” Two small features of Hebrews’ wording deserve mention. Hebrews includes the word “everything” (πάντα panta), which is not in the Hebrew of 25:40 but fairly summarizes 25:9 (“according to all that I am showing you”); interestingly, Philo also includes “everything” when he cites this verse (Allegorical Interpretation 3.102), so the word may reflect a form of the Greek text current at the time. And Hebrews uses an aorist participle, “that was shown,” looking back on the revelation as a completed past event. Neither change alters the meaning; both underline that the whole tabernacle, in every detail, was built to correspond to what God had revealed.

What exactly did Moses see? The Hebrew word tavnit can mean a model or blueprint (as in 1 Chr 28:11–19, where David gives Solomon the tavnit of the temple, “all in writing from the hand of the Lord,” 28:19), or a visible form or likeness (Deut 4:16–18; Ezek 8:3). The text of Exodus does not tell us whether Moses saw a scale model, a plan, or a vision of God’s heavenly dwelling itself. Hebrews, reading Exodus in the light of the rest of Scripture’s testimony that God’s true throne and temple are in heaven (Ps 11:4; Isa 6:1–4; Mic 1:2–3; compare Rev 11:19; 15:5), concludes that the pattern corresponded to a heavenly reality, “the true tabernacle which the Lord pitched” (8:2). This is a legitimate reading of Exodus: the tabernacle was designed by God himself, as a dwelling on earth that answered to his dwelling in heaven.

Why does the preacher cite this verse here? Because it proves his point from Moses himself. The very book that established the tabernacle also testifies that the tabernacle was a copy. Moses did not invent the design; he received it. The tabernacle’s authority came from its correspondence to something beyond itself. For readers tempted to treat the earthly sanctuary as the ultimate meeting place of God and humanity, this is decisive: the Torah itself points away from the copy to the original. The command to build “according to the pattern” was a gracious provision for Israel, giving them a true, divinely authorized meeting place with God—but it was never meant to be the end of the story.

Interpretive Options: Platonic Philosophy or Biblical Eschatology?

The language of 8:5—“copy,” “shadow,” “true,” “heavenly things”—sounds to many modern readers like the philosophy of Plato. In Plato’s famous allegory of the cave (Republic, book 7), prisoners see only shadows cast on a wall and mistake them for reality; true reality belongs to the eternal, unchanging “Forms” or “Ideas,” of which the things of the visible world are imperfect copies. Philo of Alexandria, who blended Jewish Scripture with Platonic philosophy, interpreted Exodus 25:40 in just this way: Moses saw with the eye of his soul the incorporeal forms of the things to be made, and the material tabernacle was an imitation of these timeless ideas (On the Life of Moses 2.74–76). Since Hebrews was written in polished Greek by an educated author, some scholars have concluded that Hebrews shares Philo’s Platonic worldview.

  • View 1: Hebrews is fundamentally Platonic (or Philonic). On this view, the author thinks in terms of two levels of reality, the eternal heavenly ideal and the changing earthly copy, and Christ’s work is the movement from the lower level to the higher. Earlier scholarship often argued for direct dependence on Philo. Some recent interpreters, such as Attridge, see Hebrews using vocabulary at home in Middle Platonism while reshaping it for its own purposes.
  • View 2: Hebrews’ framework is biblical and Jewish-apocalyptic: vertical and eschatological. On this view, the “heavenly sanctuary” comes not from Plato but from the Old Testament (Exod 25:40; Ps 11:4; Isa 6) and from Jewish apocalyptic writings that speak of a heavenly temple where God is enthroned and angels worship (for example, 1 Enoch 14; the Dead Sea Scrolls’ Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice; 2 Bar 4:2–7; compare Wis 9:8; Rev 4–5; 11:19). This background is “vertical” (heaven above, earth below) but also “eschatological” (the heavenly reality is what will be revealed and established at the end, “the age to come”). Interpreters such as Bruce, Lane, Cockerill, and Schreiner, and before them Geerhardus Vos, emphasize this framework.

The text of Hebrews itself decides the question in favor of the second view, for several reasons.

First, the heavenly sanctuary is not an abstract, timeless idea but a place where a person acts. Jesus, a man of flesh and blood (2:14), who died in history “outside the gate” (13:12), has entered it bodily and “sat down” (8:1). For Plato and Philo, the material world could never enter the realm of the Forms. For Hebrews, the incarnate, crucified, and risen Son has gone into heaven itself (9:24). The very heart of the gospel—the Word made flesh, the bodily resurrection, the ascension of a human high priest—is foreign to Platonism.

Second, Hebrews’ “shadow” is cast forward in time, not merely upward in space. In 10:1 the law has “a shadow of the good things to come.” The tabernacle was “a parable for the present time” (9:9), imposed “until the time of reformation” (9:10). Christ appeared “at the end of the ages” (9:26), in “these last days” (1:2). The movement in Hebrews is from promise to fulfillment, from the first covenant to the new, from the old age to “the world to come” (2:5; 6:5). That is the biblical shape of history, not the Platonic contrast between time and timelessness. Notice that the preacher’s next step after 8:5 is not a philosophical discussion of ideal forms but a quotation from a prophet about “days to come” (8:8).

Third, Hebrews grounds the earthly-heavenly correspondence in Scripture, not in philosophy. The authority for the claim is Exodus 25:40, read as God’s word. And the Old Testament itself already speaks of God enthroned in a heavenly temple (Ps 11:4; 18:6; Isa 6:1; Mic 1:2–3), and of a sanctuary built to correspond to it.

Fourth, Hebrews’ view of the material creation is positive in a way Platonism’s is not. God made the world through the Son (1:2), and the Son “became like his brothers in every respect” (2:17). What will be “shaken” and removed at the end is not matter as such but the created order in its present, provisional form, so that what cannot be shaken may remain (12:26–28).

It is fair to say that the author, writing for Greek-speaking readers, used words that educated people of his day would have recognized, and he may even have enjoyed their resonance. But the content he poured into them is biblical. Like Paul (Col 2:17), he uses “shadow” and “reality” to describe the relation between the old covenant’s institutions and the person and work of Christ. His framework is the Bible’s own: God’s heavenly dwelling, revealed in pattern to Moses, entered in reality by Jesus, and opened to God’s people at the climax of history.

One more observation keeps the verse in proportion. Calling the tabernacle a “copy and shadow” does not belittle it. A shadow proves that the reality is near and has a definite shape. The tabernacle, with its curtains, altar, lampstand, and ark, taught Israel for centuries how holy God is, how sin separates, how blood atones, and how God nonetheless desires to dwell among his people. The preacher will dwell lovingly on its furnishings in 9:1–5. But a shadow is not something to embrace once the one who casts it has come. To return to the tabernacle’s rites after Christ has entered the heavenly sanctuary would be like turning from a friend’s face to study his shadow on the wall.

8:6

But as it is, he has obtained a more excellent ministry, to the same degree that he is also the mediator of a better covenant, which has been enacted on the basis of better promises.

After the parenthesis of 8:4–5, the preacher returns to his main line with νυνὶ δέ nyni de, “but as it is” (or “but now”). The phrase can be temporal (“now, in the present time”) or logical (“but, as things actually stand”). Here both senses are present: in contrast to the hypothetical “if he were on earth” (8:4), the actual situation is that Christ is in heaven; and in contrast to the old order of shadows, the new order has now arrived. The same phrase introduces the decisive turning point in 9:26: “but as it is, he has been manifested once for all at the end of the ages.”

διαφορωτέρας τέτυχεν λειτουργίας diaphorōteras tetychen leitourgias, “he has obtained a more excellent ministry.” The adjective διαφορώτερος diaphorōteros, “more excellent, superior,” appears in the New Testament only here and in 1:4, where the Son “has inherited a more excellent name” than the angels. The echo is surely deliberate. As the Son’s name surpasses the angels’, so his ministry surpasses that of the Levitical priests. The verb τέτυχεν tetychen, “he has obtained,” is in the perfect tense, which in Greek describes a past action with continuing results. Christ obtained this ministry (at his exaltation, by God’s appointment, 5:5–6) and holds it still. “Ministry” (λειτουργία leitourgia) picks up “minister” from 8:2.

The measure of this excellence is then given: ὅσῳ καὶ κρείττονός ἐστιν διαθήκης μεσίτης hosō kai kreittonos estin diathēkēs mesitēs, “to the same degree that he is also the mediator of a better covenant.” The construction “by as much as” (hosō) sets up a proportion, again as in 1:4 (“having become as much better than the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent”). A priest’s ministry is only as good as the covenant it serves. The Levitical priests served the Sinai covenant; Christ serves, and mediates, a better one. So the superiority of the covenant becomes the gauge of the superiority of his ministry.

Word Study: μεσίτης mesitēs, “mediator”

A μεσίτης mesitēs is literally “one in the middle” (from mesos, “middle”): a go-between, arbitrator, or guarantor who stands between two parties to establish or secure an agreement. In ordinary Greek usage the word could describe a negotiator in a business transaction or a third party who guaranteed that terms would be kept. In biblical tradition the great mediator of the first covenant was Moses, who went up the mountain to receive God’s words and came down to deliver them, and who sprinkled “the blood of the covenant” on the people (Exod 24:3–8). Paul refers to the law as “ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator” (Gal 3:19), meaning Moses. Hebrews calls Jesus the mediator of the new covenant three times: here, in 9:15 (“he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that… those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance”), and in 12:24 (“Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and the sprinkled blood”). Paul makes the same confession: “there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all” (1 Tim 2:5–6).

Jesus is a better mediator than Moses not only because of who he is (the Son over God’s house, not a servant in it, 3:5–6) but because of what he does. Moses delivered the terms of the covenant and sprinkled the blood of animals; Jesus establishes the new covenant by his own blood (9:15–18; 10:29; 13:20) and guarantees it by his indestructible life. Earlier the preacher called him “the guarantor (ἔγγυος engyos) of a better covenant” (7:22). As mediator he brings God and his people together; as guarantor he ensures that the covenant’s promises will be kept.

The covenant is described further: ἥτις ἐπὶ κρείττοσιν ἐπαγγελίαις νενομοθέτηται hētis epi kreittosin epangeliais nenomothetētai, “which has been enacted on the basis of better promises.” The verb νομοθετέω nomotheteō means “to legislate, to enact as law” (it is related to nomos, “law,” and to nomothetēs, “lawgiver”). The preacher used the same verb in 7:11, where he said that the people “received the law” (nenomothetētai) on the basis of the Levitical priesthood. Here the choice is pointed. The new covenant is not lawless. It too has been formally and legally enacted, with God as its legislator. But whereas the Sinai covenant was enacted in connection with the Levitical priesthood, the new covenant is enacted “on the basis of better promises.” Its foundation is not, in the first place, what the people undertake to do, but what God pledges to do. The perfect tense (“has been enacted”) again indicates a completed act with continuing force: the new covenant stands in effect now.

What are these “better promises”? The preacher does not leave us guessing; he immediately quotes them in Jeremiah’s words (8:10–12): God will put his laws into their minds and write them on their hearts; he will be their God and they will be his people; all will know him, from least to greatest; and he will be merciful to their unrighteousness and remember their sins no more. These promises are “better” not because the God who made them is different from the God of Sinai, nor because the Sinai covenant contained no promises (it did; see Exod 19:5–6; Lev 26:3–13). They are better because they promise what the first covenant could not supply: an inward transformation of the heart that ensures faithfulness, and a once-for-all cleansing of sin that perfects the conscience (9:9, 14; 10:14). The first covenant said, “Do this, and you shall live” (compare Lev 18:5); the new covenant says, “I will do this in you.”

The word “promise” (ἐπαγγελία epangelia) is a major theme in Hebrews (used more often here than in any other New Testament book). The readers are urged to imitate those “who through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12), and the preacher has reminded them of God’s promise to Abraham confirmed with an oath (6:13–18). The new covenant’s “better promises” are not in competition with the promise to Abraham; they are the means by which that promise of blessing comes to its fulfillment. In Christ, God’s people receive “the promised eternal inheritance” (9:15).

Verse 6 is the hinge of the chapter. It gathers up 8:1–5 (the more excellent ministry in the true sanctuary) and introduces 8:7–13 (the better covenant and its promises). For the readers, it answers a quiet fear. If they cling to Christ and lose the temple, they are not exchanging something real for something vague. They are exchanging a ministry of copies for a ministry of reality, a covenant that could not perfect for one that does, and promises that awaited fulfillment for promises God has already enacted in his Son.
