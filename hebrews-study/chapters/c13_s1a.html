Sacrifices Pleasing to God (13:1–19)

Translation

1Let brotherly love continue. 2Do not neglect hospitality to strangers, for through it some, without knowing it, have entertained angels. 3Remember the prisoners as though bound with them, and those who are mistreated, since you yourselves also are in the body. 4Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterers. 5Let your way of life be free from the love of money; be content with what you have, for he himself has said,

“I will never let go of you, nor will I ever forsake you.”

6So we can say with confidence,

“The Lord is my helper; I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?”

7Remember your leaders, who spoke the word of God to you; consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. 8Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever. 9Do not be carried away by various and strange teachings; for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods, which have not benefited those who walk in them. 10We have an altar from which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat. 11For the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the sanctuary by the high priest as an offering for sin are burned outside the camp. 12Therefore Jesus also, in order to sanctify the people through his own blood, suffered outside the gate. 13So then, let us go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach. 14For here we have no lasting city, but we are seeking the city that is to come. 15Through him, then, let us continually offer up to God a sacrifice of praise, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name. 16And do not neglect doing good and sharing, for with such sacrifices God is well pleased. 17Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they keep watch over your souls as those who will give an account, so that they may do this with joy and not with groaning; for that would be of no advantage to you. 18Pray for us, for we are persuaded that we have a good conscience, desiring to conduct ourselves honorably in all things. 19And I urge you all the more earnestly to do this, so that I may be restored to you the sooner.

Setting and Structure

To modern readers the move from the thunder of Sinai and Zion (12:18–29) to short instructions about hospitality and money can feel abrupt. For the author it is not. Chapter 12 ended with a call to “worship God in a way pleasing to him” (12:28). Chapter 13 shows what that worship is. The key words of these nineteen verses come from the temple: “altar” (13:10), “those who serve the tabernacle” (13:10), “blood” brought “into the sanctuary” by “the high priest” (13:11), “sanctify” (13:12), “offer up a sacrifice” (13:15), “such sacrifices” and “well pleased” (13:16). In Israel’s worship a sacrifice “pleasing to the Lord” was one he accepted, a “pleasing aroma” (Lev 1:9). The author has spent chapters 7–10 showing that Christ’s single self-offering has made the Levitical sacrifices obsolete (10:18). Still, he does not conclude that believers now have no sacrifices at all. Their sacrifices are of a different kind. They do not atone, because Christ has atoned once for all. They are offerings of gratitude: praise on the lips, and goodness and sharing toward others (13:15–16). Paul speaks in the same way when he calls believers to present their bodies as “a living sacrifice” (Rom 12:1), as does Peter when he speaks of “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ” (1 Pet 2:5).

The section falls into two parts.

  • 13:1–6: The shape of love in the community. Five brief commands come in pairs. Love for brothers and love for strangers (13:1–2). Remembering prisoners and the mistreated (13:3). Purity in marriage and freedom from love of money (13:4–5). Each command is short, often without a main verb in Greek, in the compact style of early Christian moral instruction (compare Rom 12:9–21; 1 Thess 5:12–22). The paragraph closes with two Old Testament texts (13:5–6). The first is God’s promise not to abandon his people. The second is the believer’s confident answer from Psalm 118.
  • 13:7–19: Leaders, the unchanging Christ, and the altar outside the camp. This paragraph is framed by references to “your leaders” at its beginning (13:7) and end (13:17). It closes with the author’s request for prayer (13:18–19). Within the frame, 13:8 states the center: Jesus Christ does not change. From that the author draws a warning against “various and strange teachings” about foods (13:9). He then makes his last argument from the Day of Atonement (13:10–12) and closes with three exhortations: go out to him (13:13), offer praise (13:15), and do not neglect doing good (13:16).

The two paragraphs belong together. The love, hospitality, and generosity of 13:1–6 are the “sacrifices” with which “God is well pleased” (13:16). The refusal to love money (13:5) fits a people who “have no lasting city” (13:14). The call to share (13:16) echoes their earlier generosity, when they “sympathized with the prisoners” and “joyfully accepted the plundering” of their property (10:34).

The historical setting fills out the picture. The readers were a community that had once suffered public abuse, imprisonment, and confiscation of property (10:32–34). It still had members in prison or under mistreatment (13:3). It was apparently tempted to find stability in “foods” connected with the old cult (13:9) and to avoid the social shame of identification with a crucified Messiah (13:13). In the Roman world, belonging to the synagogue meant belonging to a long-established people with ancestral customs that Roman authorities generally tolerated. A group devoted to a man executed by a Roman governor had no such standing. The appeal of drawing back, of finding again a place “inside the camp,” would have been strong. Against that pull the author sets the one fact that does not move: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever.”

Verse by Verse

13:1

Let brotherly love continue.

The first command is three words in Greek: Ἡ φιλαδελφία μενέτω hē philadelphia menetō, “Let brotherly love remain.” The command does not tell the readers to start loving one another. It tells them to keep doing what they are already doing. The author has praised their love before: “God is not unjust so as to forget your work and the love you showed for his name, in having served the saints and still serving them” (6:10). He has also urged them to “consider how to stir one another up to love and good works” (10:24). The danger he sees is not that love never existed, but that under pressure and weariness (12:3, 12) it might fade.

Word Study: φιλαδελφία philadelphia

Outside the New Testament, philadelphia meant the affection between literal brothers and sisters, children of the same parents. Greek moralists praised it, and in Jewish writings it describes the loyalty of brothers (4 Macc 13:23, 26 uses it for the seven martyred brothers). Early Christians used the word for the love between believers. They did so because they saw themselves as one family, children of one Father, with Christ as their elder brother. Hebrews has laid the ground for this. The Son “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:11). He was “made like his brothers in every respect” (2:17). The readers are “holy brothers” (3:1). In a society where kinship defined loyalty and obligation, calling fellow believers “brothers” meant they owed each other the duties of family: protection, provision, and faithfulness. The New Testament uses the word several times (Rom 12:10; 1 Thess 4:9; 1 Pet 1:22; 2 Pet 1:7).

The verb μένω menō, “remain, continue, last,” is a key word in Hebrews. Christ “remains” a priest forever (7:3, 24). The readers have a “better and lasting” possession (10:34). God will shake heaven and earth “so that the things that cannot be shaken may remain” (12:27). Here there is “no lasting city” (13:14). Placed right after 12:27, the command “let brotherly love remain” suggests that love among believers belongs to the unshakable kingdom. When everything created is shaken, faith, hope, and love remain (1 Cor 13:13).

Brotherly love comes first in the list, and the rest of the list grows out of it. Hospitality is brotherly love toward the traveler. Care for prisoners is brotherly love toward the suffering. Faithful marriage protects the family. Freedom from greed leaves something to share. Love is not one virtue among several here. It is the root from which the others grow.

13:2

Do not neglect hospitality to strangers, for through it some, without knowing it, have entertained angels.

Brotherly love (philadelphia) is followed by love of strangers, φιλοξενία philoxenia, built from philos, “loving,” and xenos, “stranger, guest.” The two words were clearly chosen to sound alike: love for the brother inside, love for the stranger at the door. The verb is μὴ ἐπιλανθάνεσθε mē epilanthanesthe, literally “do not forget.” This is the same command repeated in 13:16, “do not neglect doing good and sharing.” Hospitality was not likely to be openly rejected. It was likely to be quietly forgotten, especially when welcoming a fellow Christian brought risk or cost.

The reason given contains a play on words. ἔλαθόν τινες ξενίσαντες ἀγγέλους elathon tines xenisantes angelous means literally “some escaped notice in entertaining angels.” The Greek verb lanthanō, “escape notice, be hidden,” combined with a participle, is the normal way of saying someone did something without being aware of it. The command “do not forget” (epi-lanthanesthe) and the reason “some without knowing it” (elathon) come from the same root. In effect: do not let hospitality slip from your mind, because in hospitality some have had guests whose identity slipped past them.

Historical Background: Angels Entertained Unawares, and Ancient Hospitality

The clearest Old Testament case is Abraham at Mamre. Abraham “lifted up his eyes and looked, and behold, three men standing near him; and when he saw them he ran from the entrance of the tent to meet them and bowed down to the ground” (Gen 18:2). He fed them a calf, curds, and milk (Gen 18:8) before he understood that the Lord himself was speaking with him (Gen 18:10–14). The Hebrew says simply שְׁלֹשָׁה אֲנָשִׁים sheloshah anashim, “three men.” Abraham did not know at first who they were. The next chapter tells of Lot, who sat in the gate of Sodom and urged “the two angels” (שְׁנֵי הַמַּלְאָכִים shene hammal’akhim, Gen 19:1) to turn aside to his house, wash their feet, and spend the night. He “pressed them strongly” until they came in, and he made them a feast (Gen 19:2–3). Gideon (Judg 6:11–24) and Manoah and his wife (Judg 13:2–23) also offered food to a visitor before learning he was the angel of the Lord. In the book of Tobit, popular among Jews of the period, the angel Raphael travels with Tobias for a long journey disguised as a kinsman and is revealed only at the end (Tob 5; 12:15). Jewish tradition celebrated Abraham as the model of hospitality. Philo devotes a long passage to his welcome of the three visitors (On Abraham 107–118). At the end of the first century, the Roman church’s letter known as 1 Clement says that Abraham was given a son in old age “because of his faith and hospitality” and that Lot was saved “because of hospitality and godliness” (1 Clem 10:7; 11:1).

Hospitality also met a practical need. Inns in the Roman world had a poor reputation. They were often dirty and dangerous, and they were widely associated with prostitution and heavy drinking. Travelers who could stay with family, friends, or members of an association did so. For Christians the need was pressing. Missionaries, messengers carrying letters, believers fleeing persecution, and ordinary travelers on business depended on finding a Christian home in each city. Much of the spread of the gospel depended on this network of open homes. Hospitality was therefore required of every believer (Rom 12:13; 1 Pet 4:9, “without grumbling”) and especially of overseers (1 Tim 3:2; Titus 1:8). John commends Gaius for receiving traveling brothers who were “strangers” to him, and says, “we ought to support such people, so that we may be fellow workers for the truth” (3 John 5–8). Because hospitality could be abused, early instruction also set limits on it. The Didache says that a traveling apostle should be welcomed but should stay only a day, or two if needed, and that one who stays three days or asks for money is a false prophet (Did. 11:4–6). Generosity and discernment went together, as Hebrews 13:9 will also show.

The point is not that Christians should be hospitable in the hope of meeting an angel. Abraham and Lot did not know who their guests were. The point is that hospitality may carry a significance far greater than the host can see. Jesus made the same point more strongly: “I was a stranger and you welcomed me” (Matt 25:35). The righteous do not know until the judgment whom they have served (Matt 25:37–40). There is also a fitting connection with the earlier argument. The readers are themselves “strangers and exiles on the earth” (11:13), with no lasting city (13:14). People who know they are pilgrims should open their homes to other pilgrims. Israel was told to love the stranger for the same reason: “you know the heart of a stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (Exod 23:9; compare Lev 19:34; Deut 10:19).

13:3

Remember the prisoners as though bound with them, and those who are mistreated, since you yourselves also are in the body.

The third command reaches beyond the believer’s home to the prison. μιμνῄσκεσθε τῶν δεσμίων mimnēskesthe tōn desmiōn means “remember the prisoners.” In biblical usage, “remember” means more than recalling. It means paying attention to someone in a way that leads to action. When God “remembered” Noah (Gen 8:1) or his covenant (Exod 2:24), he acted. The readers are to remember the prisoners in that way: visit them, feed them, pray for them, and speak for them.

The manner of remembering is ὡς συνδεδεμένοι hōs syndedemenoi, “as though bound with them,” a perfect participle meaning “as those who have been chained together with them.” Believers are to feel the chain as if it were on their own wrists. This is the community’s earlier example renewed. In the “former days” they “sympathized with the prisoners” (τοῖς δεσμίοις συνεπαθήσατε tois desmiois synepathēsate, 10:34). The word “prisoners” is the same there and here. Some of those who suffered earlier may still be in custody, or new arrests may have taken place. The author asks the church to keep doing what it once did gladly.

Historical Background: Roman Prisons

Roman prisons were usually not places where sentences were served. They were places of custody, where the accused were held until trial, until execution, or until a magistrate decided what to do with them. The state provided little or nothing. Prisoners depended on family and friends for food, clothing, and other basic needs. Cells were often dark, crowded, and filthy, and prisoners might be chained. Visiting a prisoner, especially one held for a disreputable cause, could expose the visitor to shame and suspicion. That is why the New Testament praises those who were not ashamed of a prisoner. Onesiphorus “often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain,” Paul writes. When he came to Rome, Onesiphorus searched for Paul diligently until he found him (2 Tim 1:16–17). The Philippians sent Epaphroditus with gifts to Paul in custody (Phil 2:25; 4:18). Even outsiders noticed this Christian practice. In the second century the satirist Lucian mocked the Christians for their devotion to an imprisoned man, Peregrinus. He describes them bringing him meals, attending him from early morning, and spending money on him (The Passing of Peregrinus 12–13). Lucian intended ridicule, but he bears witness to a church that obeyed Hebrews 13:3.

The second group is τῶν κακουχουμένων tōn kakouchoumenōn, “those who are mistreated” or “ill-treated.” The same verb described the heroes of faith in 11:37: “destitute, afflicted, mistreated.” The faithful of old suffered in this way, and believers in the present should stand with those who suffer it now.

The reason is ὡς καὶ αὐτοὶ ὄντες ἐν σώματι hōs kai autoi ontes en sōmati, “since you yourselves also are in the body.” A few interpreters have taken “the body” as the church, the body of Christ, in which, as Paul says, “if one member suffers, all suffer together” (1 Cor 12:26). That truth is biblical, but it is probably not the point here. The Greek has no article (“in body,” not “in the body of Christ”). Hebrews never uses “body” for the church. And the parallel with “bound with them” points to physical vulnerability. The readers are themselves living in the flesh, open to the same chains and blows. What happened to the prisoners could happen to them tomorrow. In 10:33 the readers had sometimes suffered directly and sometimes become “partners” with those who did. The author asks for imaginative solidarity: treat the prisoner as you would want to be treated in his place (Matt 7:12). There is also a Christ-shaped pattern here. The Son shared “blood and flesh” (2:14), and therefore “he is able to sympathize with our weaknesses” (4:15). Those who have such a high priest learn to sympathize as he does.

13:4

Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterers.

From prison the author turns to the home. The Greek has no verb: τίμιος ὁ γάμος ἐν πᾶσιν καὶ ἡ κοίτη ἀμίαντος timios ho gamos en pasin kai hē koitē amiantos, “honored the marriage among all, and the bed undefiled.” Grammatically, “is” could be supplied, making it a statement (“marriage is honorable”). But the surrounding verses are all commands, and the warning of judgment that follows shows that the author is urging conduct, not simply stating a principle. So most interpreters supply “let … be.” The word τίμιος timios means “precious, valuable, held in honor.” Peter uses it of the “precious blood of Christ” (1 Pet 1:19). Marriage is to be treated as something of great worth.

The phrase ἐν πᾶσιν en pasin can be read as “among all people” (masculine) or “in every respect” (neuter). Both make good sense. On the first reading, everyone in the community, married or single, is responsible to honor marriage, both their own and that of others. On the second, marriage is to be honored in every part of life. The parallel with “all” in 13:18 (“in all things”) allows the neuter, but the emphasis on community in the paragraph slightly favors “among all.” Either way, the command shuts the door both to those who would defile marriage and to those who would despise it.

Word Study: κοίτη koitē and ἀμίαντος amiantos

Koitē means “bed,” and in particular the marriage bed. Like the English “bed” in some expressions, it was a modest way of referring to sexual union (compare Rom 13:13, where the plural means “sexual excess”). Amiantos means “undefiled, unstained.” It is a temple word: what is amiantos is ritually and morally clean, fit for God. Hebrews has used it once before, of Christ himself: our high priest is “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26). James uses it of “pure and undefiled religion” (Jas 1:27), and Peter of the believer’s “undefiled” inheritance (1 Pet 1:4). The choice is significant. The author has just warned against the “root of bitterness” by which “many are defiled” (μιανθῶσιν mianthōsin, 12:15) and against anyone who is “sexually immoral or godless like Esau” (12:16). Sexual faithfulness in marriage is part of the holiness “without which no one will see the Lord” (12:14). The marriage bed is to be kept as clean as the things of the sanctuary.

The reason follows: πόρνους γὰρ καὶ μοιχοὺς κρινεῖ ὁ θεός pornous gar kai moichous krinei ho theos, “for the sexually immoral and adulterers God will judge.” Two words cover two kinds of sin. πόρνος pornos (from the same root as porneia) is broad: a person who practices any sexual activity outside marriage. μοιχός moichos is narrower: one who violates a marriage, his own or someone else’s. This is the seventh commandment, לֹא תִּנְאָף lo tin’af, “You shall not commit adultery” (Exod 20:14). In Greek the word “God” comes last, for emphasis. People may tolerate or excuse such sins, and they may be hidden from human courts, but God will judge. The warning recalls the solemn language of the sermon: “The Lord will judge his people. It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God” (10:30–31). They have come to “God, the judge of all” (12:23), and “our God is a consuming fire” (12:29).

Historical Background: Sexual Ethics in the Greco-Roman World

Christian teaching on marriage stood out sharply in its setting. In Greco-Roman society a wife was expected to be faithful to her husband, and adultery with a married woman of citizen rank was treated as a serious offense against her husband and family. Augustus made it a public crime by law in 18 BC. But a husband’s sexual relations with slaves, prostitutes, or unmarried women of low status were widely tolerated and seldom considered adultery at all. Male sexual freedom was common, and prostitution was legal and ordinary. Against this double standard, the Christian message applied one standard to both husband and wife. Each belongs to the other (1 Cor 7:2–4). Marriage is a lifelong union in which “the two become one flesh” (Gen 2:24; Mark 10:8), and every sexual relationship outside it is sin (1 Thess 4:3–6; 1 Cor 6:9–20).

There was an opposite error. Some religious and philosophical movements, and later certain Christian groups, regarded marriage and sexual relations as inferior or even defiling. Paul warns against those “who forbid marriage and require abstinence from foods, which God created to be received with thanksgiving” (1 Tim 4:3). It is worth noticing that the false teaching in 1 Timothy links marriage and foods, the same pair that occurs in Hebrews 13:4 and 13:9. Hebrews 13:4 answers both errors at once. Marriage is honorable, against those who would despise it. The marriage bed must be undefiled, against those who would abuse it. Scripture does the same. The Song of Songs and Proverbs celebrate married love (“rejoice in the wife of your youth,” Prov 5:18). Paul warns married couples not to deprive each other (1 Cor 7:5). And Jesus honored the wedding at Cana with his first sign (John 2:1–11).

Coming between hospitality and money, the command fits a pattern. The open home of 13:2 could be a place of temptation as well as service. Sexual sin and greed are paired again and again in the New Testament’s lists of sins (1 Cor 5:10–11; 6:9–10; Eph 5:3, 5; Col 3:5). Both are forms of grasping desire: taking what God has not given. The next verse turns to the second.

13:5

Let your way of life be free from the love of money; be content with what you have, for he himself has said, “I will never let go of you, nor will I ever forsake you.”

Again the Greek has no verb: ἀφιλάργυρος ὁ τρόπος aphilargyros ho tropos, “free from love of money, the way of life.” τρόπος tropos means “manner, way,” and here “character” or “way of living.” ἀφιλάργυρος aphilargyros is made of a- (“not”), philos (“loving”), and argyros (“silver,” and so “money”). This is a third phil- word in five verses. The church is to love the brothers (philadelphia) and to love strangers (philoxenia), but not to love silver. Paul requires the same quality in overseers (1 Tim 3:3) and calls the love of money “a root of all kinds of evils” (1 Tim 6:10). Luke describes certain Pharisees as “lovers of money” who scoffed at Jesus’ teaching on wealth (Luke 16:14).

The positive side is ἀρκούμενοι τοῖς παροῦσιν arkoumenoi tois parousin, “being content with the things that are present,” that is, with what you have now. The participle continues the command. Paul uses the same verb: “if we have food and covering, with these we will be content” (1 Tim 6:8). He speaks of having learned contentment in plenty and in want (Phil 4:11–13). For these readers the matter was not abstract. They had once “joyfully accepted the plundering of [their] property, knowing that [they] had a better and lasting possession” (10:34). Some may now be tempted to make up their losses or to protect what they still have by avoiding further shame. Love of money could easily make a person unwilling to go “outside the camp” (13:13). Contentment comes from knowing what one already has: a lasting possession, an unshakable kingdom (12:28), a city to come (13:14), and most of all the presence of God.

That is the reason the author gives: αὐτὸς γὰρ εἴρηκεν autos gar eirēken, “for he himself has said.” “He himself” is God, and the words are emphatic. The perfect tense eirēken means God has spoken and the word still stands. Hebrews often introduces Scripture in this way, as God’s present speech (1:5–13; 3:7; 4:3–4; 10:9). The ground of contentment is not that we have enough money. It is that we have God, who has promised never to leave us.

Word Study: The Five Negatives of Οὐ μή σε ἀνῶ οὐδ’ οὐ μή σε ἐγκαταλίπω

The quotation in Greek reads Ou mē se anō oud’ ou mē se enkatalipō. Greek can deny something simply with ou, “not.” To deny it as strongly as possible, Greek combines two negatives, οὐ μή ou mē, with an aorist subjunctive. This construction means “certainly not, by no means, never.” Here it occurs twice, joined by οὐδ’ oud’, “nor,” giving five negative words in one short sentence. English cannot copy this without awkwardness, but its force is something like, “I will never, no never, let you go; nor will I ever, ever forsake you.” Older English preachers liked to point out these five negatives, and they were right to feel the emphasis.

The first verb, ἀνῶ anō, from ἀνίημι aniēmi, means “loosen, let go, let drop, leave unsupported.” The second, ἐγκαταλίπω enkatalipō, from ἐγκαταλείπω enkataleipō, means “leave behind, abandon, forsake,” especially to leave someone helpless in distress. This is the verb Jesus used on the cross: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Matt 27:46; Mark 15:34, quoting Ps 22:1). It is also the verb of Hebrews 10:25: “not forsaking our own assembling together.” The one who was forsaken in our place has secured this promise for us. And those whom God will never forsake must not forsake one another.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 31:6, 8; Joshua 1:5

The promise does not match any single Old Testament verse word for word. It gathers a promise God made repeatedly at turning points in Israel’s history. When Moses was about to die and Israel was about to cross the Jordan, he told the people: “Be strong and courageous; do not fear or tremble before them, for the Lord your God is the one who goes with you. He will not let you go, and he will not forsake you” (Deut 31:6). Hebrew: לֹא יַרְפְּךָ וְלֹא יַעַזְבֶךָּ — lo yarpekha ve-lo ya‘azvekka, “he will not let you drop, and he will not forsake you.” He said the same to Joshua (Deut 31:8). Then God himself said to Joshua, in the first person: כַּאֲשֶׁר הָיִיתִי עִם־מֹשֶׁה אֶהְיֶה עִמָּךְ לֹא אַרְפְּךָ וְלֹא אֶעֶזְבֶךָּ — ka’asher hayiti ‘im-Mosheh ehyeh ‘immakh, lo arpekha ve-lo e‘ezvekka, “As I was with Moses, I will be with you. I will not let you drop, and I will not forsake you” (Josh 1:5). David passed the same words on to Solomon as he began building the temple (1 Chr 28:20), and God had said something similar to Jacob as he fled from home: “I will not forsake you until I have done what I have promised you” (Gen 28:15).

The Hebrew verb רָפָה rafah in this form means “let drop, let go, let sink.” It is the picture of a hand loosening its grip. The Greek aniēmi, “loosen, let go,” renders it well. The second verb, עָזַב ‘azav, means “leave, abandon,” and matches enkataleipō. The form in Hebrews is first person, as in Joshua 1:5: God himself is speaking. The exact wording of Hebrews, with its doubled emphatic negatives, does not match the usual Greek translation of any one of these verses. However, Philo quotes the promise in exactly the same wording as Hebrews (On the Confusion of Tongues 166). This suggests that the promise circulated in this emphatic form in Greek-speaking Judaism, perhaps through use in worship or preaching.

The setting matters for Hebrews. These words were spoken to a people on the edge of the promised land, with enemies ahead and their leader about to be taken from them. The readers are in the same position. They are a pilgrim people who have not yet entered God’s rest (4:1–11). Earlier leaders have died (13:7). Opposition is real. Hebrews has already compared them to the wilderness generation and named Joshua (4:8). The promise given to Israel at the Jordan is now spoken to the church: the God who was with Moses and with Joshua will not let go of you.

13:6

So we can say with confidence, “The Lord is my helper; I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?”

God has spoken (13:5); now his people answer. ὥστε θαρροῦντας ἡμᾶς λέγειν hōste tharrountas hēmas legein, “so that we, being confident, say.” θαρρέω tharreō means “be of good courage, be bold.” Paul uses it of the confidence of believers facing death (2 Cor 5:6, 8). This is the pattern of biblical faith: God’s promise first, and then the believer’s confession built on it. The author does not give his readers a slogan to repeat to themselves. He gives them God’s own words to take up and pray back to him. Elsewhere he speaks of the “confession” the readers must hold fast (4:14; 10:23). This is one form of that confession.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 118:6 (LXX 117:6)

Hebrew: יְהוָה לִי לֹא אִירָא מַה־יַּעֲשֶׂה לִי אָדָם — YHWH li, lo ira; mah-ya‘aseh li adam, “The Lord is for me; I will not fear. What can man do to me?” (Ps 118:6). The Hebrew YHWH li, literally “the Lord is to me,” means “the Lord is on my side.” The next verse continues: יְהוָה לִי בְּעֹזְרָי — YHWH li be‘ozrai, “the Lord is for me among my helpers” (Ps 118:7).

Hebrews quotes the Greek translation exactly: Κύριος ἐμοὶ βοηθός, οὐ φοβηθήσομαι· τί ποιήσει μοι ἄνθρωπος; Kyrios emoi boēthos, ou phobēthēsomai; ti poiēsei moi anthrōpos? The Greek translators made the Hebrew’s brief “the Lord is for me” explicit as “the Lord is my helper.” This was probably influenced by “my helpers” in the next verse. The sense is the same. If God is on my side, he is my helper (compare Rom 8:31, “If God is for us, who can be against us?”). Here “Lord” (Kyrios) stands for the divine name YHWH. The word βοηθός boēthos, “helper,” links with two earlier promises in Hebrews. Because he suffered when tempted, Jesus “is able to help those who are being tempted” (2:18). And believers come to the throne of grace to find “grace for timely help” (4:16).

Psalm 118 is a song of thanksgiving by one whom God rescued when surrounded by enemies (118:10–13). It was sung at the great festivals, and it was among the Hallel psalms sung at Passover. The early church loved it. It speaks of “the stone the builders rejected” that became the cornerstone (118:22; Mark 12:10; Acts 4:11; 1 Pet 2:7), and of “blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” (118:26; Mark 11:9). The readers would know it as the psalm of the rejected one whom God vindicated.

The question “What can man do to me?” does not deny that people can do terrible things. The readers knew about prison, plunder, and public abuse (10:32–34). Jesus himself said that persecutors can kill the body (Matt 10:28). The question means that nothing human beings do can undo what God has promised or reach the life that God protects. Hebrews has already shown this courage in its heroes of faith. Moses’ parents “were not afraid of the king’s edict” (11:23), and Moses left Egypt “not fearing the anger of the king, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible” (11:27). The fear of God drives out the fear of man. The readers are to “fear” falling short (4:1) and to remember that “it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God” (10:31). People who fear God in that way are free from the fear of people.

The two quotations in 13:5–6 also complete the moral commands before them. A person who is free from the love of money is not a person with nothing. He is a person with God, and therefore with enough. Material security is the shelter many seek against an uncertain future, and the author removes it by pointing to a better shelter: “The Lord is my helper.”
