4:11

Let us therefore strive to enter that rest, so that no one may fall by following the same pattern of disobedience.

The exposition of 4:3–10 now turns into exhortation, closing the frame opened in 4:1. There the preacher said, “Let us fear”; here he says, “Let us strive.” The verb is σπουδάσωμεν spoudasōmen, another hortatory subjunctive (“let us …”), placed first for emphasis. The word “therefore” (οὖν oun) ties the command to everything just proved: since a Sabbath-rest truly remains, and since it is God’s own rest, it is worth every effort to enter it.

Word Study: σπουδάζω spoudazō

The verb means “to hasten, to be eager, to make every effort, to be diligent.” It combines speed and seriousness. Paul uses it when he tells Timothy, “Be diligent to present yourself to God as one approved” (2 Tim 2:15), and Peter when he urges, “be all the more diligent to make your calling and election sure” (2 Pet 1:10). The related noun σπουδή spoudē, “eagerness, diligence,” appears in Hebrews 6:11–12: “we desire each one of you to show the same diligence for the full assurance of hope until the end, so that you may not be sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience inherit the promises.” That passage is the best commentary on this one. The opposite of diligence is sluggishness (nōthros, 5:11; 6:12), the drifting, half-hearted condition the preacher fears in his hearers. The diligence he calls for is the diligence of faith and patience.

There is a paradox here that should not be missed: “let us strive to enter rest.” How can one labor to enter rest, especially a rest described in 4:10 as ceasing from one’s works? The answer is that the striving is not an effort to earn the rest. The rest is God’s gift, prepared from the foundation of the world (4:3) and entered by faith (4:3). The striving is the earnest, persevering exercise of that faith—holding fast (3:6, 14), listening to God’s voice “Today” (4:7), resisting the hardening of the heart (3:13), refusing to drift (2:1). Israel did not fail in the wilderness because they did not work hard enough; they failed because they did not believe God. The striving that Hebrews calls for is the opposite of what Israel did: it is wholehearted trust that keeps going until it arrives. Jesus spoke in similar terms: “Strive to enter through the narrow door” (Luke 13:24). Grace does not make effort unnecessary; it makes effort possible and purposeful.

The goal is stated negatively: “so that no one may fall.” Again the concern is for each individual (“no one,” τις tis; compare 3:12–13; 4:1). The verb “fall” (πέσῃ pesē, from πίπτω piptō) recalls 3:17, where the corpses of the rebels “fell in the wilderness,” echoing Numbers 14:29: בַּמִּדְבָּר הַזֶּה יִפְּלוּ פִגְרֵיכֶם bammidbar hazzeh yippelu figreikhem, “in this wilderness your corpses shall fall.” The fall in view is not a minor stumble but the final ruin of those who refuse God’s word.

They fall “by the same pattern of disobedience.” The noun ὑπόδειγμα hypodeigma means “example, pattern, model, copy.” Hebrews uses it later for the earthly sanctuary as a “copy” of the heavenly (8:5; 9:23). Here the wilderness generation is a pattern—a warning example. The Greek phrase can be understood in two ways: that no one should fall by imitating the same pattern of disobedience, or that no one should fall and so become another example of disobedience like them. Both thoughts are true, and both are present in the passage. The first is probably primary: the danger is that the hearers will repeat Israel’s pattern of hearing without believing. Paul draws the same lesson from the same events: “these things happened to them as examples, and they were written down for our instruction, on whom the ends of the ages have come” (1 Cor 10:11; compare 10:6). The Old Testament stories are not merely history; they are given by God as warnings and patterns for his people in every generation.

4:12

For the word of God is living and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing as far as the division of soul and spirit, of joints and marrow, and able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.

The word “for” (γάρ gar) gives the reason for the urgent call of 4:11. Why strive to enter, and why fear to fall? Because the word that holds out the promise of rest is not a dead letter. It is the living speech of God, and it searches the heart. The voice that says “Today” is the voice that judges. Verses 12–13 form a rhythmic, almost hymnic conclusion to the whole exposition of Psalm 95 begun at 3:7. In the Greek, the first word of the sentence is Ζῶν Zōn, “living,” placed first for emphasis.

Interpretive Options: What is “the word of God” here?

  • Christ, the personal Word. Some early Christian interpreters took “the word” as the divine Logos of John 1:1, the Son himself. Verse 13 speaks of “his eyes” and of the one “to whom we must give account,” which can sound personal.
  • God’s spoken and written word. Most interpreters understand “the word of God” as God’s speech—the message he has spoken, now heard in Scripture and in the proclamation of the gospel.

The second view is clearly right in this context. Hebrews never uses logos as a title for Christ. Just two verses earlier, “the word” (logos) was “the message they heard” (4:2). The whole passage has been about God speaking through Psalm 95: “as the Holy Spirit says” (3:7), “he has said” (4:3, 4), “Today, if you hear his voice” (3:7, 15; 4:7). The sermon opened with “God … has spoken to us in a Son” (1:1–2), and it will close with “See that you do not refuse him who is speaking” (12:25). Elsewhere Hebrews speaks of having tasted “the good word of God” (6:5) and of leaders who “spoke the word of God to you” (13:7). The “word of God” in 4:12 is God’s own speech, addressed to his people through the Scriptures and supremely through his Son. This does not separate the word from Christ, for God’s final word is spoken in the Son; but the immediate reference is to the word that the hearers hear “Today.”

Living. The word is “living” (ζῶν zōn) because it is the word of “the living God” (3:12; 10:31; 12:22). It is not like a law code carved on a monument, whose authors have long since died. It carries the life and presence of the one who speaks it. As Moses told Israel as they were about to enter the land, כִּי לֹא־דָבָר רֵק הוּא מִכֶּם כִּי־הוּא חַיֵּיכֶם ki lo-davar req hu mikkem, ki-hu chayyeikhem, “for it is not an empty word for you; it is your life” (Deut 32:47). Stephen called the words given at Sinai “living oracles” (Acts 7:38), and Peter speaks of “the living and abiding word of God” (1 Pet 1:23). Psalm 95 had been written centuries earlier, yet for the preacher it was God’s present voice. That is why the warning is so serious: the same God is speaking to them now.

Active. The word is also “active” (ἐνεργής energēs, “effective, at work,” the source of English “energy”). God’s word does not merely describe; it accomplishes. Through Isaiah God declares, כֵּן יִהְיֶה דְבָרִי אֲשֶׁר יֵצֵא מִפִּי לֹא־יָשׁוּב אֵלַי רֵיקָם ken yihyeh devari asher yetse mippi lo-yashuv elai reqam, “so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty” (Isa 55:11), but shall accomplish what God purposes. Through Jeremiah he asks, הֲלוֹא כֹה דְבָרִי כָּאֵשׁ נְאֻם־יְהוָה וּכְפַטִּישׁ יְפֹצֵץ סָלַע halo khoh devari ka’esh ne’um-YHWH ukhefattish yefotsets sala‘, “Is not my word like fire, declares the Lord, and like a hammer that shatters rock?” (Jer 23:29). Paul thanks God that the Thessalonians received the message as “the word of God, which is at work [energeitai] in you who believe” (1 Thess 2:13). The word that created the world (11:3) and upholds it (1:3) is the same word that works in the hearts of those who hear it. It never leaves its hearers unchanged: it either softens or hardens, saves or judges.

Sharper than any two-edged sword. The comparative adjective τομώτερος tomōteros means “sharper, more cutting.” The word μάχαιρα machaira refers to a short sword or large knife—the weapon of a soldier in close combat, and also the knife used in sacrifice. The adjective δίστομος distomos, “two-edged,” literally means “two-mouthed.” This reflects a Hebrew idiom in which the edge of a sword is its “mouth”: חֶרֶב פִּיּוֹת cherev piyyot, “a sword of mouths,” a two-edged sword (Prov 5:4; compare Ps 149:6). A sword that devours with its “mouth” is a fitting image for the word that comes from God’s mouth. The servant of the Lord says, וַיָּשֶׂם פִּי כְּחֶרֶב חַדָּה wayyasem pi kecherev chaddah, “he made my mouth like a sharp sword” (Isa 49:2). Paul calls “the word of God” the “sword [machaira] of the Spirit” (Eph 6:17), and John sees the risen Christ with a sharp two-edged sword coming from his mouth (Rev 1:16; 19:15). In the Wisdom of Solomon, God’s all-powerful word is pictured leaping down from heaven like a warrior bearing the sharp sword of his command (Wis 18:15–16). The image is of a word that cuts cleanly and deeply, missing nothing.

There may also be a deliberate echo of the wilderness story. When the generation that had refused to enter the land changed its mind the next morning and tried to go up without God, Moses warned them, וּנְפַלְתֶּם בֶּחָרֶב unefaltem becharev, “and you shall fall by the sword” (Num 14:43), and they were struck down (Num 14:45). Those who fell in the desert fell before the sword. Hebrews warns its hearers that a sharper sword than any Amalekite blade confronts those who refuse God’s voice: the word of God itself.

Piercing as far as the division of soul and spirit, of joints and marrow. The participle διϊκνούμενος diikneoumenos means “going through, penetrating, reaching all the way.” The phrase ἄχρι μερισμοῦ achri merismou means “as far as the dividing” or “to the point of separation.” What follows are two pairs: “soul and spirit” (ψυχῆς καὶ πνεύματος psychēs kai pneumatos), “joints and marrow” (ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν harmōn te kai myelōn). The first pair describes the inner, immaterial life of a person; the second describes the hidden depths of the body—the joints where bones meet, and the marrow hidden inside the bones.

Interpretive Options: “The division of soul and spirit”

  • The word separates soul from spirit. Some have read the verse as teaching that the human being has two distinct immaterial parts, a “soul” and a “spirit,” and that the word of God divides one from the other—for example, distinguishing what is merely natural in us from what is spiritual. Appeal is made to 1 Thessalonians 5:23, “spirit and soul and body.”
  • The word penetrates to the innermost depths of the whole person. Most interpreters understand the phrase as a vivid, cumulative way of saying that the word reaches all the way in, to the deepest and most hidden parts of a person, inner and outer. It does not cut the soul off from the spirit; it pierces through both.

The second view is much more likely. The parallel phrase “joints and marrow” shows that the author is not offering a lesson in anatomy. Joints and marrow are not normally separated from each other by a sword-stroke; the point is that the blade reaches the hidden places that ordinary blades cannot reach. In the same way “soul and spirit” together describe the inner life in its depth. Scripture often uses “soul” and “spirit” in parallel for the same inner self. Mary sings, “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior” (Luke 1:46–47). Jesus says, “Now my soul is troubled” (John 12:27), and shortly after the evangelist writes that he “was troubled in spirit” (John 13:21). The verse is not built to settle debates about how many parts make up a human being. Its message is that nothing in us—no layer of thought, feeling, or bodily life—lies beyond the reach of God’s word.

Able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart. The final phrase moves from the image of a sword to the image of a judge. The adjective κριτικός kritikos means “able to judge, discerning” (English “critic” and “critical” come from it). It appears only here in the New Testament. The word of God is not something we sit in judgment over; it sits in judgment over us. It judges “the thoughts” (ἐνθυμήσεων enthymēseōn, “reflections, inward deliberations”; compare Matt 9:4, where Jesus knows the “thoughts” of the scribes) and “intentions” (ἐννοιῶν ennoiōn, “purposes, attitudes of mind”; compare 1 Pet 4:1) “of the heart” (καρδίας kardias).

The mention of the heart is not accidental. The heart has been central throughout this exposition: “do not harden your hearts” (3:8, 15; 4:7), “they always go astray in their heart” (3:10), “an evil heart of unbelief” (3:12). Unbelief begins in the heart, often hidden even from the person himself. The heart can deceive; Jeremiah says, עָקֹב הַלֵּב מִכֹּל וְאָנֻשׁ הוּא מִי יֵדָעֶנּוּ ‘aqov hallev mikkol we’anush hu, mi yeda‘ennu, “The heart is more deceitful than all things, and incurably sick; who can know it?” and God answers, “I the Lord search the heart” (Jer 17:9–10). The word of God does what we cannot do for ourselves. It exposes the beginnings of hardening—the first drift, the secret reluctance, the unspoken unbelief—before they become settled. A congregation tempted to shrink back while keeping up appearances needed to hear this. One may deceive fellow believers, but not the word of God.

Pastorally, this verse is both a warning and a mercy. It is a warning because no one can hide behind outward religion. It is a mercy because the sharp blade of the word is the surgeon’s knife as well as the judge’s sword. God exposes in order to heal. He brings the hidden things of the heart to light so that they may be confessed and cleansed, and he does it while it is still “Today.” The preacher has just urged his hearers to “exhort one another every day” (3:13); the word of God is the chief instrument of that exhortation. Those who read Scripture discover that Scripture reads them.

4:13

And no creature is hidden before him, but all things are naked and laid bare to the eyes of him to whom we must give account.

Verse 12 described the penetrating power of God’s word; verse 13 moves seamlessly from the word to God himself. “No creature is hidden before him” (οὐκ ἔστιν κτίσις ἀφανὴς ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ ouk estin ktisis aphanēs enōpion autou). The pronoun “him” could grammatically refer to “the word,” but the following phrases—“his eyes” and “to whom we must give account”—show that God himself is in view. The shift is natural, because God’s word is God speaking. To be addressed by his word is to stand in his presence.

The noun κτίσις ktisis means “creature” or “created thing.” Everything God made—every person, every angel, every thought—is included. The adjective ἀφανής aphanēs means “invisible, hidden from view.” Nothing is concealed from him. The psalmist confesses this: יְהוָה חֲקַרְתַּנִי וַתֵּדָע YHWH chaqartani watteda‘, “Lord, you have searched me and known me” (Ps 139:1), and adds that even darkness is not dark to God (Ps 139:11–12). Proverbs observes, שְׁאוֹל וַאֲבַדּוֹן נֶגֶד יְהוָה אַף כִּי־לִבּוֹת בְּנֵי־אָדָם she’ol wa’avaddon neged YHWH, af ki-libbot benei-adam, “Sheol and Destruction lie open before the Lord—how much more the hearts of the children of man!” (Prov 15:11). From the first sin, human beings have tried to hide from God; Adam said, “I heard your voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself” (Gen 3:10). Hebrews tells its hearers that hiding is impossible.

Instead, “all things are naked and laid bare to his eyes.” The adjective γυμνά gymna means “naked, uncovered.” The second word is unusual and vivid.

Word Study: τετραχηλισμένα tetrachēlismena

This is the perfect passive participle of τραχηλίζω trachēlizō, a verb formed from τράχηλος trachēlos, “neck, throat.” It occurs only here in the Bible. The perfect tense describes a state: everything stands exposed. Its basic sense in context is clear—“laid bare, exposed, helpless”—but the picture behind it has been explained in several ways:

  • Wrestling. In Greek usage the verb could describe a wrestler seizing an opponent by the neck and throwing him down, leaving him helpless. Philo uses it of people being overpowered or brought down. On this view, all creatures are like a defeated wrestler, pinned and unable to escape the gaze of the victor.
  • Sacrifice. The neck of a sacrificial animal was bent back to expose the throat to the knife, and after slaughter the victim was flayed so that all its inner parts lay open. Chrysostom explained the word by the image of a sacrificial victim stripped and opened so that everything within is visible. The mention of a machaira, which could be a sacrificial knife, in 4:12 might support this.
  • Exposure of a prisoner. Some have suggested an image of a condemned person whose head is forced back so that his face is exposed to the view of all, unable to look away or hide.

We cannot be certain which picture the author had in mind, and he may have used the word simply in its derived sense, “exposed.” All the proposed images share the same point: complete, defenseless exposure. The neck, the most vulnerable part of the body, is bared. Nothing can be covered, and no resistance is possible. Joined with “naked,” the word paints a picture of total transparency before the God who sees.

The verse ends with a phrase that is short and hard to translate: πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος pros hon hēmin ho logos. Word for word it says, “toward whom to us the word.” The word logos can mean “word,” “message,” or “account, reckoning.” The phrase has been taken in several ways:

  • “To whom we must give account.” Logos often means a financial or judicial “account,” as in the expression “to give an account.” Hebrews uses exactly that idiom in 13:17, where leaders keep watch over souls “as those who will give an account” (logon apodōsontes). Jesus warned that people will “give an account” for every careless word on the day of judgment (Matt 12:36), and Paul says that “each of us will give an account of himself to God” (Rom 14:12; compare 1 Pet 4:5).
  • “With whom we have to do.” Some older translations take the phrase more generally: God is the one with whom we have our dealings, the one we must reckon with.
  • “About whom we are speaking.” A few take it as a reference to the subject of discourse.

The first view fits best. The context is judgment: the word judges the heart (4:12), and nothing is hidden from God’s eyes (4:13). The picture is of the final reckoning, when every life will be laid open before the divine Judge. The second view is not far from it, and the older rendering “with whom we have to do” has its own solemn force. But the closing word of the phrase also creates a powerful wordplay. Verse 12 began with “the logos of God”; verse 13 ends with “our logos.” God has spoken his word to us; we must answer him with our account. The word God speaks to us will be followed by the word we speak to him. The question is whether we will be among those who heard his voice “Today” and did not harden their hearts.

Here the exposition of Psalm 95 ends, on a note of utter seriousness. If the sermon stopped here, the hearers might be left trembling with no place to stand. But it does not stop here. The very next words are, “Since then we have a great high priest” (4:14). The God before whom all things lie naked has himself provided one who represents us before him, who knows our weakness, and through whom we may draw near—not to a tribunal of terror, but to a throne of grace. The word exposes; the priest intercedes. Those who have been laid bare by the word are the very ones invited to approach with boldness.

For Reflection

  1. The preacher says “let us fear” (4:1) and, a few verses later, “let us draw near with boldness” (4:16). How do reverent fear of falling short and confident approach to God belong together in the Christian life? Which of the two do you tend to neglect?
  2. Israel heard good news but was not “united by faith” with those who listened (4:2). In what ways can a person hear God’s word regularly and yet receive no benefit from it? What role does the believing community play in helping us hear with faith?
  3. Hebrews teaches that the rest offered to us is God’s own Sabbath rest, which has existed since creation (4:3–4, 9). How does it change your view of salvation to see it as sharing in God’s own rest and joy, and not only as rescue from trouble?
  4. The “Today” of Psalm 95 remained open in David’s time and in the preacher’s time (4:7). What would it look like for you to treat God’s voice in Scripture as addressed to you today, rather than as a word for another day?
  5. How do “resting from our works” (4:10) and “striving to enter” (4:11) fit together? Where are you tempted either to try to earn God’s favor or to grow sluggish in faith?
  6. The word of God judges “the thoughts and intentions of the heart,” and we must give account to God (4:12–13). How should this shape the way you read and hear Scripture? How does the following promise of a sympathetic high priest (4:14–16) keep this truth from leading to despair?
