2:10

For it was fitting for him, for whom are all things and through whom are all things, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings.

Verse 9 ended with the death of Jesus. To many in the ancient world, Jew and Gentile alike, that was the great stumbling block of the Christian message (1 Cor 1:23). A crucified Messiah seemed a contradiction in terms, and a suffering Son of God seemed unworthy of God. The readers, under pressure and shame, may have felt the force of such objections themselves. Verse 10 answers them head-on. The “for” (γάρ gar) introduces an explanation of why Jesus’ suffering and death were not an embarrassment but exactly what was right.

Word Study: Ἔπρεπεν Eprepen, “it was fitting”

The verb πρέπω prepō means “to be fitting, suitable, appropriate, becoming.” It describes what corresponds to someone’s character or position. Paul uses it of conduct that “befits” saints (Eph 5:3) and that “befits” sound teaching (Titus 2:1). The imperfect tense here looks at the whole of God’s saving plan as something already carried out: “it was fitting.”

In Greek religious and philosophical discussion, writers often debated what was “fitting” for God—what was worthy of the divine nature. Usually the conclusion was that suffering, change, and humiliation were not fitting for God. Hebrews turns that assumption upside down. It was precisely fitting for God to bring his people to glory by way of the sufferings of his Son. The author is not saying that God was forced into this path by some necessity outside himself. He is saying that this path perfectly expresses who God is: his holiness that cannot overlook sin, his love that will not abandon sinners, his wisdom that brings glory out of suffering, and his faithfulness to his purpose for humanity.

The same verb reappears at a key point in the priestly argument: “For it was fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and exalted above the heavens” (7:26). The two verses frame the portrait of Christ the priest. It was fitting that he should suffer like his brothers (2:10), and fitting that he should be unlike them in his holiness (7:26). Thomas Aquinas, centuries later, devoted careful discussion to the “fittingness” of Christ’s passion, arguing from Scripture that no other way of salvation so fully displayed both God’s justice and his mercy.

The “him” for whom it was fitting is God the Father. He is described as the one “for whom are all things and through whom are all things.” The two prepositional phrases use the same preposition with different cases. δι’ ὅν di’ hon, with the accusative, means “because of whom, for whose sake”—God is the goal and purpose of everything. δι’ οὗ di’ hou, with the genitive, means “through whom, by whose agency”—God is the one who brings everything into being. Paul speaks similarly: “from him and through him and to him are all things” (Rom 11:36). In 1:2 the author said that God made the ages “through” the Son; now he says that all things are “through” God himself. There is no contradiction. The Father creates through the Son, and the Son is the agent of the Father’s will (compare 1 Cor 8:6). The reference to God as Creator and goal of all things is deliberate. The God who made the universe and directs it toward its end is not caught off guard by suffering. The cross was not an emergency measure but a path that fit his eternal purpose.

God’s purpose is stated in the phrase “in bringing many sons to glory” (πολλοὺς υἱοὺς εἰς δόξαν ἀγαγόντα pollous huious eis doxan agagonta). This is the goal of salvation. The “glory” is the “glory and honor” of Psalm 8, which Jesus already possesses (2:9) and which God intends to share with his people. Paul speaks of the same hope: “if children, then heirs—heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him so that we may also be glorified with him” (Rom 8:17). The word “sons” links the believers to the Son. In chapter 1 the Son is unique, superior even to the angels; but now we learn that God is bringing “many sons” to share the glory of the one Son. In the ancient world, “sons” were heirs; daughters as well as sons are included in this privileged status (2 Cor 6:18). The word “many” may echo the Servant of the Lord, who would “make many righteous” and “bore the sin of many” (Isa 53:11–12; compare Heb 9:28; Mark 10:45).

The verb ἄγω agō, “to lead, to bring,” suggests a journey. Hebrews will soon compare the church to Israel in the wilderness, led out of Egypt toward the land of rest (3:7–4:11). God once brought his “son,” Israel, out of Egypt (Exod 4:22, בְּנִי בְכֹרִי יִשְׂרָאֵל beni vekhori Yisra’el, “my son, my firstborn, Israel”). Now he is bringing many sons through the wilderness of this age to the glory of the world to come.

Interpretive Options: Who Is “Bringing” the Many Sons?

  • God brings the sons. The participle ἀγαγόντα agagonta, “bringing,” is accusative. In Greek the subject of an infinitive is in the accusative case, and the subject of the infinitive “to make perfect” is God. The participle therefore most naturally describes God: “it was fitting for him, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the pioneer perfect.” The grammatical difficulty is that the opening words refer to God in the dative (“for him”), and the participle has shifted to the accusative. But such shifts are common in Greek when an infinitive follows.
  • The pioneer brings the sons. The participle could agree with “the pioneer” (τὸν ἀρχηγόν ton archēgon), which is also accusative: “to make perfect through sufferings the pioneer who brings many sons to glory.” This makes good sense, since a pioneer leads others.

Most commentators favor the first view, since the whole sentence is about God’s fitting action, and the word order places the participle closer to the description of God. The theological difference is small. God brings the many sons to glory, and he does it through the pioneer, who goes ahead of them and leads them. The aorist tense of the participle does not mean the bringing is finished; it presents God’s whole saving work as a single act, viewed in its entirety from the standpoint of his purpose.

Word Study: ἀρχηγός archēgos, “pioneer”

The noun ἀρχηγός archēgos combines the ideas of archē, “beginning, first place,” and agō, “to lead.” In ordinary Greek it could mean a leader or chief; a founder of a city, a family, or a school of thought; or an originator, the one who begins something. It is used only four times in the New Testament, always of Jesus: “the archēgos of life,” whom the people killed and God raised (Acts 3:15); God exalted him as “archēgos and Savior” (Acts 5:31); “the archēgos of their salvation” (Heb 2:10); and “the archēgos and perfecter of faith” (Heb 12:2).

In the Greek Old Testament, the word frequently describes the heads of Israel’s tribes and clans. The leaders sent to spy out the land of Canaan were called by this title (Num 13:2–3). Strikingly, when Israel refused to enter the land, they said, “Let us appoint a head and return to Egypt” (Num 14:4; Hebrew נִתְּנָה רֹאשׁ וְנָשׁוּבָה מִצְרָיְמָה nittenah rosh venashuvah mitsraymah), and the Greek translators used archēgos for that “head.” The readers of Hebrews, who were being tempted to turn back, might well have heard the contrast. Israel in its unbelief wanted a leader to take it back to slavery. God has given his people a pioneer who leads them forward to glory.

This study renders the word “pioneer,” because it captures both of its main ideas: the one who goes first, opening a way where none existed, and the one who leads others along that way to its goal. Other translations use “author,” “founder,” “leader,” or “champion.” Lane, among others, has suggested “champion,” drawing on Greek traditions of heroes who fought on behalf of their people; this fits well with the victory over the devil in 2:14. All these ideas belong together. Jesus is the founder of salvation, the one whose death and resurrection created it; he is the forerunner who has gone ahead into the presence of God (6:20, πρόδρομος prodromos); and he is the leader who brings his people along the same path he walked. Hebrews 5:9 adds that, having been made perfect, he became “the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him.”

God made this pioneer “perfect through sufferings” (διὰ παθημάτων τελειῶσαι dia pathēmatōn teleiōsai). This is the first appearance of a word that is central to Hebrews: τελειόω teleioō, “to make perfect, to bring to completion, to bring to the goal.” The author uses it of Christ three times (2:10; 5:9; 7:28) and of believers several times (10:14; 11:40; 12:23).

What can it mean that Jesus was “made perfect”? It cannot mean that he was once morally imperfect or sinful and needed to be improved. Hebrews insists that he was “without sin” (4:15), “holy, innocent, undefiled” (7:26), and that he offered himself “without blemish” to God (9:14). Nor does it mean that the eternal Son was lacking anything in his divine nature (1:3). The word refers to bringing something to its intended goal. Jesus was “made perfect” in the sense that he was fully qualified and equipped for his work as Savior and high priest. He completed the course God set for him. He experienced obedience under suffering to the very end (5:8, “although he was a Son, he learned obedience from what he suffered”). He passed through death and was raised to glory. Only then did he stand complete as the pioneer and priest of his people. Jesus himself spoke in this way: “on the third day I reach my goal” (literally “I am perfected,” τελειοῦμαι teleioumai, Luke 13:32).

Old Testament Background: “Filling the Hands” of the Priest

The Greek Old Testament uses teleioō in a technical priestly sense. When Moses ordained Aaron and his sons as priests, the Hebrew says: וּמִלֵּאתָ יַד־אַהֲרֹן וְיַד־בָּנָיו umille’ta yad-’Aharon veyad-banav, “and you shall fill the hand of Aaron and the hand of his sons” (Exod 29:9). To “fill the hand” was the Hebrew idiom for consecrating a priest, installing him in office (perhaps from placing portions of the offerings in his hands, Exod 29:24). The Greek translators regularly rendered this idiom with teleioō, “to complete” the hands, that is, to consecrate fully (compare Lev 8:33; 16:32; 21:10). The ordination ceremony lasted seven days (Exod 29:35).

The author of Hebrews certainly knew this usage, and it fits his argument well. Already in 2:10 he is preparing to present Jesus as high priest (2:17). Jesus’ “perfection” through sufferings is, among other things, his consecration to his priestly office. Aaron was consecrated by ceremonies of washing, anointing, and sacrifice. Jesus was consecrated by obedient suffering and death. When the author later says that the law appoints men in their weakness as high priests, “but the word of the oath … appoints a Son who has been made perfect forever” (7:28), he has this priestly meaning clearly in view.

The plural “sufferings” covers the whole of Jesus’ human experience of hardship, temptation, opposition, and agony, culminating in death (5:7–8; 12:2–3). This is the path God chose for the pioneer, and it is the path the many sons will also walk. The readers were suffering for their faith. They needed to understand that suffering was not a sign of God’s rejection. It was the very road by which God had brought his own Son to glory, and the road by which he leads all his sons (12:5–11). The pioneer walked it first. Those who follow him do not walk alone.

2:11

For the one who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all from one. That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers,

Verse 11 explains (“for,” γάρ gar) why it was fitting for the pioneer to share the sufferings of those he leads. He and they belong together. The Greek construction ὅ τε … καὶ οἱ ho te … kai hoi, “both the one who … and those who …,” binds the two parties tightly as a single group.

The pioneer is now described as “the one who sanctifies” (ὁ ἁγιάζων ho hagiazōn), and his people as “those who are being sanctified” (οἱ ἁγιαζόμενοι hoi hagiazomenoi). The verb ἁγιάζω hagiazō, “to sanctify, to make holy, to consecrate,” is another key term in Hebrews. It belongs to the world of worship. In the Old Testament, to sanctify something was to set it apart for God and make it fit for his presence, usually by cleansing it from impurity. Hebrews uses the word in exactly this sense: “by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10); “by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14); “Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood” (13:12). Sanctification in Hebrews is first of all the cleansing and consecration that Christ’s sacrifice accomplishes, making sinners fit to approach God. It certainly includes a holy life (12:14), but its foundation is what Christ has done.

The present participles in this verse describe the parties by their characteristic activity and condition. Christ is “the sanctifier”; his people are “the sanctified ones.” The present tense of “those being sanctified” need not imply that the process is incomplete in every respect (compare 10:10, where they “have been sanctified”), but it does keep in view the ongoing application of Christ’s work to his people as they draw near to God.

Old Testament Background: “I Am the Lord Who Sanctifies You”

In the Holiness Code of Leviticus, God repeatedly identifies himself as the one who makes his people holy: וְנִקְדַּשְׁתִּי בְּתוֹךְ בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אֲנִי יְהוָה מְקַדִּשְׁכֶם — veniqdashti betokh benei Yisra’el, ani YHWH meqaddishkem, “and I will be sanctified in the midst of the sons of Israel; I am the Lord who sanctifies you” (Lev 22:32; compare Exod 31:13; Lev 20:8; 21:15). The Hebrew participle מְקַדֵּשׁ meqaddesh, “one who sanctifies,” corresponds closely to the Greek participle ho hagiazōn in Hebrews 2:11.

It is significant that Hebrews gives this role to Jesus. In the Old Testament it is the Lord himself who sanctifies his people. In Hebrews it is Jesus who sanctifies. The verse emphasizes his solidarity with his people, yet it also quietly places him on the divine side of the relationship. He is both the sanctifier who stands with God and the brother who stands with us.

The sanctifier and the sanctified “are all from one” (ἐξ ἑνὸς πάντες ex henos pantes). The phrase is compact and somewhat ambiguous. The Greek word for “one” (henos) could be masculine (“one person”) or neuter (“one source, one stock”).

Interpretive Options: “From One”

  • From one Father, God. Both Jesus and his people are sons of the one God and Father. This fits the immediate context, which speaks of God bringing “many sons” to glory (2:10) and of “the children God has given me” (2:13). Many interpreters, from Westcott onward, take this view.
  • From one ancestor, Adam. Jesus and his people share a common human origin. This fits the emphasis on shared flesh and blood (2:14) and the echoes of Psalm 8 and Genesis 1. Luke traces Jesus’ genealogy back to “Adam, the son of God” (Luke 3:38).
  • From one ancestor, Abraham. This view looks ahead to “the seed of Abraham” (2:16).
  • From one source or family (neuter). They share a common nature or stock, without specifying the ancestor.

The first view is the most likely. The whole passage centers on God’s purpose to bring sons to glory, and in the Old Testament quotations that follow, the speaker addresses God as the one whose name he declares (2:12), in whom he trusts (2:13a), and who has given him children (2:13b). The relationship of sonship to the one Father is what binds the Son and the sons together. Yet the other views capture something true: the Son’s solidarity with his brothers involves a shared human nature (2:14, 17), and that is how a common sonship becomes possible. The eternal Son became a son of Adam so that sons of Adam might become sons of God. Of course, Jesus’ sonship and theirs are not identical. He is the Son by nature, the radiance of God’s glory (1:3); they are sons by grace, brought into God’s family through him. Hebrews never blurs that difference, but it rejoices that the Son shares his family name with those he redeems.

“That is why” (δι’ ἣν αἰτίαν di’ hēn aitian, literally “for which reason”) he is not ashamed to call them brothers. The verb ἐπαισχύνεται epaischynetai, “he is ashamed,” is a strong word in a culture where honor and shame governed social life. People of high status were expected to keep their distance from those of lower rank; to acknowledge humble or disgraced people as kin was to risk one’s own honor. The readers knew this well. They had been publicly shamed for their faith (10:33), and some may have been tempted to distance themselves from fellow believers in prison (13:3).

Against that background the statement is astonishing. The Son, who is heir of all things and seated at the right hand of the Majesty on high, “is not ashamed” to call these weak, suffering, sometimes wavering people his brothers. The phrase is an understatement: he is not merely willing but glad to acknowledge them. The same verb appears again in 11:16: God “is not ashamed to be called their God,” speaking of the patriarchs who lived as strangers and exiles. Jesus warned that whoever is ashamed of him and his words, of that person the Son of Man will be ashamed (Mark 8:38). Here the positive side is stated: the Son is not ashamed of those who belong to him. After his resurrection, the first message the risen Jesus sent to his disciples—who had deserted him—was “Go to my brothers” (John 20:17; compare Matt 28:10).

The present tense “he is not ashamed” describes the exalted Christ’s present attitude. He does not merely call them brothers from the past events of his earthly life; he does so now, from the throne. The readers’ shame before the world was real, but they were honored by the highest person in the universe. Later the author will urge them to “go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach” (13:13). If he is not ashamed of them, they need not be ashamed of him.

2:12

saying, “I will proclaim your name to my brothers; in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise.”

The author now proves from Scripture that the Son calls his people “brothers.” He introduces the quotation with the present participle λέγων legōn, “saying.” The speaker is the Son himself. In chapter 1 the Father addressed the Son in the words of the Psalms; now the Son speaks, addressing the Father in the words of Psalm 22. The author hears the psalm as the voice of the Messiah. He will do the same in 10:5–7, where he places Psalm 40 on the lips of Christ “when he comes into the world.” For Hebrews, the Old Testament is not merely a collection of predictions about Christ but a place where the Son’s own voice can be heard.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 22:22 (Hebrew 22:23)

Hebrew: אֲסַפְּרָה שִׁמְךָ לְאֶחָי בְּתוֹךְ קָהָל אֲהַלְלֶךָּ — ’asapperah shimka le’echai, betokh qahal ’ahalleka, “I will recount your name to my brothers; in the midst of the assembly I will praise you.”

The Hebrew קָהָל qahal, “assembly, congregation,” is the regular word for Israel gathered before God, as at Sinai (Deut 9:10; 18:16, “the day of the assembly”). The Greek translators commonly rendered it with ἐκκλησία ekklēsia, the word the New Testament uses for “the church.” The verb אֲהַלְלֶךָּ ’ahalleka, “I will praise you,” comes from the same root as “Hallelujah.”

Hebrews follows the Greek Psalter closely, with one difference. Where the Greek Psalter has “I will recount” (a literal rendering of the Hebrew סִפֵּר sipper, “to tell, to recount”), Hebrews has ἀπαγγελῶ apangelō, “I will proclaim, I will announce.” The change may simply reflect the author’s memory or a different form of the Greek text, but it fits his purpose. Apangellō is a word for proclaiming a message (compare 1 John 1:2–3, “we proclaim to you the eternal life”). The Son does not only recount God’s deeds; he announces God’s name to his brothers as the herald of salvation.

The choice of Psalm 22 is profound. It is the psalm of the righteous sufferer. It begins with the cry Jesus uttered on the cross: אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי ’eli ’eli lamah ‘azavtani, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Ps 22:1, Hebrew 22:2; Mark 15:34). The Gospel writers saw in its descriptions of mockery, thirst, and divided garments a portrait of the crucifixion (Ps 22:7–8, 15–18; Matt 27:35, 39–43; John 19:23–24). But the psalm does not end in anguish. Suddenly, at verse 21 (Hebrew 22:22), the tone changes: “Save me from the mouth of the lion; from the horns of the wild oxen—עֲנִיתָנִי ‘anitani, you have answered me!” From that point the sufferer, now rescued, praises God in the assembly, and the psalm widens until “all the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the Lord, and all the families of the nations shall worship before you” (Ps 22:27; Hebrew 22:28).

The verse Hebrews quotes stands precisely at this turning point, the first words of the rescued sufferer. In the light of the gospel, this is the voice of the risen Christ. He who cried “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” now stands in the midst of his brothers and proclaims the name of the God who answered him by raising him from the dead. The quotation therefore fits the movement of 2:9–10 perfectly: through suffering to glory. The pioneer, having been perfected through sufferings, now gathers those he has led and leads them in praise.

Three features of the quotation support the author’s argument. First, the Son calls them “my brothers” (τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς μου tois adelphois mou). This is the word that proves verse 11: he is not ashamed to call them brothers. Second, he “proclaims your name”—that is, he makes known who God is. In biblical thought, God’s “name” is his revealed character. Jesus said in his prayer to the Father: “I have made your name known to those whom you gave me out of the world” (John 17:6, 26). The Son who is the final word of God (1:2) reveals the Father to his brothers. Third, he does this “in the midst of the assembly” (ἐν μέσῳ ἐκκλησίας en mesō ekklēsias). He does not stand above or apart from the congregation but among them, as one of them.

The final verb, ὑμνήσω hymnēsō, “I will sing hymns to you, I will sing your praise,” pictures Jesus as the leader of his people’s worship. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus sang a hymn with his disciples (Mark 14:26, using the same verb). Now the exalted Christ leads the praise of the redeemed assembly. Hebrews will later describe that assembly: “you have come to Mount Zion … to myriads of angels in festal gathering, and to the assembly (ἐκκλησίᾳ ekklēsia) of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven” (12:22–23). It will also urge the readers: “Through him, then, let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God” (13:15). Christian worship is not merely our praise offered to God; it is joining the praise of the Son, who stands in the midst of his brothers and leads them before the Father.

For a small, harassed congregation, perhaps meeting in a house in Rome, this was a powerful encouragement. When they gathered to worship, however few and despised they were, the risen Lord stood in their midst, calling them his brothers and leading their song.

2:13

And again, “I myself will put my trust in him.” And again, “Here am I, and the children God has given me.”

Two further quotations, each introduced by “and again” (καὶ πάλιν kai palin), the same formula used to string together the quotations of chapter 1 (1:5, 6). Both come from Isaiah 8:17–18, a passage that the early church read as pointing to Christ. Like Psalm 22, they are heard on the lips of the Son.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 8:17–18

Hebrew: וְחִכִּיתִי לַיהוָה הַמַּסְתִּיר פָּנָיו מִבֵּית יַעֲקֹב וְקִוֵּיתִי־לוֹ — vechikkiti laYHWH hammastir panav mibbeit Ya‘aqov veqivveiti-lo, “And I will wait for the Lord, who is hiding his face from the house of Jacob, and I will hope in him” (Isa 8:17).

הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי וְהַיְלָדִים אֲשֶׁר נָתַן־לִי יְהוָה לְאֹתוֹת וּלְמוֹפְתִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל מֵעִם יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת הַשֹּׁכֵן בְּהַר צִיּוֹן — hinneh ’anokhi vehayeladim ’asher natan-li YHWH le’otot ulemophetim beYisra’el me‘im YHWH tseva’ot hashshokhen behar Tsiyyon, “Here am I and the children whom the Lord has given me, for signs and for wonders in Israel from the Lord of hosts, who dwells on Mount Zion” (Isa 8:18).

In the Hebrew, Isaiah says he will “hope” in the Lord (קִוָּה qivvah, “to wait expectantly, to hope”). The Greek translation renders this with a phrase meaning “I will trust in him,” which Hebrews follows, adding an emphatic “I”: Ἐγὼ ἔσομαι πεποιθὼς ἐπ’ αὐτῷ Egō esomai pepoithōs ep’ autō. Nearly the same Greek wording appears in the Greek versions of David’s song of deliverance (2 Sam 22:3, where the Hebrew says אֶחֱסֶה־בּוֹ ’echeseh-bo, “I take refuge in him”) and of Isaiah 12:2 (Hebrew אֶבְטַח ’evtach, “I will trust”). But since the next quotation comes from Isaiah 8:18, the author almost certainly has Isaiah 8:17 in mind. Hope, refuge, and trust are closely related in the Old Testament: all describe a person who rests his whole weight on God and waits for him to act. Isaiah 8:18 is quoted exactly as in the Greek translation, with “God” for the divine name.

Historical Background: Isaiah and His Children

Isaiah 8 belongs to the crisis of about 734 BC, when the kings of Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel threatened Jerusalem and King Ahaz panicked (Isa 7:1–2). Isaiah urged the king to trust the Lord, but Ahaz turned instead to Assyria for help. God then told Isaiah not to walk in the way of “this people,” and not to fear what they feared: אֶת־יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת אֹתוֹ תַקְדִּישׁוּ וְהוּא מוֹרַאֲכֶם ’et-YHWH tseva’ot ’oto taqdishu vehu’ mora’akhem, “The Lord of hosts—him you shall regard as holy; let him be your fear” (Isa 8:13). The Lord would be a sanctuary for those who trusted him, but “a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense” to both houses of Israel (8:14). Isaiah was to “bind up the testimony” and “seal the instruction” among his disciples (8:16), and wait.

In this setting Isaiah and his children became living signs. Their names were messages: Isaiah’s own name means “The Lord is salvation”; his sons were named Shear-jashub, “A remnant will return” (7:3), and Maher-shalal-hash-baz, “Swift is the spoil, speedy the plunder” (8:1–4). Together, the prophet and his children formed a small, faithful remnant within an unbelieving nation, trusting God while he “hid his face” from Israel and waiting for him to act.

The New Testament writers drew repeatedly on this chapter. The “stone of stumbling” (8:14) is applied to Christ (Rom 9:33; 1 Pet 2:8), and the call not to fear what the people fear but to regard the Lord as holy is applied to believers facing persecution (1 Pet 3:14–15). The following chapter speaks of the child to be born, the “Mighty God” on David’s throne (Isa 9:6–7). Hebrews follows the same line, hearing in Isaiah’s words the voice of the one who is the true faithful remnant and who stands with the children God has given him.

“I myself will put my trust in him.” The first quotation shows the Son as a man of faith. The construction ἔσομαι πεποιθώς esomai pepoithōs combines the future of “to be” with the perfect participle of πείθω peithō, “to persuade”; in the perfect it means “to be confident, to trust.” Literally: “I will be in a state of settled trust in him.” It describes not a momentary act but an abiding posture. The emphatic “I” (Ἐγώ Egō) underlines the personal commitment.

Why does this quotation prove that Jesus shares the condition of his brothers? Because trusting God is what creatures do. The Son, having become man, lived as a man in dependence on his Father. He prayed “with loud cries and tears to the one who was able to save him from death” (5:7). He entrusted himself to God when he suffered (1 Pet 2:23). At the cross his enemies mocked him with words drawn from Psalm 22 itself: “He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him” (Matt 27:43; compare Ps 22:8). The mockers spoke more truly than they knew. Jesus did trust in God, even when God seemed to hide his face, as Isaiah’s words describe. That is why Hebrews will call him “the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2): he is the supreme example of the faith described in chapter 11, the one who trusted God through suffering to glory. In this he is truly one with his brothers. He walked the road of faith before them, and he walks it with them.

“Here am I, and the children God has given me.” The second quotation pictures the Son presenting himself before God together with his family. “Here am I” (Ἰδοὺ ἐγώ Idou egō; Hebrew הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי hinneh ’anokhi) is the language of a servant standing ready before his master. Abraham, Moses, and Samuel answered God with similar words (Gen 22:1; Exod 3:4; 1 Sam 3:4). The Son stands before the Father, and he does not stand alone. With him are “the children” (τὰ παιδία ta paidia), whom “God has given” him.

The word “children” here does not mean that believers are the Son’s offspring as if he were their father. In Isaiah the children were the prophet’s own sons, who shared his calling as signs to Israel. In Hebrews the “children” are the members of God’s family whom the Father has entrusted to the Son—the same people the author has called “sons” (2:10) and “brothers” (2:11–12). The emphasis falls on the giving. The Son’s people are the Father’s gift to him. Jesus spoke this way: “All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out” (John 6:37); “I have made your name known to the men whom you gave me out of the world. They were yours, and you gave them to me” (John 17:6). Because they are the Father’s gift, the Son will not lose them (John 6:39; 10:28–29). This is deeply reassuring for weak and wavering believers. Their security rests not finally on the strength of their grip on Christ but on the Father’s gift and the Son’s faithful keeping.

The whole of Isaiah 8:18 resonates with Hebrews 2. Isaiah and his children were “for signs and for wonders in Israel”—the very phrase used in 2:4 for God’s testimony to the gospel. They stood as a faithful remnant while the nation stumbled. Hebrews’ readers, a small group of believers set apart from the majority of their people and from the surrounding society, could recognize themselves here. Like Isaiah’s children, they were a sign. Like Isaiah, their leader trusted God when God’s purposes were hidden. And the Lord of hosts “who dwells on Mount Zion” is the God to whose city they had come (12:22).

Together the three quotations of 2:12–13 present the Son in three relationships. He is the brother who proclaims God’s name in the assembly (Ps 22:22). He is the believer who trusts God in a dark time (Isa 8:17). He is the head of the family who presents God’s children before him (Isa 8:18). In each, he stands with his people, not apart from them. This prepares directly for the next verse, which picks up the word “children” and explains what it cost the Son to stand with them.
