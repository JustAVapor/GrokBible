2:10

For it was fitting for him, for whom are all things and through whom are all things, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings.

Verse 9 ended with the death of Jesus. To many in the ancient world, Jew and Gentile alike, that was the great stumbling block of the Christian message (1 Cor 1:23). A crucified Messiah seemed a contradiction in terms, and a suffering Son of God seemed unworthy of God. The readers, under pressure and shame, may have felt the force of such objections themselves. Verse 10 answers them head-on. The “for” (γάρ gar) introduces an explanation of why Jesus’ suffering and death were not an embarrassment but exactly what was right.

Word Study: Ἔπρεπεν Eprepen, “it was fitting”

The verb πρέπω prepō means “to be fitting, suitable, appropriate, becoming.” It describes what corresponds to someone’s character or position. Paul uses it of conduct that “befits” saints (Eph 5:3) and that “befits” sound teaching (Titus 2:1). The imperfect tense here looks at the whole of God’s saving plan as something already carried out: “it was fitting.”

In Greek religious and philosophical discussion, writers often debated what was “fitting” for God—what was worthy of the divine nature. Usually the conclusion was that suffering, change, and humiliation were not fitting for God. Hebrews turns that assumption upside down. It was precisely fitting for God to bring his people to glory by way of the sufferings of his Son. The author is not saying that God was forced into this path by some necessity outside himself. He is saying that this path perfectly expresses who God is: his holiness that cannot overlook sin, his love that will not abandon sinners, his wisdom that brings glory out of suffering, and his faithfulness to his purpose for humanity.

The same verb reappears at a key point in the priestly argument: “For it was fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and exalted above the heavens” (7:26). The two verses frame the portrait of Christ the priest. It was fitting that he should suffer like his brothers (2:10), and fitting that he should be unlike them in his holiness (7:26). Thomas Aquinas, centuries later, devoted careful discussion to the “fittingness” of Christ’s passion, arguing from Scripture that no other way of salvation so fully displayed both God’s justice and his mercy.

The “him” for whom it was fitting is God the Father. He is described as the one “for whom are all things and through whom are all things.” The two prepositional phrases use the same preposition with different cases. δι’ ὅν di’ hon, with the accusative, means “because of whom, for whose sake”—God is the goal and purpose of everything. δι’ οὗ di’ hou, with the genitive, means “through whom, by whose agency”—God is the one who brings everything into being. Paul speaks similarly: “from him and through him and to him are all things” (Rom 11:36). In 1:2 the author said that God made the ages “through” the Son; now he says that all things are “through” God himself. There is no contradiction. The Father creates through the Son, and the Son is the agent of the Father’s will (compare 1 Cor 8:6). The reference to God as Creator and goal of all things is deliberate. The God who made the universe and directs it toward its end is not caught off guard by suffering. The cross was not an emergency measure but a path that fit his eternal purpose.

God’s purpose is stated in the phrase “in bringing many sons to glory” (πολλοὺς υἱοὺς εἰς δόξαν ἀγαγόντα pollous huious eis doxan agagonta). This is the goal of salvation. The “glory” is the “glory and honor” of Psalm 8, which Jesus already possesses (2:9) and which God intends to share with his people. Paul speaks of the same hope: “if children, then heirs—heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him so that we may also be glorified with him” (Rom 8:17). The word “sons” links the believers to the Son. In chapter 1 the Son is unique, superior even to the angels; but now we learn that God is bringing “many sons” to share the glory of the one Son. In the ancient world, “sons” were heirs; daughters as well as sons are included in this privileged status (2 Cor 6:18). The word “many” may echo the Servant of the Lord, who would “make many righteous” and “bore the sin of many” (Isa 53:11–12; compare Heb 9:28; Mark 10:45).

The verb ἄγω agō, “to lead, to bring,” suggests a journey. Hebrews will soon compare the church to Israel in the wilderness, led out of Egypt toward the land of rest (3:7–4:11). God once brought his “son,” Israel, out of Egypt (Exod 4:22, בְּנִי בְכֹרִי יִשְׂרָאֵל beni vekhori Yisra’el, “my son, my firstborn, Israel”). Now he is bringing many sons through the wilderness of this age to the glory of the world to come.

Interpretive Options: Who Is “Bringing” the Many Sons?

  • God brings the sons. The participle ἀγαγόντα agagonta, “bringing,” is accusative. In Greek the subject of an infinitive is in the accusative case, and the subject of the infinitive “to make perfect” is God. The participle therefore most naturally describes God: “it was fitting for him, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the pioneer perfect.” The grammatical difficulty is that the opening words refer to God in the dative (“for him”), and the participle has shifted to the accusative. But such shifts are common in Greek when an infinitive follows.
  • The pioneer brings the sons. The participle could agree with “the pioneer” (τὸν ἀρχηγόν ton archēgon), which is also accusative: “to make perfect through sufferings the pioneer who brings many sons to glory.” This makes good sense, since a pioneer leads others.

Most commentators favor the first view, since the whole sentence is about God’s fitting action, and the word order places the participle closer to the description of God. The theological difference is small. God brings the many sons to glory, and he does it through the pioneer, who goes ahead of them and leads them. The aorist tense of the participle does not mean the bringing is finished; it presents God’s whole saving work as a single act, viewed in its entirety from the standpoint of his purpose.

Word Study: ἀρχηγός archēgos, “pioneer”

The noun ἀρχηγός archēgos combines the ideas of archē, “beginning, first place,” and agō, “to lead.” In ordinary Greek it could mean a leader or chief; a founder of a city, a family, or a school of thought; or an originator, the one who begins something. It is used only four times in the New Testament, always of Jesus: “the archēgos of life,” whom the people killed and God raised (Acts 3:15); God exalted him as “archēgos and Savior” (Acts 5:31); “the archēgos of their salvation” (Heb 2:10); and “the archēgos and perfecter of faith” (Heb 12:2).

In the Greek Old Testament, the word frequently describes the heads of Israel’s tribes and clans. The leaders sent to spy out the land of Canaan were called by this title (Num 13:2–3). Strikingly, when Israel refused to enter the land, they said, “Let us appoint a head and return to Egypt” (Num 14:4; Hebrew נִתְּנָה רֹאשׁ וְנָשׁוּבָה מִצְרָיְמָה nittenah rosh venashuvah mitsraymah), and the Greek translators used archēgos for that “head.” The readers of Hebrews, who were being tempted to turn back, might well have heard the contrast. Israel in its unbelief wanted a leader to take it back to slavery. God has given his people a pioneer who leads them forward to glory.

This study renders the word “pioneer,” because it captures both of its main ideas: the one who goes first, opening a way where none existed, and the one who leads others along that way to its goal. Other translations use “author,” “founder,” “leader,” or “champion.” Lane, among others, has suggested “champion,” drawing on Greek traditions of heroes who fought on behalf of their people; this fits well with the victory over the devil in 2:14. All these ideas belong together. Jesus is the founder of salvation, the one whose death and resurrection created it; he is the forerunner who has gone ahead into the presence of God (6:20, πρόδρομος prodromos); and he is the leader who brings his people along the same path he walked. Hebrews 5:9 adds that, having been made perfect, he became “the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him.”

God made this pioneer “perfect through sufferings” (διὰ παθημάτων τελειῶσαι dia pathēmatōn teleiōsai). This is the first appearance of a word that is central to Hebrews: τελειόω teleioō, “to make perfect, to bring to completion, to bring to the goal.” The author uses it of Christ three times (2:10; 5:9; 7:28) and of believers several times (10:14; 11:40; 12:23).

What can it mean that Jesus was “made perfect”? It cannot mean that he was once morally imperfect or sinful and needed to be improved. Hebrews insists that he was “without sin” (4:15), “holy, innocent, undefiled” (7:26), and that he offered himself “without blemish” to God (9:14). Nor does it mean that the eternal Son was lacking anything in his divine nature (1:3). The word refers to bringing something to its intended goal. Jesus was “made perfect” in the sense that he was fully qualified and equipped for his work as Savior and high priest. He completed the course God set for him. He experienced obedience under suffering to the very end (5:8, “although he was a Son, he learned obedience from what he suffered”). He passed through death and was raised to glory. Only then did he stand complete as the pioneer and priest of his people. Jesus himself spoke in this way: “on the third day I reach my goal” (literally “I am perfected,” τελειοῦμαι teleioumai, Luke 13:32).

Old Testament Background: “Filling the Hands” of the Priest

The Greek Old Testament uses teleioō in a technical priestly sense. When Moses ordained Aaron and his sons as priests, the Hebrew says: וּמִלֵּאתָ יַד־אַהֲרֹן וְיַד־בָּנָיו umille’ta yad-’Aharon veyad-banav, “and you shall fill the hand of Aaron and the hand of his sons” (Exod 29:9). To “fill the hand” was the Hebrew idiom for consecrating a priest, installing him in office (perhaps from placing portions of the offerings in his hands, Exod 29:24). The Greek translators regularly rendered this idiom with teleioō, “to complete” the hands, that is, to consecrate fully (compare Lev 8:33; 16:32; 21:10). The ordination ceremony lasted seven days (Exod 29:35).

The author of Hebrews certainly knew this usage, and it fits his argument well. Already in 2:10 he is preparing to present Jesus as high priest (2:17). Jesus’ “perfection” through sufferings is, among other things, his consecration to his priestly office. Aaron was consecrated by ceremonies of washing, anointing, and sacrifice. Jesus was consecrated by obedient suffering and death. When the author later says that the law appoints men in their weakness as high priests, “but the word of the oath … appoints a Son who has been made perfect forever” (7:28), he has this priestly meaning clearly in view.

The plural “sufferings” covers the whole of Jesus’ human experience of hardship, temptation, opposition, and agony, culminating in death (5:7–8; 12:2–3). This is the path God chose for the pioneer, and it is the path the many sons will also walk. The readers were suffering for their faith. They needed to understand that suffering was not a sign of God’s rejection. It was the very road by which God had brought his own Son to glory, and the road by which he leads all his sons (12:5–11). The pioneer walked it first. Those who follow him do not walk alone.
