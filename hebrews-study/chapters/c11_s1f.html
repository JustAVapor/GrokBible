Something Better (11:39–40)

11:39

And all of them, though commended through their faith, did not receive the promise,

The great roll call ends not with a final hero but with a summary verdict that embraces everyone named and unnamed since Abel: καὶ πάντες kai pantes, “and all.” Abel and Enoch, Noah and Abraham, Sarah and the patriarchs, Moses and the Israelites at the sea, Rahab, the judges and kings, the prophets, the martyrs, the homeless wanderers in the caves: all of them are gathered into one sentence, and the sentence says two things about them. They were commended, and they did not receive.

Textual Note

The SBL Greek New Testament, followed in this study, reads καὶ πάντες kai pantes, “and all,” with a small number of early witnesses. Most manuscripts, and the Nestle-Aland text, read καὶ οὗτοι πάντες kai houtoi pantes, “and all these.” The meaning is the same either way: “all” refers to the whole company of the faithful just described.

“Though commended through their faith.” The participle μαρτυρηθέντες martyrēthentes, aorist passive of μαρτυρέω martyreō, “bear witness, testify,” means “having been attested, having received a good testimony.” It is best taken as concessive (“although they were commended”), since it stands in tension with the main verb that follows. The passive voice implies the agent: God bore witness to them, and he did so in the Scriptures that record their faith. This word forms a frame around the whole chapter. At the beginning the author said, “For by it [faith] the people of old received their commendation” (ἐμαρτυρήθησαν emartyrēthēsan, 11:2). Abel “received testimony that he was righteous, God testifying about his gifts” (11:4), and Enoch “was commended as having pleased God” (11:5). Now, at the end, the same verb sums up everyone. The chapter opens and closes with God’s approval of faith. Ancient writers called this kind of frame an inclusio; it tells the hearer that a unit of thought is complete.

The phrase διὰ τῆς πίστεως dia tēs pisteōs, “through the faith” (the article is best understood as “their faith,” the faith just described), makes clear that the commendation came not through their achievements, their moral record, or their descent, but through their faith. We have seen how flawed some of them were (11:32). What God attested in each case was their trust in him and in his promise.

The same word family will appear immediately in 12:1: “since we have so great a cloud of witnesses (μαρτύρων martyrōn) surrounding us.” Those who received God’s testimony now become witnesses themselves. They are not spectators in the stands watching the readers run, so much as people whose lives testify to what faith is and what it can endure. They have been “witnessed to,” and so they bear witness.

“Did not receive the promise.” Here is the surprising turn. οὐκ ἐκομίσαντο τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν ouk ekomisanto tēn epangelian. The verb κομίζω komizō, in the middle voice, means “to carry off for oneself, obtain, receive (what is due or promised).” It appeared in 10:36, in the exhortation that sets up this whole chapter: “you have need of endurance, so that when you have done the will of God you may receive (κομίσησθε komisēsthe) the promise.” It appeared also in 11:19, where Abraham “received back” Isaac. The readers are told that they must endure to receive the promise; now they learn that none of the heroes of faith received it in their lifetime.

As noted at 11:33, the singular with the article, τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν tēn epangelian, “the promise,” is significant. Many of these believers obtained particular promises (11:33): a son for Abraham (6:15), victory for Gideon, a kingdom for David, the restoration of a child for the widow. But none received the ultimate promise to which all of these pointed. What is that promise? Hebrews has described it in several ways: the promise of entering God’s rest (4:1), “the promised eternal inheritance” (9:15), the heavenly homeland and “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (11:10, 14–16), the “better resurrection” (11:35), and “the world to come” (2:5). It is the full and final salvation that God pledged to Abraham with an oath (6:13–18), which comes through the Messiah and is consummated at his return (9:28).

The author already made this point at the midpoint of the chapter: “These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them from afar and greeted them” (11:13). Now he repeats it as the conclusion of the whole list. This is crucial for the readers. If Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets, the greatest heroes of Israel’s story, lived and died without receiving what God had promised, and yet were commended by God for their faith, then the readers should not be surprised or discouraged that they too are waiting. The delay of the promise is not evidence of God’s unfaithfulness or of their own failure. It is the normal condition of faith in this age. Faith is precisely “the assurance of things hoped for” (11:1), and “hope that is seen is not hope” (Rom 8:24). The heroes of faith did not receive and yet kept believing; that is what made them heroes of faith.

But verse 39 does not stand alone. It is followed at once by a statement that changes everything: the reason they did not receive the promise is that God was waiting to include “us.”
