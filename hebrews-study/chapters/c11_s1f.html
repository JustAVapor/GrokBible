Something Better (11:39–40)

11:39

And all of them, though commended through their faith, did not receive the promise,

The great roll call ends not with a final hero but with a summary verdict that embraces everyone named and unnamed since Abel: καὶ πάντες kai pantes, “and all.” Abel and Enoch, Noah and Abraham, Sarah and the patriarchs, Moses and the Israelites at the sea, Rahab, the judges and kings, the prophets, the martyrs, the homeless wanderers in the caves: all of them are gathered into one sentence, and the sentence says two things about them. They were commended, and they did not receive.

Textual Note

The SBL Greek New Testament, followed in this study, reads καὶ πάντες kai pantes, “and all,” with a small number of early witnesses. Most manuscripts, and the Nestle-Aland text, read καὶ οὗτοι πάντες kai houtoi pantes, “and all these.” The meaning is the same either way: “all” refers to the whole company of the faithful just described.

“Though commended through their faith.” The participle μαρτυρηθέντες martyrēthentes, aorist passive of μαρτυρέω martyreō, “bear witness, testify,” means “having been attested, having received a good testimony.” It is best taken as concessive (“although they were commended”), since it stands in tension with the main verb that follows. The passive voice implies the agent: God bore witness to them, and he did so in the Scriptures that record their faith. This word forms a frame around the whole chapter. At the beginning the author said, “For by it [faith] the people of old received their commendation” (ἐμαρτυρήθησαν emartyrēthēsan, 11:2). Abel “received testimony that he was righteous, God testifying about his gifts” (11:4), and Enoch “was commended as having pleased God” (11:5). Now, at the end, the same verb sums up everyone. The chapter opens and closes with God’s approval of faith. Ancient writers called this kind of frame an inclusio; it tells the hearer that a unit of thought is complete.

The phrase διὰ τῆς πίστεως dia tēs pisteōs, “through the faith” (the article is best understood as “their faith,” the faith just described), makes clear that the commendation came not through their achievements, their moral record, or their descent, but through their faith. We have seen how flawed some of them were (11:32). What God attested in each case was their trust in him and in his promise.

The same word family will appear immediately in 12:1: “since we have so great a cloud of witnesses (μαρτύρων martyrōn) surrounding us.” Those who received God’s testimony now become witnesses themselves. They are not spectators in the stands watching the readers run, so much as people whose lives testify to what faith is and what it can endure. They have been “witnessed to,” and so they bear witness.

“Did not receive the promise.” Here is the surprising turn. οὐκ ἐκομίσαντο τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν ouk ekomisanto tēn epangelian. The verb κομίζω komizō, in the middle voice, means “to carry off for oneself, obtain, receive (what is due or promised).” It appeared in 10:36, in the exhortation that sets up this whole chapter: “you have need of endurance, so that when you have done the will of God you may receive (κομίσησθε komisēsthe) the promise.” It appeared also in 11:19, where Abraham “received back” Isaac. The readers are told that they must endure to receive the promise; now they learn that none of the heroes of faith received it in their lifetime.

As noted at 11:33, the singular with the article, τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν tēn epangelian, “the promise,” is significant. Many of these believers obtained particular promises (11:33): a son for Abraham (6:15), victory for Gideon, a kingdom for David, the restoration of a child for the widow. But none received the ultimate promise to which all of these pointed. What is that promise? Hebrews has described it in several ways: the promise of entering God’s rest (4:1), “the promised eternal inheritance” (9:15), the heavenly homeland and “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (11:10, 14–16), the “better resurrection” (11:35), and “the world to come” (2:5). It is the full and final salvation that God pledged to Abraham with an oath (6:13–18), which comes through the Messiah and is consummated at his return (9:28).

The author already made this point at the midpoint of the chapter: “These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them from afar and greeted them” (11:13). Now he repeats it as the conclusion of the whole list. This is crucial for the readers. If Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets, the greatest heroes of Israel’s story, lived and died without receiving what God had promised, and yet were commended by God for their faith, then the readers should not be surprised or discouraged that they too are waiting. The delay of the promise is not evidence of God’s unfaithfulness or of their own failure. It is the normal condition of faith in this age. Faith is precisely “the assurance of things hoped for” (11:1), and “hope that is seen is not hope” (Rom 8:24). The heroes of faith did not receive and yet kept believing; that is what made them heroes of faith.

But verse 39 does not stand alone. It is followed at once by a statement that changes everything: the reason they did not receive the promise is that God was waiting to include “us.”

11:40

since God had provided something better for us, so that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

The chapter that has been entirely about “them” ends with the word “us.” Twice in this short verse the author uses the first person plural, περὶ ἡμῶν peri hēmōn, “for us,” and χωρὶς ἡμῶν chōris hēmōn, “apart from us.” The long gallery of the faithful turns out to be not a museum of the past but the story of one people, of which the readers are a part, and the story’s ending depends on them. That is the bridge to the summons of 12:1: “Therefore, since we have so great a cloud of witnesses …”

The grammar. The verse is a genitive absolute: τοῦ θεοῦ περὶ ἡμῶν κρεῖττόν τι προβλεψαμένου tou theou peri hēmōn kreitton ti problepsamenou, literally “God having foreseen [or provided] something better concerning us.” A genitive absolute is a participial clause loosely attached to the main sentence, giving its circumstances or reason; here it explains why the faithful of old did not receive the promise (11:39). Then follows a purpose clause: ἵνα μὴ χωρὶς ἡμῶν τελειωθῶσιν hina mē chōris hēmōn teleiōthōsin, “in order that apart from us they should not be made perfect.” The delay was not an accident, not a failure, and not a mere postponement. It was God’s design.

Word Study: προβλέπομαι problepomai

The verb occurs only here in the New Testament. It is formed from πρό pro, “before,” and βλέπω blepō, “see,” and means literally “to see beforehand, foresee.” From foreseeing it naturally came to mean “to provide for, to select or arrange in advance.” The middle voice here suggests that God made this provision as his own plan. English “provide” has the same history (from Latin providere, “to see ahead”).

Readers who knew the Hebrew Scriptures may hear an echo of the Abraham story that Hebrews has just recalled (11:17–19). On the way to Moriah, Abraham told Isaac: אֱלֹהִים יִרְאֶה־לּוֹ הַשֶּׂה לְעֹלָה בְּנִי Elohim yir’eh-lo hasseh le‘olah beni, literally “God will see for himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son” (Gen 22:8), and afterward he named the place יְהוָה יִרְאֶה YHWH yir’eh, “the Lord will see,” that is, “will provide” (Gen 22:14). In Hebrew as in Greek, God’s “seeing” is his providing. Whether or not the author intended the echo, the theology is the same: the God who provided the ram in Isaac’s place had, from the beginning, foreseen and provided the “something better” that would come in his Son.

“Something better.” κρεῖττόν τι kreitton ti. “Better” is the signature word of Hebrews. The Son is “better than the angels” (1:4); he brings “a better hope” (7:19), is guarantor of “a better covenant” (7:22) enacted on “better promises” (8:6), offers “better sacrifices” (9:23), and his sprinkled blood “speaks better” than Abel’s (12:24). The readers have “a better possession” (10:34); the patriarchs desired “a better country” (11:16); the martyrs sought “a better resurrection” (11:35). Now the chapter closes: God has provided “something better for us.” The indefinite “something” (ti) is not vague; it gathers up all these “better” things into one. The something better is the whole reality of the new covenant in Christ: the once-for-all sacrifice that cleanses the conscience (9:14), the access to God through the curtain (10:19–20), the eternal inheritance (9:15), and the heavenly city (12:22–24).

But better than what, and better for whom? A careful reading shows that the comparison is not that “we” get a better salvation than “they” do. The verse says that God provided something better for us, so that they should not be made perfect apart from us. The better thing benefits both. For the faithful of old, it is better than anything they received in their lifetime, since what they received were particular, provisional promises, and they died still looking ahead (11:13, 39). For us, it is better in that we live in the time when the promise has begun to be fulfilled: God “has spoken to us in these last days in his Son” (1:2), and believers “have come” to Mount Zion and the city of the living God (12:22). And it is better for all in that God’s plan, by waiting, made room for “us” to be included in the one company of his people. Had the consummation come in Abraham’s time, or Moses’ or David’s, the readers would never have been born into the family of faith. The apparent delay is the measure of God’s patience and the breadth of his purpose (compare 2 Pet 3:9).

“Made perfect.” The final verb, τελειωθῶσιν teleiōthōsin, aorist passive subjunctive of τελειόω teleioō, brings us to one of the great themes of Hebrews. To understand what it means for the faithful of old to be “made perfect,” we must trace how Hebrews has used the word.

Word Study: τελειόω teleioō in Hebrews

The verb comes from τέλος telos, “end, goal,” and means “to bring to completion, bring to the intended goal, make perfect.” In Hebrews it rarely means moral flawlessness. It means reaching the destination God appointed.

Of Christ. God made “the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings” (2:10); “having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him” (5:9); the oath appointed “a Son made perfect forever” (7:28). Christ was never imperfect morally (4:15; 7:26); his “perfecting” was the completion of his qualification as high priest and Savior through his obedience, suffering, death, and exaltation.

Of what the law could not do. “If perfection had been through the Levitical priesthood …” (7:11); “the law made nothing perfect” (7:19); the old sacrifices “cannot perfect the worshiper as to the conscience” (9:9); the law “can never … make perfect those who draw near” (10:1). Under the old covenant, sins were remembered year by year (10:3), and the way into the holy place was not yet open (9:8).

Of what Christ has done. “By a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14). The perfect tense (τετελείωκεν teteleiōken) marks a completed act with permanent results: through Christ’s sacrifice, his people are definitively cleansed, consecrated, and brought into God’s presence.

Of the departed saints. In 12:23 the readers are told that they have come “to the spirits of the righteous made perfect” (τετελειωμένων teteleiōmenōn), again a perfect participle.

So “perfection” in Hebrews is the God-appointed goal of salvation: full and final cleansing from sin, unhindered access to God, and the enjoyment of the promised inheritance. It is achieved only through Christ, and it reaches its final form in the resurrection and the unshakable kingdom.

With this background, the meaning of 11:40 comes into focus. The faithful of the old covenant could not be “made perfect” by the old covenant itself; the law “made nothing perfect” (7:19). Their perfection depended on something that had not yet happened in their lifetime: the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ. Hebrews makes this explicit in 9:15: Christ is the mediator of a new covenant, “so that, since a death has occurred for the redemption of the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance.” Christ’s death reaches backward to redeem those who lived and believed under the first covenant. Paul says the same: God in his forbearance “passed over former sins,” and the cross demonstrated his righteousness in doing so (Rom 3:25–26). Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets were saved by faith in the promise of God, and the promise was always, at its heart, the promise of the Christ (Gal 3:8, 16; John 8:56; 1 Pet 1:10–12). Their faith looked forward to the one in whom “we” now believe as having come.

Interpretive Options: When Are They “Made Perfect”?

  • At the consummation. On this view, “made perfect” refers to the final state, the resurrection and the full inheritance at Christ’s return (9:28). The faithful of old await, together with us, the day when all God’s people will be raised and glorified at once. Paul’s teaching that the dead in Christ and the living will be gathered together at his coming (1 Thess 4:15–17), and the picture of the martyrs told to wait “until the number of their fellow servants … should be complete” (Rev 6:9–11), support this.
  • At the cross and exaltation of Christ. On this view, the perfection is the cleansing and access accomplished by Christ’s sacrifice (10:14), which the faithful of old received when Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary with his own blood (9:12, 24). The perfect participle of 12:23, “the spirits of the righteous made perfect,” suggests that the departed saints already enjoy this perfection in God’s presence.

These views are not truly rivals; Hebrews holds both together. The decisive act of perfecting has already been accomplished by Christ’s one offering (10:14), and its benefits have already been applied to the departed righteous, who now stand as “spirits … made perfect” in the heavenly Jerusalem (12:23). Yet the final goal, bodily resurrection and the inheritance of the unshakable kingdom, still lies ahead for them and for us together (11:35; 12:26–28; 13:14). They are perfected through the same sacrifice as we are, and they will be glorified at the same moment as we will be. In both respects it is true that “apart from us they should not be made perfect.” Scripture leaves no room for the idea that the saints of the old covenant were saved by some other means than Christ’s sacrifice, or that they belong to a different destiny or a separate people from those who believe in Christ now.

One people of God. This leads to the deepest theological point of the verse: there is one people of God across the ages, saved by one Mediator, through one sacrifice, by one faith, for one inheritance. The heroes of Hebrews 11 and the readers of Hebrews belong to a single company. That is why the author can say “apart from us,” binding together the dead patriarchs and the living congregation into one body whose completion requires both. Hebrews 12:22–24 will picture this company assembled: “the festal gathering and assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven,” “the spirits of the righteous made perfect,” and “Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant.” Old covenant saints and new covenant believers stand together around the same Mediator. The rest of the New Testament teaches the same unity. Paul describes one olive tree, the people of God rooted in the patriarchs, into which believing Gentiles are grafted alongside believing Israel (Rom 11:17–24); he says that those who believe are “sons of Abraham” and “Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise” (Gal 3:7, 29); he speaks of Gentiles once “strangers to the covenants of promise” now made “fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God” (Eph 2:12, 19). In the vision of the new Jerusalem, the gates bear the names of the twelve tribes of Israel and the foundations bear the names of the twelve apostles (Rev 21:12–14): one city, one people, old and new together.

Jesus himself spoke of the privilege of living in the time of fulfillment: “Many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see, and did not see it, and to hear what you hear, and did not hear it” (Matt 13:17). Peter wrote that the prophets “searched and inquired carefully” about the salvation to come, and “it was revealed to them that they were serving not themselves but you” (1 Pet 1:10–12). Hebrews 11:40 says the same thing from the other side: the faithful of old waited so that we might share their inheritance, and now we may not fail them by falling away.

The force of the argument for the readers. We must not lose sight of why the preacher says this. His congregation was tempted to draw back from Christ toward the visible securities of the old covenant: the earthly sanctuary, the Levitical priesthood, the annual sacrifices. Hebrews 11:39–40 strikes at the heart of that temptation. The heroes they revered, Abraham and Moses and David, did not find their perfection in the old covenant. They were looking forward to something better, and that something better has now come in Jesus. To turn back from Christ to the old order would be to turn away from the very thing the patriarchs and prophets were waiting for. It would be like reaching the door of the house to which one’s ancestors journeyed for centuries, and then walking back out into the wilderness. The fathers did not receive the promise in their lifetime; the readers now have “come to Mount Zion” (12:22), have “a great high priest who has passed through the heavens” (4:14), and have “boldness to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus” (10:19). Shall they throw that away?

At the same time, the verse dignifies the readers’ endurance. If the faithful of old are not to be made perfect without “us,” then the readers’ perseverance is not a private matter. They are part of the story that began with Abel, and the story is moving toward its ending in which all God’s people will be brought to the goal together. Every believer who endures is one more participant in a single great race (12:1), run by generations of the faithful and finished by the one who is “the pioneer and perfecter (τελειωτής teleiōtēs) of faith” (12:2). The very word that closes chapter 11, “made perfect,” is echoed in the title given to Jesus in the next breath. The perfecting of the saints is the work of the Perfecter. He is the “something better” that God foresaw and provided. To him the whole gallery of faith points, and to him the readers are now told to look.

For Reflection

  1. Hebrews 11 defines faith as “the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (11:1), and then shows that definition in action, from Abel to the martyrs. Which example in the chapter most clearly shows you what it means to treat the unseen God and his promises as more real than what you can see? How might that example change a decision you face now?
  2. Moses “considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt” (11:26), and the readers are later called to go out to Jesus “bearing his reproach” (13:13). What “treasures of Egypt” (status, security, approval, comfort) are most likely to compete with your loyalty to Christ and his people? What would it look like for you to choose to be “mistreated along with the people of God” rather than to keep your distance from them?
  3. The chapter includes deeply flawed people: Sarah who laughed, Jacob the deceiver, Rahab the prostitute, Gideon, Samson, Jephthah, and David. What does it teach you about God’s grace that he commends their faith rather than their record? How should this shape the way you view your own failures and the failures of other believers?
  4. In 11:33–35a faith conquers kingdoms, escapes the sword, and receives the dead back to life; in 11:35b–38 faith is tortured, sawn in two, and dies by the sword. How does this pairing guard you against assuming that faith always leads to deliverance in this life? What does it mean for you to trust God for “a better resurrection” when deliverance does not come?
  5. The world was “not worthy” of those it drove into deserts and caves (11:38). How does this verdict challenge the way your culture, and perhaps your own heart, measures the worth of a person or a life? How can your church honor those who suffer for Christ, including believers imprisoned or mistreated today (13:3)?
  6. “God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect” (11:40). How does knowing that you belong to one people of God with Abraham, Moses, and the prophets, saved by the same Christ and awaiting the same city, strengthen you to keep running the race of 12:1–2? What would it mean, in light of this verse, to “draw back” (10:38–39), and why would it be such a loss?
