13:13

So then, let us go out to him outside the camp, bearing his reproach.

From doctrine the author moves to the call he has been preparing for. τοίνυν toinyn, “so then, accordingly,” is a strong word drawing a conclusion. It is rare in the New Testament (elsewhere Luke 20:25; 1 Cor 9:26). Then comes ἐξερχώμεθα πρὸς αὐτὸν ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς exerchōmetha pros auton exō tēs parembolēs, “let us go out to him outside the camp.” This is the last of the great “let us” exhortations of Hebrews: “let us fear” (4:1), “let us strive to enter” (4:11), “let us hold fast” (4:14; 10:23), “let us draw near” (4:16; 10:22), “let us go on to maturity” (6:1), “let us consider one another” (10:24), “let us run with endurance” (12:1), “let us have grace” (12:28). The present tense suggests an ongoing movement: “let us keep going out.” This is not a single decision but a direction of life.

The striking thing is the direction. Throughout the sermon the call has been to draw near, to enter the sanctuary, to approach the throne of grace (4:16; 7:25; 10:19–22; 12:22). Now the call is to go out. But the two are not opposed. They describe the same movement from two sides. Those who have drawn near to God through Jesus’ blood in the heavenly sanctuary must, on earth, take their place with Jesus outside the camp. Access to God’s presence and exclusion from the world’s approval go together, as they did for Jesus himself.

The key words are πρὸς αὐτόν pros auton, “to him.” The readers are not called to go outside for its own sake, or to seek suffering as a virtue. They go out because that is where he is. If Jesus is outside the camp, then the place of true worship and fellowship with God is outside the camp, wherever he is.

Old Testament Text: Exodus 33:7

There is an Old Testament precedent for going out of the camp to seek God. After the golden calf, God refused to go up in the midst of the people (Exod 33:3), and Moses pitched a tent outside: וּמֹשֶׁה יִקַּח אֶת־הָאֹהֶל וְנָטָה־לוֹ מִחוּץ לַמַּחֲנֶה הַרְחֵק מִן־הַמַּחֲנֶה וְקָרָא לוֹ אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד וְהָיָה כָּל־מְבַקֵּשׁ יְהוָה יֵצֵא אֶל־אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד אֲשֶׁר מִחוּץ לַמַּחֲנֶה — u-Mosheh yiqqach et-ha’ohel ve-natah-lo michuts lammachaneh harcheq min-hammachaneh, ve-qara lo ohel mo‘ed; ve-hayah kol-mevaqqesh YHWH yetse el-ohel mo‘ed asher michuts lammachaneh, “Now Moses used to take the tent and pitch it outside the camp, far from the camp, and he called it the tent of meeting. And everyone who sought the Lord would go out to the tent of meeting, which was outside the camp” (Exod 33:7).

Hebrews does not quote this verse, and we should not claim that the author had it in mind. But the parallel is suggestive. When the camp was defiled by idolatry, the presence of the Lord was found outside it, and those who sought him had to go out. The phrase “outside the camp” in Exodus 33:7 is the same Hebrew phrase as in Leviticus 16:27. The readers too are called to leave the place that has rejected God’s Son and to seek the Lord where he may now be found.

The manner of going is τὸν ὀνειδισμὸν αὐτοῦ φέροντες ton oneidismon autou pherontes, “bearing his reproach.” ὀνειδισμός oneidismos means “reproach, insult, public disgrace.” It was the readers’ experience in their earlier days, when they were “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:33). It was the choice of Moses, who “considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt” (11:26). Now the readers are to carry the reproach that belongs to Jesus, the shame of being associated with a crucified man. Jesus himself “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2). “Bearing” recalls the picture of Jesus carrying his own cross out of the city (John 19:17), and his saying, “Whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me cannot be my disciple” (Luke 14:27).

Interpretive Options: What Is “the Camp”?

  • Judaism and the temple cult. Many commentators take “the camp” to mean the religious system of the old covenant, including the temple, the priesthood, the sacrifices, and the sacred meals of 13:9–10. To go outside the camp is to break decisively with any reliance on that system, accepting the reproach of being cast out by the synagogue community.
  • The world and its securities. Others take “the camp” more broadly as any human society, religious or secular, that offers security and status at the price of disowning Jesus. Going outside means accepting the life of a stranger and exile (11:13), with no lasting city (13:14).

The context favors the first meaning as the primary one. The passage concerns the altar, the tabernacle, the high priest, and the foods of 13:9–11. The readers were tempted to draw back toward the old covenant, which offered a recognized community and an established standing. In the Roman world Judaism was an ancient and generally tolerated religion. A community devoted to a crucified Messiah, and cut off from the synagogue, had no such shelter. To “go out to him” meant giving up the protection, belonging, and respectability that remaining inside would offer.

But the next verse broadens the scope: “here we have no lasting city.” The principle applies to every “camp” that would keep believers from Jesus, whether a religious system that offers security without Christ or a society that offers acceptance if one is ashamed of him. Many interpreters through the centuries have applied the words to the believer’s pilgrimage through the world. That application is legitimate, as long as the original focus is not lost. For the first readers, the specific camp was the old covenant order that had rejected Jesus.

The call to go outside the camp is not a call to despise Israel or its Scriptures. The whole sermon is built on Israel’s Scriptures, and its heroes are Israel’s saints. Nor is it a call for Christians to withdraw from society. It is a call to stand with Jesus, wherever he is, and to accept whatever loss comes from doing so. Moses chose to “suffer affliction with the people of God” rather than enjoy “the fleeting pleasures of sin” (11:25). The readers must make the same choice.

13:14

For here we have no lasting city, but we are seeking the city that is to come.

The “for” gives the reason why it is not a loss to leave the camp. οὐ γὰρ ἔχομεν ὧδε μένουσαν πόλιν ou gar echomen hōde menousan polin, “for we do not have here a lasting city.” “Here” (ὧδε hōde) means on earth, in the present age. μένουσαν menousan, “lasting, remaining,” is the same word used for the “lasting possession” of 10:34 and for the unshakable things that “remain” in 12:27. Every earthly city is among the things that can be shaken. Only what God establishes remains.

Then the positive side: ἀλλὰ τὴν μέλλουσαν ἐπιζητοῦμεν alla tēn mellousan epizētoumen, “but the coming one we are seeking.” The verb ἐπιζητέω epizēteō means “seek, strive for, long for.” It is the same verb used of the patriarchs in 11:14: “those who say such things make it clear that they are seeking a homeland.” The city “that is to come” is the city of chapter 11, the city “that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God,” which Abraham was looking for (11:10). God “has prepared for them a city” (11:16). In one sense the readers have already come to it: “You have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (12:22). In another sense they are still seeking it, because they have not yet entered into its full possession. Like the “world to come” (2:5) and the “good things to come” (9:11; 10:1), the city belongs to the future that has already broken into the present through Christ.

To the first readers, this verse may have had a pointed relevance. If, as this study holds, Hebrews was written before AD 70, then Jerusalem, with its temple, still stood. To many Jews it seemed the most permanent of cities, the city of God, the place where his name dwelt. Within a few years Roman armies would besiege and destroy it, burning the temple. Jesus had foretold this: “Not one stone will be left here upon another that will not be thrown down” (Mark 13:2; compare Luke 19:43–44; 21:20–24). The author does not mention that coming destruction, and we should not read it into his words. But he states the principle that it would soon illustrate. Earthly Jerusalem is not the lasting city. Neither is Rome, which Roman poets were already calling the “eternal city,” and where the readers probably lived. Neither is any city of this age.

This has practical consequences for everything in the chapter. People who seek a coming city can afford to open their homes to strangers (13:2), to identify with prisoners (13:3), to be content with what they have (13:5), and to bear reproach (13:13). They do not need to cling to what they will have to leave behind. Augustine’s great work The City of God is, in part, an extended meditation on this truth: the people of God live in the earthly city as pilgrims, citizens of a city whose founder is God. Paul says the same: “our citizenship is in heaven, from which we also eagerly await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ” (Phil 3:20).

13:15

Through him, then, let us continually offer up to God a sacrifice of praise, that is, the fruit of lips that confess his name.

Now comes the positive side of the altar. Believers have an altar (13:10). What do they offer on it? Not sin offerings, since Christ’s one sacrifice has made those unnecessary forever (10:18). They offer sacrifices of gratitude.

The sentence begins with δι’ αὐτοῦ di’ autou, “through him,” placed first for emphasis. Christian worship is always offered through Jesus, the high priest who brings his people to God (7:25). Under the old covenant, offerings were brought to the priest, who presented them at the altar. Now the great high priest presents his people’s praise. Peter speaks in the same way of offering “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ” (1 Pet 2:5).

ἀναφέρωμεν θυσίαν αἰνέσεως διὰ παντὸς τῷ θεῷ anapherōmen thysian aineseōs dia pantos tō theō, “let us offer up a sacrifice of praise continually to God.” ἀναφέρω anapherō, “offer up,” is a temple word, used of bringing a sacrifice up onto the altar. Hebrews used it of the high priests “offering up” sacrifices daily, and of Christ, who “offered up himself” (7:27; compare 9:28). διὰ παντός dia pantos, “continually,” also has a temple background. The daily burnt offering, a lamb every morning and every evening, was called עֹלַת תָּמִיד ‘olat tamid, “the continual burnt offering” (Exod 29:42; Num 28:3). The same Hebrew word appears in David’s vow of praise: תָּמִיד תְּהִלָּתוֹ בְּפִי tamid tehillato befi, “his praise shall continually be in my mouth” (Ps 34:1; Hebrew 34:2). The priests of the old covenant offered the continual lamb. The people of the new covenant offer continual praise (compare 1 Thess 5:16–18).

Word Study: θυσία αἰνέσεως thysia aineseōs, “sacrifice of praise”

This phrase is the usual Greek Old Testament rendering of the Hebrew זֶבַח תּוֹדָה zevach todah, “sacrifice of thanksgiving.” The todah was a kind of peace offering brought in gratitude for deliverance (Lev 7:12). Its distinctive feature was a public testimony: the worshiper told the assembly what God had done. The psalms show how the idea grew. “Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving, and pay your vows to the Most High” (Ps 50:14). “The one who offers thanksgiving as his sacrifice honors me” (Ps 50:23). “To you I will offer the sacrifice of thanksgiving and call on the name of the Lord” (Ps 116:17: לְךָ־אֶזְבַּח זֶבַח תּוֹדָה וּבְשֵׁם יְהוָה אֶקְרָא lekha-ezbach zevach todah u-veshem YHWH eqra). “Let them offer sacrifices of thanksgiving and tell of his works with joyful song” (Ps 107:22). Already within the Old Testament, the spoken thanksgiving was understood as the heart of the offering. The psalmists knew that “the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit” (Ps 51:17; Hebrew 51:19). A later Jewish tradition held that in the age to come all sacrifices would cease except the thank offering. Whether or not the author knew that saying, he agrees with its insight. The sacrifice of praise is the one sacrifice that the new covenant does not set aside.

The author then explains what he means: τοῦτ’ ἔστιν καρπὸν χειλέων ὁμολογούντων τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ tout’ estin karpon cheileōn homologountōn tō onomati autou, “that is, fruit of lips confessing his name.”

Old Testament Text: Hosea 14:2 (Hebrew 14:3)

The phrase “fruit of lips” comes from Hosea’s call to Israel to return to the Lord: “Take words with you and return to the Lord; say to him, ‘Take away all iniquity and accept what is good, and we will pay the bulls of our lips’” (Hos 14:2; Hebrew 14:3). The Hebrew phrase is וּנְשַׁלְּמָה פָרִים שְׂפָתֵינוּ — u-neshallemah farim sefatenu, “and let us pay [as] bulls our lips.” The repentant people promise to offer their words of confession and praise in place of the bulls of sacrifice. The Greek translation reads instead “we will render the fruit of our lips” (καρπὸν χειλέων karpon cheileōn). The difference arises from the Hebrew consonants. The word for “bulls,” farim, is written with the letters p-r-y-m. Without its final m, it is פְּרִי peri, “fruit.” If that final m is read as the Hebrew prefix meaning “from,” attached to the next word, the phrase becomes “fruit from our lips.” The Greek translators apparently read the consonants in this way.

Both readings make the same point. In the Hebrew, lips take the place of bulls: words of repentance and praise are offered in place of animal sacrifices. In the Greek, praise is the “fruit” that lips produce. Hebrews uses the Greek wording, but the Hebrew meaning fits its argument even more sharply. Under the new covenant, the bulls are gone (10:4). What remains is the offering of lips that confess God’s name.

The participle ὁμολογούντων homologountōn, “confessing,” comes from the same family as ὁμολογία homologia, “confession,” a key word in Hebrews. Jesus is “the apostle and high priest of our confession” (3:1). “Let us hold fast our confession” (4:14). “Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering” (10:23). The patriarchs “confessed that they were strangers and exiles on the earth” (11:13). The construction here, with the dative (“confessing to his name”), is unusual. It probably reflects the Greek Old Testament’s use of a related verb for giving thanks and praise to God’s name (as in many psalms). So the phrase means both “acknowledging his name” and “praising his name.” Praise in this sense is public. It is the open acknowledgment, before others, of the God who has acted in Jesus. In the setting of 13:13, that confession could bring reproach. The sacrifice of praise was not merely an emotion. It cost something to say aloud, in Rome or anywhere else, that the crucified Jesus is Lord.

13:16

And do not neglect doing good and sharing, for with such sacrifices God is well pleased.

Praise with the lips has a partner: generosity with the hands. τῆς δὲ εὐποιΐας καὶ κοινωνίας μὴ ἐπιλανθάνεσθε tēs de eupoiias kai koinōnias mē epilanthanesthe, “and doing good and sharing do not forget.” The command repeats the verb of 13:2 (“do not neglect hospitality”), which forms a kind of frame around the paragraph. εὐποιΐα eupoiia, “doing good, beneficence,” is found only here in the New Testament. It refers to practical kindness toward others. κοινωνία koinōnia means “fellowship, partnership, sharing.” Here it has its concrete sense of sharing one’s goods. Paul uses it of the collection for the poor in Jerusalem, “a contribution (koinōnia) for the poor among the saints” (Rom 15:26; 2 Cor 9:13). The first Christians in Jerusalem devoted themselves “to the fellowship” and had “all things in common” (Acts 2:42, 44). The readers knew this well. They had become “partners” (κοινωνοί koinōnoi) with those who were mistreated (10:33). They had served and were still serving the saints (6:10).

The reason is τοιαύταις γὰρ θυσίαις εὐαρεστεῖται ὁ θεός toiautais gar thysiais euaresteitai ho theos, “for with such sacrifices God is well pleased.” “Such sacrifices” includes both praise (13:15) and generosity (13:16). εὐαρεστέω euaresteō, “please, be well pleased,” takes up the language of 12:28. Its use in chapter 11 is also important: Enoch “was commended as having pleased God,” and “without faith it is impossible to please him” (11:5–6). The God who was pleased with faith is pleased with the fruit of faith in love.

This is not a new idea in Scripture. The prophets constantly taught that God desires mercy and justice more than animal sacrifices. “For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings” (Hos 6:6). “To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to the Lord than sacrifice” (Prov 21:3). “He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the Lord require of you but to do justice, to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?” (Mic 6:8). Jesus quoted Hosea against those who neglected mercy (Matt 9:13; 12:7). Paul describes the Philippians’ gift to him as “a fragrant offering, an acceptable sacrifice, pleasing (εὐάρεστον euareston) to God” (Phil 4:18). The Levitical sacrifices are gone, but the sacrificial life is not. It takes the form of praise to God and service to others. The two always go together. James warns that praise without practical help is empty (Jas 2:15–16), and John asks how God’s love can abide in one who sees a brother in need and closes his heart (1 John 3:17).

This verse also shows that the earlier commands of the chapter (love, hospitality, care for prisoners, contentment) are more than moral advice. They are acts of worship. Hospitality, visiting the prisoner, and sharing with the needy are sacrifices placed on the altar of Christ’s cross, offered through him, and accepted by God because of him.

13:17

Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they keep watch over your souls as those who will give an account. Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you.

The paragraph that began with the leaders of the past (13:7) closes with the leaders of the present. The readers are to remember the first and obey the second. πείθεσθε τοῖς ἡγουμένοις ὑμῶν καὶ ὑπείκετε peithesthe tois hēgoumenois hymōn kai hypeikete, “obey your leaders and submit.” πείθεσθε peithesthe is the middle-passive of πείθω peithō, “persuade.” In this form it means “be persuaded by, listen to, follow, obey.” The word suggests the obedience that comes from being convinced, not merely forced. The second verb, ὑπείκω hypeikō, “yield, give way, submit,” occurs only here in the New Testament. It suggests deference: giving way to another’s direction rather than insisting on one’s own. Both verbs are present imperatives, describing a settled attitude rather than a single act.

Why should the readers be told this? The context suggests a congregation under strain. Some were neglecting the assembly (10:25). Some were attracted to “strange teachings” (13:9). In such a situation the faithful leaders who held to the apostolic message were a safeguard. To resist them was to expose oneself to the very dangers the sermon warns against. The author’s own authority supports theirs. At the end of the letter he asks the readers to “greet all your leaders” (13:24), a sign that he stands with them.

The reason given is αὐτοὶ γὰρ ἀγρυπνοῦσιν ὑπὲρ τῶν ψυχῶν ὑμῶν ὡς λόγον ἀποδώσοντες autoi gar agrypnousin hyper tōn psychōn hymōn hōs logon apodōsontes, “for they keep watch over your souls as those who will give an account.”

Word Study: ἀγρυπνέω agrypneō, “keep watch”

The verb means literally “to be sleepless, to lie awake,” and so “to keep watch, be alert.” Jesus used it in calling his disciples to stay awake and watch for his return (Mark 13:33; Luke 21:36). Paul used it of persevering in prayer (Eph 6:18). The picture is of a shepherd who stays awake through the night to protect the flock from thieves and wild animals (compare Luke 2:8, “keeping watch over their flock by night”), or a watchman on the city wall. God appointed Ezekiel as “a watchman for the house of Israel,” responsible to warn the wicked; if he failed to warn them, their blood would be required at his hand (Ezek 3:17–21; 33:1–9). Paul told the Ephesian elders, “Pay attention to yourselves and to all the flock … to shepherd the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood” (Acts 20:28), and reminded them that he had warned each one “night and day with tears” (Acts 20:31). Faithful leadership costs sleep. Its concern is not the leader’s comfort or status but the people’s “souls,” their life before God (compare 10:39, “those who have faith for the preserving of the soul”).

The phrase ὡς λόγον ἀποδώσοντες hōs logon apodōsontes, “as those who will give an account,” uses a future participle. The leaders will one day answer to God for how they cared for the flock. Hebrews has already said that all creation lies “naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account” (4:13, where the same word logos means “account”). Leaders face that judgment with special seriousness. James warns that teachers “will receive a stricter judgment” (Jas 3:1). Peter urges elders to shepherd God’s flock “not under compulsion, but willingly … not domineering over those in your charge,” knowing that “when the chief Shepherd appears” they will receive the crown of glory (1 Pet 5:2–4). This accountability limits the leaders’ authority as much as it supports it. They are not lords of the flock but servants who will answer to its true owner. Their authority holds only as long as they speak “the word of God” (13:7). Leaders who teach “strange teachings” (13:9) have no claim on the church’s obedience (compare Gal 1:8–9).

The next clause is ἵνα μετὰ χαρᾶς τοῦτο ποιῶσιν καὶ μὴ στενάζοντες hina meta charas touto poiōsin kai mē stenazontes, “so that they may do this with joy and not groaning.” The congregation can make its leaders’ work a joy or a burden. στενάζω stenazō, “groan, sigh,” describes the inward distress of those weighed down (Rom 8:23; 2 Cor 5:2, 4). The author then adds a sober understatement: ἀλυσιτελὲς γὰρ ὑμῖν τοῦτο alysiteles gar hymin touto, “for this would be unprofitable for you.” Alysitelēs, “unprofitable, of no advantage,” is a mild word that means much more than it says. A congregation that makes its shepherds groan harms itself. It loses the benefit of their care, and it brings their sorrowful “account” before God against itself. The verse is not about protecting leaders’ feelings. It is about the good of the people.

The verse should be read in the light of the whole New Testament. Leaders are to be loved and respected “because of their work” (1 Thess 5:12–13). They are to be shown honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching (1 Tim 5:17). At the same time they must be examples, not tyrants (1 Pet 5:3), and the church must test what it hears against the word of God (Acts 17:11; 1 John 4:1). Hebrews 13:17 does not give leaders unlimited authority. It calls a congregation to trust and follow faithful shepherds who are watching over them in the fear of God.

13:18

Pray for us, for we are persuaded that we have a good conscience, desiring to conduct ourselves honorably in all things.

The author now speaks of himself. Προσεύχεσθε περὶ ἡμῶν Proseuchesthe peri hēmōn, “keep praying for us.” The present tense again suggests continuing prayer. Paul often made the same request (Rom 15:30; 2 Cor 1:11; Col 4:3; 1 Thess 5:25; 2 Thess 3:1). The plural “us” may include the author and his companions in ministry, or it may be a plural of modesty in which the writer refers to himself. The author has used “we” of himself before (2:5; 5:11; 6:9, 11). In the next verse he switches to “I.” Whichever is meant, the request shows the humility of a teacher who has just spoken with great authority. He has urged and warned. Now he asks for help. He needs the readers’ prayers as much as they need his exhortation.

The reason is πειθόμεθα γὰρ ὅτι καλὴν συνείδησιν ἔχομεν peithometha gar hoti kalēn syneidēsin echomen, “for we are persuaded that we have a good conscience.” πειθόμεθα peithometha comes from the same verb as “obey” in 13:17 (peithō). Here it means “we are confident, we are convinced.” συνείδησις syneidēsis, “conscience,” has played a large part in the sermon. The old sacrifices could not perfect the worshiper “as to the conscience” (9:9). The blood of Christ “cleanses our conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14). Believers draw near with hearts “sprinkled clean from an evil conscience” (10:22). The “good conscience” of the author is not a claim to sinlessness. It is the clean conscience that Christ’s blood provides, together with a sincere integrity of life. Paul could speak in the same way before the council (Acts 23:1; 24:16; compare 2 Cor 1:12).

Why mention his conscience at all? Some think the author’s conduct had been questioned. Perhaps his absence had been misunderstood, or his hard words had caused offense. The verse may hint at this, but the text does not say so, and we should not build much on it. At the least, the author offers his sincerity as the reason they may pray for him with confidence. He is not asking them to support a man with a hidden agenda.

The final phrase is ἐν πᾶσιν καλῶς θέλοντες ἀναστρέφεσθαι en pasin kalōs thelontes anastrephesthai, “in all things desiring to conduct ourselves honorably.” The verb is related to anastrophē, “way of life,” in 13:7. The author wants his own conduct, like that of the former leaders, to be worth imitating. “Honorably” (καλῶς kalōs, “well, nobly, rightly”) echoes the “good” (kalos) conscience. There is an inner integrity and an outward conduct that matches it. Notice the word “desiring.” The author does not claim to have achieved perfect conduct. He claims a sincere will to live well in every area of life. That is exactly what God works in his people through the new covenant (13:21; compare 8:10).

13:19

And I urge you all the more earnestly to do this, so that I may be restored to you the sooner.

For the first time the author uses the first person singular in a personal way (compare the “I” of 11:32). περισσοτέρως δὲ παρακαλῶ τοῦτο ποιῆσαι perissoterōs de parakalō touto poiēsai, “and all the more I urge you to do this.” περισσοτέρως perissoterōs, “more abundantly, especially,” adds urgency. παρακαλῶ parakalō, “I urge, I exhort,” is the verb behind “the word of exhortation” in 13:22. The author has been exhorting them throughout the sermon (3:13; 10:25). Now he exhorts them to pray for him.

The purpose is ἵνα τάχιον ἀποκατασταθῶ ὑμῖν hina tachion apokatastathō hymin, “so that I may be restored to you the sooner.” ἀποκαθίστημι apokathistēmi means “restore, give back, bring back to a former state.” It is used of a withered hand restored to health (Mark 3:5) and of the kingdom being restored to Israel (Acts 1:6). Here it implies that the author has been with the readers before and belongs with them. He is not a stranger writing to strangers. He is a member of the community, or at least a teacher well known to it, who is now separated from them and longs to return.

What was keeping him away? The text does not say. Some have suggested imprisonment, but 13:23 implies that the author is free to travel once Timothy arrives. Illness, the demands of ministry elsewhere, or dangers on the road are all possible. What is clear is that the author believes prayer makes a difference to such circumstances. God’s providence governs his travels, and the prayers of the church are among the means God uses. Paul expressed the same confidence when he asked the Romans to pray that he might come to them “with joy, by the will of God” (Rom 15:30–32), and when he told Philemon, “I hope that through your prayers I will be graciously given to you” (Phlm 22). Paul’s verb in Philemon, “be graciously given,” is different, but the hope is the same. The bond between teacher and congregation is one of mutual love and prayer. He has prayed for them through this sermon. Now he asks them to pray for him.

For Reflection

  1. The commands of 13:1–6 concern ordinary things: love among believers, hospitality, prisoners, marriage, and money. Why does the author present these as part of the worship that pleases God (12:28; 13:16)? Which of them is most neglected in your own life or church?
  2. Hebrews 13:5 grounds contentment not in having enough but in God’s promise, “I will never let go of you, nor will I ever forsake you.” How does knowing that Jesus was forsaken on the cross (Matt 27:46) strengthen that promise for you?
  3. “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and forever” (13:8). In a time of changing leaders, changing culture, and changing personal circumstances, how does this truth steady faith? How does it guard against “various and strange teachings” (13:9)?
  4. The Day of Atonement sacrifice was burned outside the camp, and Jesus suffered outside the gate (13:11–12). What “camp” offers you security, belonging, or respectability at the cost of being identified with Jesus? What would it mean for you to “go out to him … bearing his reproach” (13:13)?
  5. The only sacrifices left for believers are the sacrifice of praise and the sacrifices of doing good and sharing (13:15–16). How might your public speech about God and your generosity to others be offered “through him” as worship?
  6. How do 13:7, 13:17, and 13:18–19 together describe a healthy relationship between a congregation and its leaders? What responsibilities fall on each side?
