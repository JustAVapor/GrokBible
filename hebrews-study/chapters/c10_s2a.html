Draw Near, Hold Fast, Do Not Throw Away Your Confidence (10:19–39)

Translation

19Having, therefore, brothers, boldness for entrance into the holy places by the blood of Jesus — 20an entrance which he inaugurated for us as a new and living way through the curtain, that is, his flesh — 21and having a great priest over the house of God, 22let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our body washed with pure water. 23Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful. 24And let us consider one another so as to stir one another up to love and good works, 25not forsaking our own assembling together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near.

26For if we go on sinning willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, 27but a certain fearful expectation of judgment and a fury of fire that is about to consume the adversaries. 28Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without mercy on the testimony of two or three witnesses. 29How much worse punishment, do you suppose, will he be judged to deserve who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, and has regarded as common the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and has insulted the Spirit of grace? 30For we know him who said, “Vengeance is mine; I will repay,” and again, “The Lord will judge his people.” 31It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.

32But keep remembering the former days, in which, after you had been enlightened, you endured a great contest of sufferings, 33sometimes being made a public spectacle by reproaches and afflictions, and sometimes becoming partners with those who were treated so. 34For you indeed shared the sufferings of the prisoners, and you accepted with joy the plundering of your possessions, knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one. 35Do not, therefore, throw away your boldness, which has a great reward. 36For you have need of endurance, so that, having done the will of God, you may receive the promise.

37For yet “a very little while” — “the Coming One will come and will not delay; 38but my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul takes no pleasure in him.”

39But we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul.

Setting and Structure

With 10:19 the sermon reaches its great hinge. For six chapters (4:14–10:18) the preacher has unfolded what he himself called the “main point” (8:1): we have a high priest after the order of Melchizedek, seated at God’s right hand, who has entered the true sanctuary by his own blood, inaugurated the new covenant, and by one offering perfected for all time those who are being sanctified (10:14). The exposition is now finished. The little word οὖν oun, “therefore,” gathers all of it into a single hand and turns it toward the hearers. Everything that follows — the call to draw near, the terrible warning, the tender reminder of past faithfulness, the summons to endurance — rests on what Christ has done.

The opening verses deliberately echo 4:14–16, where the central exposition began. There the author wrote, “Having, therefore, a great high priest … let us hold fast the confession … let us draw near with boldness.” Here he writes, “Having, therefore … boldness … and having a great priest … let us draw near … let us hold fast the confession.” The same participle (“having”), the same conjunction (“therefore”), the same two exhortations, the same key words (“boldness,” “great priest,” “confession,” “draw near”). Many commentators, Lane and Guthrie among them, recognize these two passages as bookends framing the sermon’s central teaching about Christ’s priesthood. But between 4:14 and 10:19 something has changed: then the readers were told to approach “the throne of grace”; now they are told precisely how the way has been opened — by blood, through the curtain, by a living way.

The section has three movements, and each has a different emotional temperature:

1. Invitation (10:19–25). In Greek this is one long, carefully built sentence. It gives two grounds (“having … boldness” and “having … a great priest,” 10:19–21) and then three exhortations in the first-person plural — the preacher includes himself — “let us draw near” (10:22), “let us hold fast” (10:23), “let us consider one another” (10:24). The three exhortations are tied to the three great Christian graces: faith (“full assurance of faith,” 10:22), hope (“the confession of our hope,” 10:23), and love (“to love and good works,” 10:24). The same triad appears across the New Testament (1 Cor 13:13; Col 1:4–5; 1 Thess 1:3; 5:8). The sentence ends with two participles that show how the third exhortation is carried out: not forsaking the gathering, but encouraging one another — with the Day in view.

2. Warning (10:26–31). Without pause (“For …”) the tone darkens. This is the fourth of the sermon’s five great warning passages (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:26–39; 12:14–29), and it is perhaps the most severe. Its logic is simple: if Christ’s sacrifice is the one and only sacrifice that removes sin (10:1–18), then the person who deliberately and persistently repudiates it has nowhere else to go. The author argues “from the lesser to the greater” (as in 2:2–3 and 12:25): if rejecting Moses’ law brought death, how much worse to spurn the Son of God. Two quotations from the Song of Moses (Deut 32:35–36) seal the warning, and the paragraph closes with a sentence that has rung in the church’s ears ever since: “It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.”

3. Encouragement (10:32–39). Then, as in 6:9–12 after the warning of 6:4–8, the preacher turns from severity to warmth. He calls the congregation to remember their own past: soon after their conversion they had endured public abuse, imprisonment of their members, and the loss of their property — and they had borne it with joy. Do not throw away that boldness now, he urges; you need endurance. A combined quotation of Isaiah 26:20 and Habakkuk 2:3–4 promises that the Coming One will soon come and that God’s righteous one lives by faith. The final verse (10:39) is a confident declaration that the preacher and his hearers belong to faith, not to shrinking back — and the word “faith” becomes the launching pad for the great roll call of chapter 11.

Two features of this structure deserve notice. First, the warning is framed on both sides by assurance: it is preceded by the open way into God’s presence and followed by the memory of the readers’ past faithfulness and the confident “we are not of those who shrink back.” The warning is not the whole message, but it is an indispensable part of it. Second, key words bind the section together. “Boldness” (παρρησία parrēsia) opens it (10:19) and returns at its climax (10:35): the boldness to enter God’s presence is the same boldness the readers must not throw away. “Fearful” frames the warning (10:27, 31). “Endure/endurance” links the readers’ past (10:32) to their present need (10:36) and points ahead to 12:1–3. And “faith” runs from 10:22 through 10:38–39 into chapter 11.

Historically, the section gives us our clearest window into the congregation’s life: gatherings that some were abandoning (10:25), and “former days” of public reproach, imprisonment, and plundered property — but not yet martyrdom (12:4). (The likely setting of those sufferings is discussed at 10:34.) The danger they now faced was apparently not fresh violence but weariness, fear, social pressure, and the pull back toward the recognized religion of the synagogue, while the old sacrifices still smoked in Jerusalem. To such people the author says: the way into God’s true presence is open now, and it is open only through Jesus. Go in — and stay together while you go.

Verse by Verse

The Way Is Open: Draw Near, Hold Fast, Consider One Another (10:19–25)

10:19

Having, therefore, brothers, boldness for entrance into the holy places by the blood of Jesus —

The sentence begins with a present participle, Ἔχοντες echontes, “having.” The tense matters: this is not something the readers once had or will have someday, but a present possession. Before the author tells them what to do, he tells them what they already have. That order — first the gift, then the summons — is the shape of the gospel throughout Scripture. Israel was redeemed from Egypt before it was given the commandments (Exod 20:2); believers are first “sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10), and only then urged to draw near.

The address “brothers” (ἀδελφοί adelphoi, which in the plural includes the whole family of believers, sisters as well) is warm and deliberate. The author last used it in 3:12 (“Watch out, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief”). It reminds the hearers that they belong to the family which the Son is “not ashamed to call brothers” (2:11).

What they have is παρρησίαν parrēsian, “boldness.” In the Greek city the word described the free citizen’s right to speak openly in the assembly, without fear — a privilege slaves and foreigners did not have. In the Greek Old Testament and the New Testament it came to describe the freedom with which one may come before God or speak his truth before people (Acts 4:13, 29, 31; Eph 3:12; 1 John 3:21; 4:17). In Hebrews it carries both an objective and a subjective side. Objectively, it is the right of access that Christ’s blood has secured; subjectively, it is the confidence with which believers use that right (3:6; 4:16; 10:35). Here the objective side is foremost: the boldness is “for entrance” and it is “by the blood of Jesus.” It does not rest on the worshiper’s feelings or record, but on what has been done for him.

Word Study: εἴσοδος eisodos

The noun εἴσοδον eisodon means “entrance,” either the act of going in or the way by which one goes in. It is the opposite of what the author has described under the old covenant: the Holy Spirit was showing that “the way into the holy places has not yet been made manifest while the first tent still has standing” (9:8). Only the high priest went in, once a year, and not without blood (9:7). Now the entrance belongs to “us” — to all the people of God. The phrase “the holy places” (τῶν ἁγίων tōn hagiōn) is the same expression the author has used for the inner sanctuary into which Christ entered (9:12, 24–25): not the earthly tent but heaven itself, “the very presence of God” (9:24). What was once the most restricted place in Israel’s worship is now open to every believer.

“By the blood of Jesus” (ἐν τῷ αἵματι Ἰησοῦ en tō haimati Iēsou): the preposition en here has the sense “by means of” or “on the basis of.” The high priest on the Day of Atonement entered the Most Holy Place “with blood” (Lev 16:14–15; Heb 9:7, 25), carrying the blood of a bull and a goat. Jesus entered “by his own blood” (9:12), and now his people enter in the same way — not carrying blood of their own but covered by his. It is noteworthy that the author uses the simple human name “Jesus,” placed emphatically at the end of the clause. He frequently does this when he wants to stress the real humanity and the real suffering of the Savior (2:9; 6:20; 7:22; 12:2; 13:12): the blood that opens heaven was the blood of a man who lived and died in Judea.

Here the reader should feel the wonder that the original hearers, many of them raised on the temple and its rituals, would have felt. In Israel the curtain, the priestly hierarchy, and the strict regulations of the Day of Atonement all proclaimed one message: God is holy, and sinners may not casually approach him. The Mishnah, recalling the temple ritual, describes how the high priest, after entering the Most Holy Place, prayed only briefly so as not to alarm the people waiting outside (m. Yoma 5:1) — a vivid reminder of the danger the place represented. Hebrews never softens God’s holiness (10:31; 12:29). What it announces is that his holiness has been fully satisfied by a sacrifice that cleanses the conscience (9:14), so that ordinary believers may come where once even the high priest came only in fear.

10:20

— an entrance which he inaugurated for us as a new and living way through the curtain, that is, his flesh —

The relative pronoun ἣν hēn, “which,” is feminine, and most interpreters take its antecedent to be the “entrance” of 10:19 (also feminine), with “way” (ὁδόν hodon, likewise feminine) standing as a further description of it: “the entrance, which he inaugurated for us as a new and living way.” The entrance is not a bare doorway; it is a road on which believers travel.

Word Study: ἐγκαινίζω enkainizō

The verb ἐνεκαίνισεν enekainisen (aorist, a completed act) means “inaugurated,” “dedicated,” “opened for use.” It is built on the root kain-, “new”: to “make new” in the sense of putting something into service for the first time. The Greek Old Testament uses it for the dedication of Solomon’s temple (1 Kgs 8:63, “they dedicated the house of the Lord”), and the related noun gave its name to the Feast of Dedication (John 10:22) commemorating the rededication of the temple after its defilement by Antiochus (1 Macc 4:36–59). Hebrews has already used the same verb of the first covenant: “not even the first covenant was inaugurated (ἐγκεκαίνισται enkekainistai) without blood” (9:18). The link is deliberate. As Moses inaugurated the Sinai covenant with blood (9:18–20; Exod 24:8), so Jesus has inaugurated the way of the new covenant with his own blood. He did not merely show the way; he opened it, as a new temple is opened for worship, and he did so “for us” (ἡμῖν hēmin).

The way is “new and living.” The first adjective, πρόσφατον prosphaton, originally seems to have meant “freshly slaughtered” (from a root connected with slaying), and some older interpreters drew a moving lesson from this: the way is as fresh as a sacrifice just slain. By the first century, however, the word had long been used simply for what is “recent,” “fresh,” or “new”: the Greek Old Testament uses it for fresh grapes (Num 6:3) and in Ecclesiastes’ famous line that there is nothing new under the sun (Eccl 1:9), and Luke uses the related adverb of Aquila and Priscilla, “recently come from Italy” (Acts 18:2). We should not build doctrine on an etymology the author may never have had in mind. The point is that the way is recent — it did not exist before Christ opened it (9:8) — and it never grows old. It is the way of the new covenant (8:8, 13), which will never become “obsolete and growing old” as the first did (8:13).

The second adjective, “living” (ζῶσαν zōsan, a present participle), is even more striking. A road is not ordinarily alive. But this way is inseparable from the living Christ, who “always lives to intercede” for those who draw near to God through him (7:25), who serves “by the power of an indestructible life” (7:16). The old way into the sanctuary was through the blood of dead animals; the new way is through one who died and lives forever. In this light it is natural to remember the Lord’s own words, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). The way is living because the one who is the way is alive; it also gives life to those who walk it (contrast the “dead works” of 9:14).

The way leads “through the curtain” (διὰ τοῦ καταπετάσματος dia tou katapetasmatos). In the tabernacle a heavy woven curtain separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (Exod 26:31–33); behind it stood the ark and the mercy seat. Hebrews has already spoken of Christ entering “the inner place behind the curtain” as our forerunner (6:19–20) and of the curtain as the boundary that excluded all but the high priest (9:3, 7–8).

Then come the words that have puzzled readers ever since: τοῦτ’ ἔστιν τῆς σαρκὸς αὐτοῦ tout’ estin tēs sarkos autou, “that is, his flesh.” What does “his flesh” explain?

Interpretive Options: “Through the Curtain, That Is, His Flesh”

  • View 1: “His flesh” explains “the curtain.” The curtain through which believers pass is Christ’s flesh — his human body given up in death. Grammatically this is the most natural reading, because “of his flesh” is in the genitive case, exactly matching “of the curtain” after the preposition “through” (dia), and the phrase “that is” (tout’ estin) normally explains the word immediately before it (as in 7:5; 9:11; 13:15). Supporters point to the Gospels: at the moment Jesus died, “the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom” (Mark 15:38; Matt 27:51). When his flesh was torn on the cross, the barrier was removed. They also point to 10:5 and 10:10: the “body prepared” for the Son is the means by which he did God’s will and sanctified his people.
  • View 2: “His flesh” explains “the way.” Some interpreters, troubled that the curtain is in Hebrews a barrier (9:8) and reluctant to call Christ’s flesh an obstacle, connect “his flesh” with the more distant word “way”: the new and living way is his flesh, that is, his incarnate life and sacrificial death. The difficulty is grammatical: “way” is in the accusative case while “flesh” is genitive, so the author would have written the phrase very awkwardly if he meant this.
  • View 3: “Through … his flesh” is instrumental. A related proposal notes that the preposition dia can mean both “through” in a spatial sense and “by means of.” With “the curtain” it is spatial (the way passes through the curtain); with “his flesh” it is instrumental (he opened the way by means of his flesh, that is, by his bodily self-offering). On this reading the phrase does not equate the flesh and the curtain point for point but explains how the passage through the curtain was achieved.

Weighing these, View 1 has the strongest grammatical claim, and the objection to it is weaker than it first appears. The author is not saying that Christ’s humanity was ever a barrier between God and us; he is saying that the way through the barrier was opened by means of his flesh offered in death — which is where View 3 helps. A single preposition can carry both nuances in one compact phrase: the way passes through the curtain, and the curtain was opened through his flesh. Calvin made a similar point with pastoral beauty: just as the curtain both concealed the sanctuary and yet was the way into it, so Christ’s flesh veiled his divine majesty and yet is the very means by which we come to God. The Gospel record of the torn curtain (Mark 15:38) provides a striking historical confirmation, even though Hebrews never mentions that event directly. The best reading, then, is that the author identifies the curtain with Christ’s flesh in the sense that his body, torn in death, is the means by which the barrier was removed and the way opened (compare 10:10, “through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all”).

Whatever the precise syntax, the theological point is unmistakable and wholly consistent with the rest of Scripture: there is no access to God that bypasses the incarnation and the cross. Not angels, not Moses, not the Levitical priests, not the worshiper’s own merit — only the flesh and blood of Jesus open the way. “For there is one God, and one mediator between God and human beings, a man, Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom on behalf of all” (1 Tim 2:5–6). Paul expresses the same truth in different imagery when he says that Christ “in his flesh” broke down the dividing wall and that through him “through him we both have our access in one Spirit to the Father” (Eph 2:14, 18).
