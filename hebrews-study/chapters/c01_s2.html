The Son Superior to the Angels (1:5–14)

Translation

5For to which of the angels did he ever say,

“You are my Son; today I have begotten you”?

And again,

“I will be to him a Father, and he will be to me a Son”?

6And again, when he brings the firstborn into the world, he says,

“And let all God’s angels worship him.”

7And of the angels he says,

“He makes his angels winds, and his ministers a flame of fire.”

8But to the Son:

“Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, and the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom. 9You loved righteousness and hated lawlessness; therefore God, your God, anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.”

10And,

“You, Lord, in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the works of your hands. 11They will perish, but you remain; and they will all grow old like a garment, 12and like a cloak you will roll them up; like a garment they will also be changed. But you are the same, and your years will not come to an end.”

13And to which of the angels has he ever said,

“Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet”?

14Are they not all ministering spirits, sent out for service for the sake of those who are about to inherit salvation?

Setting and Structure

Having announced in 1:4 that the Son has become “better than the angels” and has inherited a more excellent name, the author now proves the point from Scripture. He does it by stringing together seven Old Testament quotations, a form scholars call a catena (Latin for “chain”). The quotations are drawn from the Psalms (2:7; 104:4; 45:6–7; 102:25–27; 110:1), from the oracle of Nathan to David (2 Sam 7:14), and from the Song of Moses (Deut 32:43, with an echo of Ps 97:7). Every one of them is introduced as something God himself “said” or “says.” For the author, the voice heard in the Psalms and the Law is the voice of the Father speaking about and to his Son.

The arrangement is careful:

  • 1:5 — The Son’s unique relation to the Father. Two quotations (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14), joined by “and again,” introduced by the question, “To which of the angels did he ever say …?”
  • 1:6 — The angels worship the Son. One quotation (Deut 32:43 in its Greek form; compare Ps 97:7).
  • 1:7–12 — Contrast: the angels and the Son. A μέν … δέ men … de construction, the Greek way of saying “on the one hand … on the other.” On the one hand, the angels are changeable servants (Ps 104:4, one quotation). On the other hand, the Son is the eternal divine King (Ps 45:6–7) and the unchanging Creator (Ps 102:25–27), two quotations.
  • 1:13 — The Son enthroned. One quotation (Ps 110:1), introduced by the same question as 1:5, “To which of the angels has he ever said …?” This repeated question forms a frame (an inclusio) around the whole chain.
  • 1:14 — Conclusion about the angels. A final question that sums up their role: they are ministering spirits sent to serve the heirs of salvation.

Read together, the quotations trace the same path as the opening sentence, though not in the same order. The Son is declared Son and heir (1:5; compare 1:2a); he is the Creator of heaven and earth (1:10; compare 1:2b); he is God, whose throne is eternal (1:8; compare 1:3a); and he is seated at God’s right hand (1:13; compare 1:3b). The catena is the scriptural proof of the confession made in 1:1–4.

The method of stringing together texts on a theme was familiar in Judaism. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, a document known as the Florilegium (4Q174) gathers and comments on texts about the coming “branch of David,” and it links the promise of 2 Samuel 7 (“I will be to him a father, and he will be to me a son”) with Psalm 2, the same pairing that appears in Hebrews 1:5. Another Qumran text, the Testimonia (4Q175), simply lists a series of messianic and prophetic passages one after another. Hebrews thus uses a recognized Jewish way of arguing from Scripture, and some of its texts were already read messianically by Jews before Christ. What is new is the identification of the Son of these texts with Jesus, and the boldness with which texts addressed to God are applied to him (1:8, 10–12).

Most of the quotations follow the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament used by Greek-speaking Jews and by the early church. In several places the Greek differs from the Hebrew text preserved by the Masoretes (the medieval Jewish scholars whose text underlies our Hebrew Bibles). The notes below set out the Hebrew, the Greek, and how the author’s reading relates to the meaning of the original. As will be seen, the Christological point never rests on a mere peculiarity of translation; in each case it is grounded in the meaning of the passage within the whole witness of Scripture.

Historical Background: Why the Angels Mattered

Why should a Christian preacher spend a whole chapter proving that Christ is greater than the angels? The answer lies in the place angels held in Jewish belief in the first century.

Angels and the law. The Old Testament describes the Lord coming at Sinai with “myriads of holy ones” (Deut 33:2; compare Ps 68:17). Jewish tradition drew from this the belief that angels were present at, and took part in, the giving of the law. The New Testament reflects this belief without disputing it: Stephen tells the council that Israel “received the law as ordained by angels” (Acts 7:53; compare 7:38), and Paul says the law “was ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator” (Gal 3:19). Hebrews itself calls the Sinai law “the word spoken through angels” (2:2). The Jewish historian Josephus has Herod the Great tell his troops that the Jews learned their most excellent doctrines and holiest laws through messengers from God (Ant. 15.136), and the book of Jubilees presents an angel of the presence dictating the history of Israel to Moses on the mountain. In this setting, the angels stood for the glory and authority of the old covenant revelation. To show that the Son is far above them is to show that the new revelation is far above the old.

Angels in Second Temple speculation. In the centuries before Christ, Jewish interest in angels grew considerably. The book of Daniel names Gabriel and Michael and speaks of Michael as the “prince” who stands for Israel (Dan 10:13, 21; 12:1). 1 Enoch lists the names and duties of archangels and describes vast heavenly hierarchies. Tobit introduces Raphael, “one of the seven holy angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord” (Tob 12:15). Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice depict angels as priests serving in the heavenly sanctuary; the War Scroll (1QM) foresees Michael exalted among the heavenly beings in the final battle; and the text known as 11QMelchizedek portrays Melchizedek as an exalted heavenly figure who executes God’s judgment. Philo of Alexandria could even call the divine Logos God’s “firstborn” and the eldest of the angels, “as it were an archangel.” In such a world, a Jewish believer might be tempted to think of Jesus as one more exalted heavenly being, perhaps the greatest of the angels, but still one of them. Hebrews 1 rules that out decisively: the angels worship the Son (1:6); they are creatures who are “made” (1:7); the Son is addressed as God and Creator (1:8, 10).

Was angel worship a problem in this church? Some scholars have suggested that the readers were attracted to angel veneration, as the Colossians apparently were (Col 2:18), or that they had been influenced by the kind of Judaism reflected in the Qumran scrolls. Yigael Yadin, a leading early scholar of the scrolls, proposed that Hebrews was written to converts from a Qumran-like background. The text of Hebrews, however, gives no direct evidence of angel worship among the readers. The connection the author himself draws is with the giving of the law (2:2). The most natural reading is that the angels represent the old covenant revelation in its glory, and the author’s point is that the word spoken in the Son is greater still. This also explains why the warning that follows (2:1–4) argues from the lesser to the greater: if the word spoken through angels was binding, how much more the word spoken by the Lord.

Verse by Verse

1:5

For to which of the angels did he ever say, “You are my Son; today I have begotten you”? And again, “I will be to him a Father, and he will be to me a Son”?

The little word γάρ gar, “for,” shows that what follows is the proof of 1:4. The Son’s name is more excellent than the angels’ names, for God has said things to him that he never said to any of them. The author makes his case with a rhetorical question: Τίνι … εἶπέν ποτε τῶν ἀγγέλων Tini … eipen pote tōn angelōn, “To which of the angels did he ever say …?” The word ποτε pote, “ever, at any time,” sweeps across the whole of Scripture. The expected answer is: to none. The subject of “said” is God, though he is not named; the whole chapter assumes that the God who “has spoken” (1:1–2) is the speaker in these texts.

It is true that the Old Testament occasionally calls angels collectively “sons of God” (Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7) or “sons of the mighty” (Ps 29:1, בְּנֵי אֵלִים bene elim). But that general designation of heavenly beings as belonging to God’s court is very different from what the author has in view. Never is any single angel singled out and addressed by God with the words “You are my Son,” installed as king, and promised the nations as an inheritance. That is said to one alone.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 2:7

Hebrew: אֲסַפְּרָה אֶל חֹק יְהוָה אָמַר אֵלַי בְּנִי אַתָּה אֲנִי הַיּוֹם יְלִדְתִּיךָ — asapperah el choq YHWH amar elay beni attah ani hayyom yelidtikha, “I will tell of the decree of the Lord: he said to me, ‘You are my son; I today have begotten you.’”

Hebrews quotes the Greek, Υἱός μου εἶ σύ, ἐγὼ σήμερον γεγέννηκά σε Huios mou ei sy, egō sēmeron gegennēka se, which renders the Hebrew exactly. In both languages the pronoun “I” is emphatic: it is God himself who declares the relationship. The Greek verb γεγέννηκα gegennēka, “I have begotten,” is perfect tense, stressing a settled and lasting relationship.

Psalm 2 is a royal psalm. It pictures the nations and their rulers conspiring “against the Lord and against his anointed” (Ps 2:2, עַל־יְהוָה וְעַל־מְשִׁיחוֹ al-YHWH ve’al-meshicho; the word mashiach, “anointed one,” is the source of “Messiah”). God laughs at their plotting and declares, “I have installed my king on Zion, my holy mountain” (2:6). Then the king himself reports the decree God spoke to him on the day of his installation: “You are my son; today I have begotten you.” In the ancient Near East, kings were often described as sons of their gods. Israel’s faith took up this language but transformed it: the Davidic king was not divine by birth, but was adopted by covenant as God’s son and representative, ruling on God’s behalf. “Today I have begotten you” is the language of coronation, the day on which the king was publicly declared and installed as God’s son.

No king of David’s line ever fulfilled the psalm’s vision of a son who would receive “the nations as your inheritance and the ends of the earth as your possession” (2:8). The psalm therefore pointed beyond every historical king toward the one in whom God’s promise would be fully realized. Jewish readers before Christ already read it messianically, as the Qumran Florilegium shows. The apostles applied it to Jesus. Paul at Pisidian Antioch says that God fulfilled the promise “by raising Jesus, as also it is written in the second psalm, ‘You are my Son, today I have begotten you’” (Acts 13:33). Paul also writes that Jesus “was declared to be the Son of God in power … by his resurrection from the dead” (Rom 1:4). Hebrews will quote Psalm 2:7 again in 5:5, where it is linked to Christ’s appointment as high priest.

Interpretive Options: When Is “Today”?

  • Eternity. Augustine, commenting on Psalm 2, took “today” to signify eternity, since in God’s eternity there is no past or future but only an abiding present. On this reading the verse describes the Son’s eternal generation from the Father.
  • The incarnation or Jesus’ baptism. Some have connected “today” with Christ’s birth or with the voice from heaven at his baptism (“You are my beloved Son,” Mark 1:11).
  • The resurrection and exaltation. “Today” is the day of the Son’s enthronement, when, having made purification for sins, he was raised and seated at God’s right hand and publicly installed as messianic King.

Evaluation: The context of Hebrews 1 points clearly to the third view. The quotation explains the name the Son “has inherited” after he “sat down at the right hand” (1:3–4), and the chain ends with the enthronement text, Psalm 110:1 (1:13). Acts 13:33 applies Psalm 2:7 to the resurrection, and Psalm 2 itself uses “begotten” as coronation language. This does not mean that Jesus became the Son only at his resurrection. Hebrews has already said that God made the ages through the Son and that the Son is the radiance of God’s glory (1:2–3); he was Son before he was enthroned. The “today” of Psalm 2 is the day the eternal Son, having become man and completed his work, was declared and installed as Son in power, as Messiah-King over all. The truth Augustine drew from the verse, that the Son is eternally from the Father, is taught elsewhere in this chapter (1:2–3, 8, 10–12), even if it is not the main point of “today.”

The second quotation, introduced by “and again” (καὶ πάλιν kai palin, the author’s standard way of adding a further text, as in 2:13 and 10:30), comes from God’s promise to David through the prophet Nathan.

Old Testament Text: 2 Samuel 7:14 (compare 1 Chronicles 17:13)

Hebrew: אֲנִי אֶהְיֶה־לּוֹ לְאָב וְהוּא יִהְיֶה־לִּי לְבֵן — ani ehyeh-lo le’av vehu yihyeh-li leven, “I will be to him a father, and he will be to me a son.”

The Greek Ἐγὼ ἔσομαι αὐτῷ εἰς πατέρα, καὶ αὐτὸς ἔσται μοι εἰς υἱόν Egō esomai autō eis patera, kai autos estai moi eis huion, literally “I will be to him for a father, and he will be to me for a son,” reproduces the Hebrew idiom closely; the preposition εἰς eis, “for, into,” mirrors the Hebrew preposition le-.

In 2 Samuel the promise continues: “When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him with the rod of men.” That clause applied to Solomon and the later kings of David’s line, who did sin. Hebrews quotes only the first half. It is worth noticing that the Chronicler, retelling the same oracle, also omits the clause about iniquity (1 Chr 17:13), already focusing on the ideal son of David. The son to whom Hebrews applies the promise is the one who is “holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners” (7:26), “tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin” (4:15).

The setting of Nathan’s oracle is rich. David wanted to build a house (a temple) for God. God replied that he would instead build a “house” (a dynasty) for David: “I will raise up your offspring after you … and I will establish his kingdom. He shall build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever” (2 Sam 7:12–13). The promise of an everlasting throne for David’s son was the root of Israel’s messianic hope, and the angel Gabriel echoed it to Mary: “the Lord God will give him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever” (Luke 1:32–33). Hebrews will soon develop the theme of the “house” as well: Christ is faithful “as a Son over his house, whose house we are” (3:6).

Together the two quotations make the point of 1:4 unmistakable. The Son stands in a relationship to God that no angel has ever been granted: God is his Father, and he is God’s Son, the heir of David’s everlasting throne. It is worth noticing, too, that the Son’s relationship to the Father becomes the ground of ours. Hebrews will say that he is “bringing many sons to glory” and “is not ashamed to call them brothers” (2:10–11), and in chapter 12 the author will remind the readers that God disciplines them “as sons” (12:5–11). What belongs to the Son by right is shared with his people by grace.

1:6

And again, when he brings the firstborn into the world, he says, “And let all God’s angels worship him.”

The third quotation moves from the Son’s relation to the Father to the angels’ relation to the Son. Not only has no angel been called “Son”; the angels themselves are commanded to worship the Son. Before looking at the quotation, we must consider the difficult introductory clause.

“When he brings the firstborn into the world.” The Greek reads ὅταν δὲ πάλιν εἰσαγάγῃ τὸν πρωτότοκον εἰς τὴν οἰκουμένην hotan de palin eisagagē ton prōtotokon eis tēn oikoumenēn, literally “but whenever again he brings in the firstborn into the inhabited world.” Three questions arise: what “again” modifies, what “the world” means, and therefore to which event the clause refers.

Word Study: πρωτότοκος prōtotokos, “firstborn”

The word literally means “first-born,” and it can refer simply to the oldest child. But in the Old Testament the firstborn son held a position of special honor, receiving a double portion of the inheritance and the leadership of the family (Deut 21:17, where the firstborn’s due is called מִשְׁפַּט הַבְּכֹרָה mishpat habbekhorah, “the right of the firstborn”). The word therefore came to denote rank and privilege as much as order of birth. God calls Israel “my son, my firstborn” (Exod 4:22, בְּנִי בְכֹרִי יִשְׂרָאֵל beni vekhori Yisra’el), a statement of Israel’s privileged status, not of its age among the nations. Most significantly, in Psalm 89, which celebrates the covenant with David, God says of the Davidic king: “I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth” (Ps 89:27; Hebrew 89:28, אַף־אָנִי בְּכוֹר אֶתְּנֵהוּ עֶלְיוֹן לְמַלְכֵי־אָרֶץ af-ani bekhor ettenehu elyon lemalkhe-arets). Here “firstborn” is explicitly a title of royal supremacy, conferred by God. Coming right after Psalm 2:7 and 2 Samuel 7:14, both Davidic texts, the title in Hebrews 1:6 carries this royal sense: the Son is the supreme heir, the one with the rights of the firstborn over all creation. It does not imply that he was created first (1:2 and 1:10 exclude that). The New Testament uses the same title of Christ as “firstborn among many brothers” (Rom 8:29) and “firstborn from the dead” (Col 1:18; Rev 1:5), and Hebrews will speak of the church as “the assembly of the firstborn” (12:23), sharing his privileges.

The verb εἰσάγω eisagō, “bring in, lead in,” is used frequently in the Greek Old Testament for God’s bringing Israel into the promised land (for example, Deut 6:10, “when the Lord your God brings you into the land”). The noun οἰκουμένη oikoumenē usually means “the inhabited world.” But Hebrews uses the same word in 2:5 with a special sense: “it was not to angels that he subjected the coming world (τὴν οἰκουμένην τὴν μέλλουσαν tēn oikoumenēn tēn mellousan), about which we are speaking.” The words “about which we are speaking” look back to chapter 1, which suggests that the “world” of 1:6 is that coming world, the realm of the Son’s rule, which has already been inaugurated by his exaltation and will be fully revealed at his return.

Interpretive Options: Which “Bringing In”?

  • The incarnation. God brought his firstborn into the world when he was born, and the angels worshiped him then (compare the angelic praise in Luke 2:13–14). On this view “again” simply introduces another quotation, as in 1:5. Many earlier interpreters took this view.
  • The second coming. The position of πάλιν palin, “again,” inside the clause, next to the verb, suggests that it modifies the verb: “when he again brings the firstborn into the world,” that is, at his return in glory, when he comes with his angels (Matt 25:31; 2 Thess 1:7). Westcott argued for this reading on grammatical grounds. The construction ὅταν hotan with the aorist subjunctive (“whenever he brings”) normally refers to a future event, which supports it.
  • The exaltation. The “bringing in” is the Son’s entrance into the heavenly realm, the “coming world” of 2:5, at his resurrection and enthronement, when he sat down at God’s right hand and all the angels were summoned to do him homage. Lane and a number of other modern commentators take this view, reading “again” as the connective that links the quotations (“And again …”).

Evaluation: The incarnation view fits poorly with the flow of the chapter, which centers on the Son’s enthronement (1:3–5, 13), and it does not explain the link with “the coming world” in 2:5. The grammatical argument for the second coming is real: Hebrews elsewhere uses “again” with verbs (5:12; 6:1, 6), and its position here is unusual for a simple connective. Yet in a chain of quotations in which “and again” has just been used as a connective (1:5) and will be used that way again (2:13; 10:30), the word can reasonably serve the same function, placed after “when” for stylistic reasons. On balance, the exaltation view fits the context best: the Son, having been begotten as King (1:5), is led into his inheritance as firstborn, and the whole heavenly court is commanded to worship him. Yet the difference between this and the second-coming view should not be overstated. Hebrews sees the Son’s enthronement and his return as two stages of one reality: he has already entered the coming world and sits at God’s right hand, and he will appear a second time to bring salvation to those who wait for him (9:28). The homage the angels give him now will be openly displayed then. Either way, the point of the verse is the same: the angels are worshipers, and the Son is the one they worship.

“And let all God’s angels worship him.” The source of this quotation is one of the most interesting textual puzzles in the New Testament’s use of the Old.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 32:43 (with Psalm 97:7)

Hebrew (Masoretic Text) of Deuteronomy 32:43: הַרְנִינוּ גוֹיִם עַמּוֹ כִּי דַם־עֲבָדָיו יִקּוֹם וְנָקָם יָשִׁיב לְצָרָיו וְכִפֶּר אַדְמָתוֹ עַמּוֹ — harninu goyim ammo ki dam-avadav yiqqom venaqam yashiv letsarav vekhipper admato ammo, “Shout for joy, O nations, over his people, for he avenges the blood of his servants; he takes vengeance on his adversaries and makes atonement for his land, his people.” The Masoretic Text has no line about worship.

The Septuagint (Greek) version of this verse is considerably longer. It begins, “Rejoice, O heavens, together with him, and let all the sons of God worship him; rejoice, O nations, with his people, and let all the angels of God be strong in him.” In the form of the Song of Moses copied among the “Odes” appended to the Psalms in some Greek manuscripts, the line reads “and let all the angels of God worship him,” exactly as in Hebrews.

For a long time scholars could not tell whether the longer Greek reading reflected an ancient Hebrew text or was an expansion by the translators. The Dead Sea Scrolls settled the question. A Hebrew manuscript of Deuteronomy from Qumran Cave 4, known as 4QDeutq, contains the longer form. Its opening lines read, in translation, “Rejoice, O heavens, with him, and bow down to him, all you gods (elohim).” This shows that the line Hebrews quotes goes back to a Hebrew text of Deuteronomy older than the Masoretic Text in this verse. The Masoretic Text appears to have lost or omitted the line, perhaps because a command to “gods” to bow down seemed problematic.

Hebrew of Psalm 97:7: יֵבֹשׁוּ כָּל־עֹבְדֵי פֶסֶל הַמִּתְהַלְלִים בָּאֱלִילִים הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ־לוֹ כָּל־אֱלֹהִים — yevoshu kol-ovde fesel hammithalelim ba’elilim hishtachavu-lo kol-elohim, “Let all who serve carved images be put to shame, who boast in worthless idols; bow down to him, all gods!” The Septuagint (Ps 96:7) renders the last line, “Worship him, all his angels.” The Hebrew אֱלֹהִים elohim, “gods,” is here understood as the heavenly beings, the angels, an interpretation the Greek translators adopted in several places (compare Ps 8:5, quoted in Heb 2:7).

So the words Hebrews quotes correspond to an ancient Hebrew reading of Deuteronomy 32:43 attested at Qumran, and their thought is identical to the Hebrew of Psalm 97:7. Whether the author had Deuteronomy or the Psalm chiefly in mind, the reading he cites is solidly grounded in the Hebrew Scriptures.

The setting of the quotation makes it even more striking. The Song of Moses (Deut 32) is a passionate denunciation of idolatry. Israel “sacrificed to demons, not God, to gods they had not known” (32:17), and the Lord declares, “See now that I, I am he, and there is no god beside me” (32:39). The song ends with the heavenly beings summoned to bow before the Lord as he comes to vindicate his people. Psalm 97 likewise celebrates the Lord’s kingship and puts idol-worshipers to shame. In both texts, the “him” whom the heavenly beings worship is the Lord, the God of Israel. Hebrews applies these words to the Son.

The verb προσκυνησάτωσαν proskynēsatōsan is a third person aorist imperative of προσκυνέω proskyneō, “bow down, prostrate oneself, worship”: “let them worship.” It is a command from God to the angels. In Scripture, worship of this kind belongs to God alone. When Satan asked Jesus to bow down to him, Jesus answered from Deuteronomy, “You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve” (Matt 4:10, quoting Deut 6:13). When John fell down to worship the angel who showed him the visions of Revelation, the angel forbade him: “Do not do that! I am a fellow servant with you … Worship God” (Rev 22:8–9; compare 19:10). The angels refuse worship because they are creatures. Yet here God himself commands all his angels to worship the Son. That command would be a summons to idolatry if the Son were anything less than God. The verse therefore makes a claim far greater than that the Son outranks the angels: he receives the worship that belongs to the Lord alone.

For the hearers, tempted to retreat to the safety of the old ways, the verse places Jesus at the center of all true worship. The angels whom Israel revered as the attendants of Sinai are themselves bowed before him. No one who worships the God of Israel can rightly refuse to worship his Son (compare John 5:23, “that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father”).

1:7

And of the angels he says, “He makes his angels winds, and his ministers a flame of fire.”

Verses 7–12 form a single contrast built on the Greek particles μέν men (1:7) and δέ de (1:8), which work like the English “on the one hand … on the other hand.” “On the one hand, of the angels he says …; on the other hand, to the Son …” One quotation is given for the angels and two for the Son, and the imbalance is itself part of the message.

The preposition πρός pros can mean “to” or “with reference to.” Psalm 104 is not addressed to the angels; it speaks about them, so here pros means “of” or “concerning.” In 1:8, as we will see, the psalm quoted actually addresses the Son directly.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 104:4

Hebrew: עֹשֶׂה מַלְאָכָיו רוּחוֹת מְשָׁרְתָיו אֵשׁ לֹהֵט — oseh mal’akhav ruchot mesharetav esh lohet, literally “making his messengers winds, his ministers a flaming fire,” or “making winds his messengers, flaming fire his ministers.”

The Hebrew can be read in two ways, because two nouns stand side by side after the verb “making,” and either could be the object. Many modern translations read, “He makes the winds his messengers, flames of fire his servants,” understanding the verse as a description of God using natural forces to do his bidding. The context supports that reading: the previous verse says God “makes the clouds his chariot” and “walks on the wings of the wind” (Ps 104:3). But the word order of 104:4 (with “his messengers” standing first, just as “clouds” stands first in 104:3) allows the other reading as well: “He makes his messengers winds.” The Septuagint (Ps 103:4) takes it this way, and Hebrews follows the Septuagint.

Two Hebrew words in the verse have double meanings, and so do their Greek equivalents. מַלְאָךְ mal’akh means both “messenger” and “angel” (so also Greek ἄγγελος angelos), and רוּחַ ruach means both “wind” and “spirit” (so also Greek πνεῦμα pneuma). The Greek reading is therefore a legitimate understanding of the Hebrew, not a distortion of it. And on either reading the essential point that Hebrews draws out remains: the angels belong to the realm of created things that God “makes” and uses, alongside wind and fire, for his purposes.

Three features of the quotation serve the author’s argument.

First, the angels are made. The participle ὁ ποιῶν ho poiōn, “the one who makes,” places them firmly among the creatures. The Son, by contrast, is the one through whom God “made” the ages (1:2, the same verb). Jewish tradition fully agreed that angels were created; the book of Jubilees, for example, lists the angels, including angels of the spirits of fire and of the winds, among the works of the first day of creation.

Second, the angels are servants. The word λειτουργός leitourgos, “minister,” was used in the Greek Old Testament for priests and Levites serving in the sanctuary, and more broadly for anyone performing a public service. The angels are God’s attendants, carrying out his will. Hebrews will pick up this word in 1:14 (“ministering spirits,” λειτουργικὰ πνεύματα leitourgika pneumata), and later will use it of Christ himself as “a minister of the sanctuary and the true tabernacle” (8:2). The difference is that the angels minister as servants, while Christ ministers as the Son enthroned at God’s right hand.

Third, the angels are changeable. God “makes” them winds and fire, that is, he gives them whatever form his purposes require. Wind and fire are images of power, but also of what is fleeting and variable; wind blows and is gone, a flame flickers and dies. Scripture shows angels appearing in varied forms: in a flame of fire in the bush (Exod 3:2), as men (Gen 18:2; Judg 13:6), with fiery chariots and horses (2 Kgs 6:17). This is precisely the contrast the following verses draw. The angels’ forms change at God’s command, but the Son’s throne is “forever and ever” (1:8), and while heaven and earth will be changed like clothing, “you are the same, and your years will not come to an end” (1:12).

None of this belittles the angels. They are mighty, holy, and glorious, and Hebrews will speak of “myriads of angels in festal gathering” in the heavenly Jerusalem (12:22). But they are servants. To give them, or anything that represented the old covenant they mediated, the place that belongs to the Son would be to confuse the servant with the Lord.

1:8

But to the Son: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, and the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.

Now comes the other side of the contrast: πρὸς δὲ τὸν υἱόν pros de ton huion, “but to the Son.” Here the preposition pros has its full sense, “to,” because the words of Psalm 45 are spoken in direct address: “your throne,” “your kingdom.” Where the angels were described as changeable servants, the Son is addressed as the eternal King, and, astonishingly, as God.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 45:6 (Hebrew 45:7)

Hebrew: כִּסְאֲךָ אֱלֹהִים עוֹלָם וָעֶד שֵׁבֶט מִישֹׁר שֵׁבֶט מַלְכוּתֶךָ — kis’akha elohim olam va’ed shevet mishor shevet malkhutekha, “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever; a scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.”

The Greek that Hebrews quotes, Ὁ θρόνος σου ὁ θεὸς εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα τοῦ αἰῶνος Ho thronos sou ho theos eis ton aiōna tou aiōnos, follows the Hebrew closely. “Forever and ever” renders the Hebrew עוֹלָם וָעֶד olam va’ed, “forever and perpetually,” and the Greek eis ton aiōna tou aiōnos, literally “to the age of the age,” an emphatic way of saying “without end.” The word for “scepter,” Hebrew שֵׁבֶט shevet, Greek ῥάβδος rhabdos, means a rod or staff, the symbol of royal authority.

Psalm 45 is a royal wedding song. The superscription calls it “a song of love.” The poet announces, “I address my verses to the king” (45:1), and praises him: “You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever” (45:2). He calls on the king to ride out victoriously “for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness” (45:4), and then, in the verse Hebrews quotes, addresses him with words that have startled readers ever since: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.” The psalm goes on to speak to the royal bride and ends with the promise that the king’s name will be remembered in all generations and that the peoples will praise him “forever and ever” (45:17).

The key question, in both the psalm and in Hebrews, is how to understand the word “God” (Hebrew אֱלֹהִים elohim; Greek ὁ θεός ho theos). Is the king being addressed as “God,” or does “God” function in some other way in the sentence?

Interpretive Options: “Your Throne, O God”

  • Vocative (“O God”). The word “God” is a direct address to the one spoken to: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.” In Greek, a noun in the nominative case with the article, such as ὁ θεός ho theos, is often used as a form of address; the tax collector prays ὁ θεός, ἱλάσθητί μοι ho theos, hilasthēti moi, “O God, be merciful to me” (Luke 18:13), and Thomas confesses Jesus as ὁ κύριός μου καὶ ὁ θεός μου ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou, “my Lord and my God” (John 20:28). This is the reading of almost all ancient versions and of most modern translations and commentators. Murray J. Harris, in his detailed study of the New Testament passages that call Jesus “God,” concludes that ho theos in Hebrews 1:8 is vocative, and that the Hebrew of Psalm 45 should also be read as an address.
  • Subject or predicate (“God is your throne” / “Your throne is God”). Grammatically, the Greek could also be read, “God is your throne forever and ever,” meaning that the king’s rule is grounded in God. Westcott inclined to this reading, partly because he found it difficult to believe that the psalm addressed a human king as “God.”
  • Adjectival (“Your divine throne”). Some translations of the psalm (for example, the Revised Standard Version) render the Hebrew “Your divine throne endures forever,” or “Your throne is like God’s throne.” This avoids calling the king “God” but requires an unusual construction in Hebrew.

Evaluation: In Hebrews, the vocative reading is clearly the most natural. (1) The quotation is introduced as spoken “to the Son,” and the psalm addresses him throughout in the second person; a vocative fits that pattern. (2) The very next quotation addresses the Son as “Lord” (κύριε kyrie, a vocative form, 1:10), so the author is clearly accustomed to applying titles of God to the Son in direct address. (3) The whole chapter is driving toward the Son’s supremacy over the angels; “God is your throne” would say nothing that could not be said of a godly human king, and so would not serve the argument. (4) The idea of God himself being someone’s throne is without parallel in Scripture, whereas the address “O God” is natural in a psalm. (5) In the Hebrew of Psalm 45:6 the most straightforward reading is likewise “Your throne, O God.”

How could a psalm originally sung at the wedding of an Israelite king address him as “God”? The Old Testament occasionally uses elohim in a derived sense of human beings who represent God: the Lord tells Moses, “See, I have made you God to Pharaoh” (Exod 7:1, נְתַתִּיךָ אֱלֹהִים לְפַרְעֹה netattikha elohim lefar‘oh), and Psalm 82:6 addresses Israel’s judges as “gods.” The Davidic king, as God’s adopted son and representative (Ps 2:7; 2 Sam 7:14), could be addressed in exalted court language. But the psalm’s language strains beyond any ordinary king. It speaks of a throne that lasts “forever and ever” and of a name that the peoples will praise “forever and ever.” Isaiah, too, foretold a son of David whose name would be called “Mighty God” (Isa 9:6; Hebrew 9:5, אֵל גִּבּוֹר el gibbor), and whose throne and kingdom would have no end (Isa 9:7). Jewish interpreters themselves came to read Psalm 45 as speaking of the Messiah; the Aramaic paraphrase of the Psalms (the Targum) interprets the king of the psalm as the King Messiah. What was said in a partial, representative sense of David’s sons finds its full and literal truth only in the Son of whom Hebrews speaks, the one who is himself “the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of his essential being” (1:3).

It is important to see that the psalm itself, and Hebrews with it, maintains a distinction even as it applies the title “God” to the Son. In the very next verse the one addressed as “God” is anointed by “God, your God” (1:9). The Son is God, and yet he is distinct from the Father, who anoints him. Here, in seed form, is the mystery that the church would later confess in the doctrine of the Trinity: the Son is truly God, not a second god or a lesser god, and yet he is not the Father. Hebrews does not explain the mystery; it simply sets it before us as the testimony of Scripture.

Textual Note: “Your Kingdom” or “His Kingdom”?

At the end of the verse a few very early and important manuscripts (including the papyrus P46 and the great codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus) read “the scepter of his kingdom” (αὐτοῦ autou) instead of “of your kingdom” (σου sou). If “his” were original, it might favor the reading “God is your throne … and the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of his [God’s] kingdom.” Most textual critics, however, including the committee that prepared the standard Greek text (whose decision Metzger records), judge “your” to be original. It agrees with the Septuagint and the Hebrew of the psalm, it fits the second-person address that runs through the whole quotation, and “his” is difficult to make sense of in context. The SBL Greek New Testament, followed here, reads “your.”

“The scepter of uprightness.” The second line describes the character of the Son’s rule. The Greek εὐθύτης euthytēs, “straightness, uprightness,” renders the Hebrew מִישֹׁר mishor, which means “level ground” and so “fairness, equity.” His scepter is not crooked; his government is perfectly just. This fulfills the hope of the prophets for a king who would “reign in righteousness” (Isa 32:1) and judge the poor with equity (Isa 11:4), the “righteous Branch” of David (Jer 23:5). Hebrews will later describe Melchizedek, the type of Christ, as “king of righteousness” (7:2). For a congregation that had suffered unjustly (10:32–34), it was a comfort to know that the throne of the universe is occupied by one whose rule is straight and whose kingdom cannot be shaken (12:28).

1:9

You loved righteousness and hated lawlessness; therefore God, your God, anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions.”

Old Testament Text: Psalm 45:7 (Hebrew 45:8)

Hebrew: אָהַבְתָּ צֶּדֶק וַתִּשְׂנָא רֶשַׁע עַל־כֵּן מְשָׁחֲךָ אֱלֹהִים אֱלֹהֶיךָ שֶׁמֶן שָׂשׂוֹן מֵחֲבֵרֶיךָ — ahavta tsedeq vattisna resha‘ al-ken meshachakha elohim elohekha shemen sason mechaverekha, “You love righteousness and hate wickedness; therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness above your companions.”

The Greek follows the Hebrew closely. The Hebrew רֶשַׁע resha‘, “wickedness,” is rendered by ἀνομία anomia, “lawlessness,” that is, the rejection of God’s law. The Hebrew verb מָשַׁח mashach, “anoint,” is the root of mashiach, “Messiah”; the Greek verb χρίω chriō, “anoint,” is the root of Χριστός Christos, “Christ.” To be anointed is to be made “Messiah,” “Christ.”

The second half of the quotation from Psalm 45 explains why the King has been exalted. He “loved righteousness and hated lawlessness.” The Greek verbs ἠγάπησας ēgapēsas, “you loved,” and ἐμίσησας emisēsas, “you hated,” are aorists. In the psalm they describe the king’s settled character. In Hebrews, applied to the Son, they naturally sum up the whole course of his earthly life: his perfect devotion to God’s will. The author will later describe that life in moving terms. Christ entered the world saying, “Behold, I have come to do your will, O God” (10:7, quoting Ps 40); he “learned obedience through what he suffered” (5:8); he was “tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin” (4:15); he is “holy, innocent, undefiled” (7:26). Love of righteousness and hatred of lawlessness are two sides of one moral perfection. He did not merely avoid evil; he loved what is right, and his love for the right included a holy hatred of what destroys it.

“Therefore” (διὰ τοῦτο dia touto, “on account of this”) links the Son’s righteousness to his anointing and exaltation. The pattern is the same as in Philippians 2:8–9: he “became obedient to the point of death … therefore God highly exalted him.” This does not mean that the Son earned a divinity he did not already possess (1:8 has just called him God). It means that as the incarnate Son, the Messiah who lived and died in perfect obedience, he was raised and enthroned as the reward and vindication of his obedience. In the Old Testament, kings were anointed with oil as a sign of God’s choice and of the gift of his Spirit; when Samuel anointed David, “the Spirit of the Lord rushed upon David from that day forward” (1 Sam 16:13). Jesus was anointed with the Spirit at the beginning of his ministry (Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38), and in his exaltation he has been publicly installed as the Christ, the anointed King over all.

“God, your God.” The Hebrew אֱלֹהִים אֱלֹהֶיךָ elohim elohekha, “God, your God,” occurs elsewhere in the same part of the Psalter (Ps 50:7, “I am God, your God”). Psalms 42–83 frequently use elohim, “God,” where other psalms would use the divine name; “God, your God” in these psalms is equivalent to “the Lord your God.” The Greek ὁ θεός, ὁ θεός σου ho theos, ho theos sou is most naturally read as the subject of the verb: “God, your God, anointed you.” (It is grammatically possible to read the first ho theos as another vocative, “Therefore, O God, your God anointed you,” and a few interpreters take it that way, but the point is the same either way.) The one who has just been addressed as “God” (1:8) has a God who anoints him. The Son, in his role as the incarnate Messiah, relates to the Father as his God, just as Jesus said after his resurrection, “I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (John 20:17). Hebrews holds together, without embarrassment, the Son’s full deity and his true humanity.

“The oil of gladness.” The anointing in Psalm 45 is not described as the solemn oil of coronation but as שֶׁמֶן שָׂשׂוֹן shemen sason, “the oil of joy,” the festive oil used at feasts and celebrations (compare Ps 23:5; 104:15). It suits a wedding song. Isaiah uses the same phrase in the prophecy Jesus read in the synagogue at Nazareth: the Anointed One is sent to give the mourners of Zion “the oil of gladness instead of mourning” (Isa 61:3). The Greek ἔλαιον ἀγαλλιάσεως elaion agalliaseōs means “oil of exultation, of exuberant joy.” Hebrews will tell us that it was “for the joy set before him” that Jesus “endured the cross, despising the shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God” (12:2). His exaltation is the joyful feast that crowns his suffering, and he shares that joy with those for whom he suffered.

Interpretive Options: Who Are the “Companions”?

  • The angels. Since the whole chapter compares the Son with the angels, some interpreters take “your companions” as the angels, over whom the Son is exalted.
  • The people of God. In the psalm, the companions are the king’s fellow kings or the members of his court. Hebrews uses the same Greek word, μέτοχος metochos, “partner, sharer, companion,” four more times, always of believers: they are “partakers of a heavenly calling” (3:1), “partners of Christ” (3:14), “partakers of the Holy Spirit” (6:4), and sharers in the discipline that all true sons receive (12:8).

Evaluation: The author’s own use of metochos strongly suggests that he hears “companions” as a reference to those who share in Christ, his “brothers” (2:11–12). The Son is anointed “beyond” them, that is, in preeminence over them, as the firstborn among many brothers; yet they are truly his companions, partners in his joy and his inheritance (1:14; 3:14). This reading also anticipates the turn of chapter 2, where the exalted Son is shown to be bound to his people in solidarity. The angels are not excluded from the Son’s superiority, but the term points chiefly to the redeemed.
