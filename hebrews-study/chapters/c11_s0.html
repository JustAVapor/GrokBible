Hebrews 11

The Faith That Pleases God

Hebrews 11 is the best-loved chapter of the letter and one of the best-known in the whole Bible. Christians have long called it the “roll call of faith” or the chapter of the “cloud of witnesses,” borrowing the phrase with which the author turns from it to exhort his hearers: “since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us” (12:1). Yet it would be a mistake to read it as a free-standing poem in praise of faith. It is a link in a chain of argument. At the end of chapter 10 the preacher quoted Habakkuk: “my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him” (10:38; Hab 2:3–4). He then drew the line between two kinds of people: “we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39). Chapter 11 answers the obvious question: what does that kind of faith look like? It shows the hearers a long line of men and women who “lived by faith,” who did not shrink back, and who pleased God. And chapter 12 then applies the lesson: surrounded by these witnesses, “let us run with endurance the race set before us, looking to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:1–2). The chapter therefore stands between a warning (10:26–39) and a summons (12:1–13), and its purpose is pastoral: to stiffen the resolve of weary believers by showing them the company they keep.

The chapter’s most striking feature is its repeated opening word. Eighteen times a new sentence begins with πίστει pistei, “by faith” (the dative case of the noun “faith,” expressing the means or manner by which something was done): by faith Abel offered, by faith Enoch was taken, by faith Noah built, by faith Abraham obeyed, and so on through Moses, the Israelites at the Red Sea, the fall of Jericho, and Rahab. Ancient teachers of rhetoric called this device anaphora, the repetition of a word at the head of successive clauses. Whatever else differs among these people—their era, their circumstances, their failures—one thing is the same in all of them. The faith that God commends is not one virtue among many; it is the root of every act the chapter records.

The chapter belongs to a recognized ancient form: the catalogue of examples, or exempla. Greek and Roman moralists taught virtue by parading famous models before their pupils, and a formal speech in praise of a person or a quality (an encomium) often marched through illustrious examples one by one. Jewish writers of the Second Temple period used the same form to rehearse the story of Israel. Sirach 44–50 opens, “Let us now praise famous men, our fathers in their generations,” and moves from Enoch and Noah through Abraham, Moses, the judges, kings and prophets, down to the high priest Simon. In 1 Maccabees 2:51–60 the dying Mattathias urges his sons to “remember the deeds of the fathers” and lists Abraham, Joseph, Phinehas, Joshua, Caleb, David, Elijah, the three young men in the furnace, and Daniel. In 4 Maccabees 16:20–23 the mother of the seven martyred brothers strengthens her sons by recalling Abraham’s readiness to offer Isaac, Daniel among the lions, and the three in the fire. Wisdom of Solomon 10 traces how divine Wisdom rescued the righteous from Adam to the exodus.

But Hebrews bends the genre to its own purpose. Sirach praises “famous men” and the glory they won; Hebrews praises no human achievement as such. Its heroes are commended because they trusted God when they could not see, and the one who gives the verdict is God himself: “by it the elders received testimony” (11:2). Again and again the chapter stresses what these people did not receive. They “died in faith, not having received the promises” (11:13); “all these, though they received testimony through their faith, did not receive what was promised” (11:39). The list does not climax in a human hero but in an open door: God “had foreseen something better for us, so that apart from us they should not be made perfect” (11:40). The real climax comes only at 12:2, with Jesus, who does not appear in the list because he is not one more example of faith but its pioneer and perfecter.

Several key words run through the chapter and tie it to the rest of the letter. Watch for the verb μαρτυρέω martyreō, “bear witness, testify,” used in the passive for receiving God’s approving testimony (11:2, 4, 5, 39), which prepares for the “witnesses” (μάρτυρες martyres) of 12:1. Watch for the language of “promise” (ἐπαγγελία epangelia), “inheritance” and “heir,” “reward” (11:6, 26; compare 10:35), “seeing” and “not seeing,” the “city” with foundations (11:10, 16; compare 12:22; 13:14), and the “better” things that run through the whole letter (11:16, 35, 40). Watch, finally, for the pattern of the lives themselves: almost every example involves an act of costly obedience performed with eyes fixed on a future that God had promised but not yet given. That is exactly the situation of the hearers, who had once “joyfully accepted the plundering of your possessions, knowing that you yourselves have a better possession and an abiding one” (10:34), and who now needed endurance to receive what was promised (10:36).
