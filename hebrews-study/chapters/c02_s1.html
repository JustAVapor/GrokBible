Do Not Drift Away (2:1–4)

Translation

1For this reason we must pay far closer attention to the things we have heard, so that we do not drift away. 2For if the word spoken through angels proved firm, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense, 3how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation—a salvation that began by being spoken through the Lord and was confirmed to us by those who heard, 4while God bore witness along with them by signs and wonders and various mighty works and distributions of the Holy Spirit according to his will?

Setting and Structure

These four verses are the first pause in the sermon. For fourteen verses the author has piled up Scripture to show that the Son is superior to the angels. Now he turns, with “for this reason” (2:1), to say what that means for his hearers. This rhythm of teaching followed by urgent application will continue throughout the book; the exhortations are not interruptions of the argument but its point. The author does not write theology for its own sake. He writes so that a weary congregation will hold on.

The passage is a single long sentence in Greek after the opening exhortation, and its logic is simple and forceful:

  • The command (2:1): pay closer attention to what you have heard, lest you drift away.
  • The reason, stated as a comparison (2:2–3a): if the lesser word (the law, given through angels) was binding and its violation punished, how will we escape if we neglect the greater word (the salvation spoken through the Lord)?
  • The greatness of that word (2:3b–4): it began with the Lord himself; it was confirmed by eyewitnesses; and God added his own testimony through miracles and gifts of the Spirit.

The argument is what Jewish teachers called qal vachomer, “light and heavy”: what is true in a lesser case is all the more true in a greater one. Jesus used the same kind of reasoning (Matt 6:30; 7:11), and Hebrews returns to it in each of its warnings (3:1–6 with 3:7–19; 9:13–14; 10:28–29; 12:25). In fact 12:25 forms a striking bookend to this passage: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking. For if they did not escape when they refused the one who warned them on earth, much less shall we, if we turn away from the one who warns from heaven.” The two passages share the same vocabulary of speaking, hearing, and escaping, and the same logic. The whole sermon is framed by the summons to listen to God who speaks.

Notice also how carefully the passage is tied to chapter 1. The author began with “God, having spoken (λαλήσας lalēsas) … spoke (ἐλάλησεν elalēsen) to us in a Son” (1:1–2). Now he speaks of “the word spoken (λαληθείς lalētheis) through angels” (2:2) and the salvation “spoken (λαλεῖσθαι laleisthai) through the Lord” (2:3). The angels of chapter 1 reappear, now as the mediators of the old revelation at Sinai. And the “salvation” that the angels serve (1:14) becomes the “so great a salvation” that must not be neglected (2:3). Having shown who the Son is, the author now shows why it matters: the greater the speaker, the greater the responsibility of those who hear.

The historical setting presses close here. According to 2:3 the readers had not heard Jesus in person; they belonged to a generation that received the gospel from eyewitnesses. Elsewhere we learn that they had once stood firm under public abuse, imprisonment of fellow believers, and the confiscation of property (10:32–34), but that they had since become “sluggish” in hearing (5:11) and some were neglecting to meet together (10:25). The danger in view is not primarily heresy or open rebellion. It is the gradual loss of attention that comes from weariness, social pressure, and the sheer passage of time. Second-generation communities in every age know this danger well. What once was fresh becomes familiar, and what is familiar is easily neglected.

The passage also introduces a theme that will grow throughout the book: the danger of falling under the judgment of God. Hebrews speaks with great warmth about the mercy of Christ (2:17–18; 4:14–16), but it never softens the reality of judgment for those who turn away (6:4–8; 10:26–31; 12:25–29). Both belong to the same message. Precisely because the salvation is “so great,” neglecting it is so serious.

Verse by Verse

2:1

For this reason we must pay far closer attention to the things we have heard, so that we do not drift away.

“For this reason” (Διὰ τοῦτο dia touto) reaches back over the whole of chapter 1. Because God has spoken his final word in a Son, and because that Son is the Creator, heir, and enthroned King who outranks every angel, the hearers must listen. The logic is that of Jesus at the Transfiguration, where the Father’s voice said of the Son, “Listen to him” (Mark 9:7). The supremacy of the speaker determines the seriousness of the hearing.

“We must” translates δεῖ dei, “it is necessary.” The word expresses obligation rooted in the way things are, not merely advice. The author says “we,” not “you.” Though he will speak sternly, he places himself under the same obligation as his hearers. This pastoral “we” recurs in nearly every warning passage (3:6, 14; 4:1, 11, 14; 10:22–26; 12:1, 25, 28). The preacher does not stand above his congregation; he stands with them before God.

“Far closer” is the adverb περισσοτέρως perissoterōs, “more abundantly, all the more.” It is a comparative: the attention due to the Son’s word exceeds even the attention Israel owed to the law. The verb is προσέχειν prosechein (from προσέχω prosechō), literally “to hold toward,” that is, to turn the mind toward something and keep it there. It is the ordinary word for paying heed (Acts 8:6; 16:14, where the Lord opened Lydia’s heart “to pay attention” to Paul’s words). In Greek seafaring language the same verb could also be used of bringing a ship in toward land and holding it there. Whether or not the author intended that nuance, it fits strikingly with the verb at the end of the verse.

“The things we have heard” (τοῖς ἀκουσθεῖσιν tois akoustheisin) is a passive participle: literally “the things that were heard.” The author does not mean a vague set of religious ideas but the definite message that God spoke in his Son and that the readers received at their conversion (2:3). Hebrews is a book about hearing: “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts” (3:7, 15; 4:7). Faith comes through the message heard (compare 4:2, “the word they heard did not profit them, because it was not united by faith with those who listened”).

Word Study: παραρυῶμεν pararyōmen

The final verb is the aorist subjunctive of παραρρέω pararreō, “to flow past, to slip away, to drift.” It is built from para, “alongside, past,” and rheō, “to flow.” In ordinary Greek it described something that slips out of place without anyone noticing: water flowing by, a thing slipping from the memory, a ring sliding off a finger. Applied to a boat, it pictures a vessel carried past its harbor by the current because no one held it to its mooring.

The word appears only here in the New Testament. In the Greek translation of Proverbs it is used in a father’s plea to his son not to let go of wisdom (Prov 3:21). The Hebrew of that verse reads בְּנִי אַל־יָלֻזוּ מֵעֵינֶיךָ beni al-yaluzu me‘eineka, “My son, let them not slip away from your eyes.” In Hebrew it is the father’s teachings that might slip away from the son; in the Greek it is the son who might slip away. Hebrews uses the word in the latter, more personal sense: the danger is not only that the message slips from us, but that we slip away from it.

The aorist tense views the drifting as a whole, as an outcome: “lest we end up having drifted away.” The construction with μήποτε mēpote, “lest, so that … not,” expresses a real danger to be guarded against. Nothing in the verb suggests a dramatic act of rebellion. Drifting requires no effort at all. One need only stop paying attention.

The two verbs together form a single picture. To “pay attention” is to keep one’s boat moored; to “drift” is what happens when one does not. The current in the readers’ case was strong. They faced social pressure, the loss of property, and the temptation to return to the visible, honored, ancestral forms of worship that the old covenant offered (10:32–39; 13:9–14). No one need decide to abandon Christ in order to lose him; one need only stop holding fast. Later the author will offer the counterpart image: the hope set before us is “an anchor of the soul, sure and firm” (6:19). The cure for drifting is to be anchored in Christ himself.

Interpreters from the early church onward have drawn out the point that the danger here lies in neglect rather than in some great act of wickedness: those who drift are carried off by the current while they are not watching, and the word may be lost by carelessness as surely as by open contempt. The application is plain for every generation. Faith is sustained by attention: by hearing God’s word, by meeting together (10:25), by daily mutual exhortation (3:13). Where these are neglected, drift is not a possibility but a certainty.

2:2

For if the word spoken through angels proved firm, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense,

Verse 2 opens the comparison that explains the urgency of verse 1 (“for,” γάρ gar). The “if” (εἰ ei) introduces a condition the author assumes to be true; it has the force of “since, as we all know.” No Jewish listener would dispute it.

“The word spoken through angels” is the law given at Sinai. The author does not need to explain this to his readers, which tells us that the tradition was widely known. The Old Testament accounts of Sinai speak of fire, cloud, thunder, and the sound of a trumpet (Exod 19:16–19), but they do not explicitly say that angels delivered the law. The idea grew from several poetic texts that speak of God’s heavenly host present at Sinai, and it became common in Second Temple Judaism and in the early church.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 33:2 and Psalm 68:17

In Moses’ blessing, the Hebrew reads: יְהוָה מִסִּינַי בָּא וְזָרַח מִשֵּׂעִיר לָמוֹ הוֹפִיעַ מֵהַר פָּארָן וְאָתָה מֵרִבְבֹת קֹדֶשׁ — YHWH missinai ba vezarach misse‘ir lamo hophia‘ mehar Paran ve’atah merivevot qodesh, “The Lord came from Sinai and dawned from Seir upon them; he shone forth from Mount Paran, and he came from the myriads of holy ones.” The next words are textually difficult; the Masoretic tradition reads them as “from his right hand a fiery law for them.” The Greek translation of Deuteronomy, which the readers of Hebrews would have known, renders the end of the verse so that angels are said to be with the Lord at his right hand.

Psalm 68 (Hebrew 68:18; English 68:17) likewise pictures the Lord’s arrival at Sinai with his heavenly host: רֶכֶב אֱלֹהִים רִבֹּתַיִם אַלְפֵי שִׁנְאָן אֲדֹנָי בָם סִינַי בַּקֹּדֶשׁ — rekev Elohim ribbotayim alphei shin’an, Adonai vam, Sinai baqqodesh, “The chariots of God are twice ten thousand, thousands upon thousands; the Lord is among them; Sinai is in holiness.”

From such texts Jewish tradition concluded that angels took part in the giving of the law. The New Testament repeats this understanding three times outside Hebrews: Stephen speaks of the angel who spoke to Moses on Mount Sinai (Acts 7:38) and says Israel “received the law as ordained by angels” (Acts 7:53), and Paul says the law “was ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator” (Gal 3:19). Josephus, in a speech he attributes to Herod the Great, has Herod say that the Jews learned the holiest of their laws from God “through angels” (Ant. 15.136), though some think he means human messengers there. The book of Jubilees (1:27–2:1) presents an “angel of the presence” instructing Moses on the mountain.

Why does the author mention the angels at all? Not to disparage the law. The law was God’s own word, and the author never denies it. His point is comparative. In chapter 1 he showed that the angels are servants and the Son is Lord. If a message delivered through servants carried such authority, then a message delivered by the Son must carry far more. In Galatians Paul used the angelic mediation of the law to show its subordinate place in the history of salvation (Gal 3:19–20). Hebrews uses it to show the law’s seriousness—and therefore the even greater seriousness of the gospel. The two uses do not conflict. Both treat the law as truly from God, yet as a preparatory word that points beyond itself.

That word “proved firm” (ἐγένετο βέβαιος egeneto bebaios). The adjective βέβαιος bebaios means “firm, steadfast, reliable,” and in legal and commercial Greek it described a guarantee or contract that was valid and enforceable. The word belongs to the author’s legal vocabulary: the oath of God is given “for confirmation” (βεβαίωσιν bebaiōsin, 6:16), and a will is “valid” (βεβαία bebaia) at death (9:17). In verse 3 he will use the related verb: the gospel “was confirmed” (ἐβεβαιώθη ebebaiōthē) to us. The parallel is deliberate. The old word was legally binding; the new word has been legally attested. Neither can be ignored with impunity.

Word Study: παράβασις parabasis and παρακοή parakoē

The author names two kinds of sin. παράβασις parabasis, “transgression,” is literally “a stepping beside” or “overstepping”: crossing a line that God has drawn. It describes positive acts of breaking a known command (Rom 4:15, “where there is no law, there is no transgression”). παρακοή parakoē, “disobedience,” comes from para + akouō, “to hear.” It is “hearing amiss,” a refusal to listen, the failure to act on what one has heard (Rom 5:19, the “disobedience” of Adam; 2 Cor 10:6).

The second word is chosen with care. The whole passage is about hearing: “the things we have heard” (2:1), “those who heard” (2:3). Parakoē is the opposite of the attentive hearing the author calls for. It is the sin of the wilderness generation, who heard God’s voice and hardened their hearts (3:7–19). Between them the two words cover sins of commission and sins of neglect—what one does against God’s word, and what one fails to do because one has not truly listened. The word “every” (πᾶσα pasa) makes the point that none escaped God’s notice.

Each such sin “received a just recompense.” The noun μισθαποδοσία misthapodosia literally means “payment of wages.” It is a word Hebrews alone uses in the New Testament, and elsewhere in the book it describes the reward God gives the faithful (10:35; 11:26; compare 11:6, God is a “rewarder,” μισθαποδότης misthapodotēs, of those who seek him). Here it is negative: the wages of transgression. The adjective ἔνδικον endikon, “just, in accordance with what is right,” insists that God’s judgments under the old covenant were not arbitrary or excessive. The penalty fit the offense.

The readers would have remembered many examples. The law distinguished unintentional sins, for which sacrifice was provided, from sins committed “with a high hand,” in deliberate defiance: כִּי דְבַר־יְהוָה בָּזָה וְאֶת־מִצְוָתוֹ הֵפַר הִכָּרֵת תִּכָּרֵת הַנֶּפֶשׁ הַהִוא — ki devar-YHWH bazah ve’et-mitsvato hepher, hikkaret tikkaret hannephesh hahi’, “because he has despised the word of the Lord and broken his commandment, that person shall surely be cut off” (Num 15:31). Notice the phrase “despised the word of the Lord”—it is exactly the kind of contempt for God’s spoken word that Hebrews warns against. The author will later appeal to the law’s demand that anyone who set aside the law of Moses died without mercy on the testimony of two or three witnesses (10:28, drawing on Deut 17:6). Above all, the readers would remember the generation that came out of Egypt, heard God’s voice at Sinai, and yet fell in the wilderness because of unbelief and disobedience (3:16–19; Num 14:20–35). Chapter 3 will develop that example at length.

The point of verse 2 is therefore not that the old covenant was harsh and the new covenant lenient. The author’s logic runs in the opposite direction. The old covenant showed that God takes his word seriously. The new covenant, spoken by the Son, carries even greater weight, and the consequences of neglecting it are proportionally greater (10:29: “how much worse punishment …”).

2:3

how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation—a salvation that began by being spoken through the Lord and was confirmed to us by those who heard,

Now comes the “how much more.” The question “how shall we escape?” (πῶς ἡμεῖς ἐκφευξόμεθα pōs hēmeis ekpheuxometha) expects the answer “we shall not.” The pronoun “we” is emphatic in Greek (it is stated, though the verb already contains it): “we”—who have heard not angels but the Lord. The verb ἐκφεύγω ekpheugō means “to flee out of, to escape,” and the future tense looks ahead to the final judgment. What is to be escaped is not stated, but the context makes it clear: the “just recompense” of verse 2, now intensified. Hebrews repeats the same verb in 12:25: “if they did not escape … much less shall we.”

The sin in view is to “neglect” (ἀμελήσαντες amelēsantes, from ἀμελέω ameleō). The word means “to be unconcerned about, to disregard, to pay no attention to.” Jesus used it in his parable of the wedding banquet: when the king’s servants announced that the feast was ready, the invited guests “paid no attention” (ἀμελήσαντες) and went off, one to his field, another to his business (Matt 22:5). They did not curse the king. They simply had other things to do. That is exactly the danger in Hebrews. The participle here is conditional—“if we neglect”—and it matches the drifting of verse 1. Neglect is the sin of the drifting boat. One need not attack the gospel to lose it; it is enough to ignore it. The same verb appears again, with sobering effect, in the new covenant prophecy quoted in 8:9: because Israel did not continue in God’s covenant, “I too disregarded them” (ἠμέλησα ēmelēsa), says the Lord. Those who neglect God’s word cannot expect to escape his neglect of them.

What is neglected is “so great a salvation” (τηλικαύτης … σωτηρίας tēlikautēs … sōtērias). The demonstrative tēlikoutos means “so great, so vast,” and the word order in Greek separates it from “salvation” by the participle, so that the greatness is held before the hearer’s eyes. Salvation (σωτηρία sōtēria) in Hebrews is a rich term. It looks back to what Christ has done (purification for sins, 1:3; deliverance from death’s power, 2:14–15), and forward to what believers will inherit (1:14; 9:28, “he will appear a second time … for salvation to those who eagerly wait for him”). It is the entrance of the “many sons” into glory (2:10) and of the people of God into God’s rest (4:1–11). Christ is its “pioneer” (2:10) and its “source” (5:9). The rest of the sermon is an unfolding of what makes this salvation “so great”: a better priest, a better sacrifice, a better covenant, a better sanctuary, a better hope.

The author then gives three reasons why this salvation demands attention, piling them into a relative clause that runs through verse 4. The first two are in verse 3.

First, its origin: “it began by being spoken through the Lord.” Literally, it “received a beginning to be spoken through the Lord” (ἀρχὴν λαβοῦσα λαλεῖσθαι διὰ τοῦ κυρίου archēn labousa laleisthai dia tou kyriou). The “Lord” here is Jesus. In 1:10 the author has already applied to the Son a psalm addressed to “Lord” (κύριε kyrie), meaning the Lord of Israel (Ps 102:25), and now he calls the earthly Jesus by that title. The word that came “through angels” is contrasted with the word that came “through the Lord.” The prepositional phrases are parallel (“through,” διά dia, in both), but the one through whom the second word came is the Son who outranks all angels. The phrase “began to be spoken” points to Jesus’ earthly ministry. He announced the kingdom of God, called people to repentance and faith, and declared the forgiveness of sins (Mark 1:14–15; Luke 4:18–21). Luke opens Acts by describing his Gospel as an account of “all that Jesus began to do and to teach” (Acts 1:1)—the same thought of a beginning that others then continued.

Second, its confirmation: “it was confirmed to us by those who heard.” The “ones who heard” (τῶν ἀκουσάντων tōn akousantōn) are the eyewitnesses who heard Jesus himself—the apostles and others who accompanied him (compare Luke 1:2, “those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word”; 1 John 1:1–3). They “confirmed” the message (ἐβεβαιώθη ebebaiōthē), the verb corresponding to “firm” (bebaios) in verse 2. The gospel did not reach the readers as a rumor. It came to them legally attested by competent witnesses, as the law required for any serious matter: “on the mouth of two witnesses or three witnesses” (עַל־פִּי שְׁנַיִם עֵדִים אוֹ שְׁלֹשָׁה עֵדִים ‘al-pi shenayim ‘edim o sheloshah ‘edim, Deut 17:6). The phrase “to us” (εἰς ἡμᾶς eis hēmas) includes the author among those who received the message secondhand.

Historical Background: What 2:3 Tells Us about the Author and Readers

This short clause is one of the most important pieces of evidence for the setting of Hebrews. The author says that the salvation “was confirmed to us by those who heard.” He does not claim to have heard Jesus himself. He places himself, together with his readers, in a generation that received the gospel from the first witnesses.

This sits uneasily with the traditional attribution of Hebrews to Paul. Paul insisted that he did not receive his gospel from any human being but through a revelation of Jesus Christ (Gal 1:11–12), and he regularly appealed to having seen the risen Lord (1 Cor 9:1; 15:8). It would be strange for him to describe himself as dependent on others for the confirmation of the message. Reformation commentators already felt the force of this. Luther, in his preface to Hebrews, pointed to this verse as evidence that the author was not an apostle but a disciple of the apostles; elsewhere he suggested Apollos as a possible author. Calvin also judged that the writer, by placing himself among the disciples of the apostles, shows that he was not Paul, though Calvin gladly received the letter as apostolic in authority. The study does not depend on identifying the author (see the Introduction). What matters is that 2:3 reveals a second-generation writer addressing second-generation believers.

For the readers, this is significant. They were removed from the events of Jesus’ life by perhaps three decades. The eyewitnesses were aging; some of their leaders had already died (13:7, “remember your leaders, who spoke to you the word of God; consider the outcome of their way of life”). The temptation of every second generation is to treat a received faith as an inheritance to be maintained rather than as a living word to be heard. The author answers that temptation not by downplaying the distance but by stressing the reliability of the chain: the Lord spoke; the witnesses confirmed; God himself added his testimony (2:4). The message they received secondhand was no less the word of God than if they had heard it from Jesus’ lips.

This chain of testimony has pastoral significance for all later readers. No Christian after the first generation hears Jesus in the flesh. We all depend, as the readers of Hebrews did, on “those who heard,” whose testimony now stands in the New Testament. The author’s argument implies that this testimony is sufficient. The word that comes to us through the apostolic witness is the word of the Lord, and to neglect it is to neglect him.

2:4

while God bore witness along with them by signs and wonders and various mighty works and distributions of the Holy Spirit according to his will?

Third, its divine attestation. The human witnesses did not testify alone. The verse begins with a genitive absolute—a Greek construction that sets a circumstance alongside the main statement—“God bearing witness along with them.” The participle συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος synepimartyrountos is a rare double compound: martyreō, “to bear witness,” plus epi, “in addition,” plus syn, “together with.” It appears nowhere else in the New Testament. The author has built a word that says, in effect, “God added his testimony on top of theirs and alongside theirs.” The present tense pictures this divine witness as accompanying the apostolic preaching while it was going on. God himself became a co-witness to the message about his Son.

The means of God’s witness are listed in four terms, the first three describing miracles from different angles:

  • “Signs” (σημείοις sēmeiois): miracles viewed as pointers, acts that signify something beyond themselves. The Gospel of John calls Jesus’ miracles “signs” because they reveal who he is (John 2:11; 20:30–31).
  • “Wonders” (τέρασιν terasin): miracles viewed as astonishing, events that awaken awe. The word never appears alone in the New Testament but always paired with “signs.”
  • “Various mighty works” (ποικίλαις δυνάμεσιν poikilais dynamesin): miracles viewed as displays of divine power. δύναμις dynamis, “power,” is the usual Gospel term for Jesus’ miracles (Mark 6:2, 5). The adjective poikilos means “many-colored, manifold, of many kinds.” Later the author uses the same adjective for “various” strange teachings (13:9); here it is God’s varied power on display.

Old Testament Background: “Signs and Wonders”

The pairing “signs and wonders” comes straight from the Old Testament, where it describes above all God’s mighty acts in the exodus. Israel’s confession at the offering of firstfruits recalls: וַיּוֹצִאֵנוּ יְהוָה מִמִּצְרַיִם בְּיָד חֲזָקָה וּבִזְרֹעַ נְטוּיָה וּבְמֹרָא גָּדֹל וּבְאֹתוֹת וּבְמֹפְתִים — vayyotsi’enu YHWH mimmitsrayim beyad chazaqah uvizroa‘ netuyah uvemora’ gadol uve’otot uvemophetim, “And the Lord brought us out of Egypt with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, with great terror, and with signs and with wonders” (Deut 26:8; compare Deut 4:34; 6:22; 34:11). The Hebrew words are אוֹת ’ot, “sign,” and מוֹפֵת mophet, “wonder, portent.”

The phrase therefore carries an implication the Jewish readers could hardly miss. The first covenant was given after God redeemed his people with signs and wonders. The new covenant word has likewise been accompanied by signs and wonders. God is doing again—and doing more fully—what he did in the exodus. The same phrase will reappear, in Hebrew, in a text the author quotes in 2:13: Isaiah and the children God gave him are “for signs and for wonders in Israel” (Isa 8:18).

The fourth item is different in kind: “distributions of the Holy Spirit” (πνεύματος ἁγίου μερισμοῖς pneumatos hagiou merismois). The noun μερισμός merismos means “a dividing, an apportioning.” The author uses it again in 4:12 of the word of God that “divides” soul and spirit. Here it is plural: repeated acts of distribution, God giving the Spirit or his gifts to different people in different measures.

Interpretive Options: “Distributions of the Holy Spirit”

  • The Spirit as the gift distributed. The genitive is objective: God distributes the Holy Spirit himself to believers. Paul asks the Galatians whether God, “who supplies the Spirit to you and works mighty works among you,” does so by works of the law or by hearing with faith (Gal 3:5). Peter declares that the exalted Jesus received the promised Spirit from the Father and “poured out this” (Acts 2:33). The Old Testament provides a vivid precedent when the Lord “took some of the Spirit that was on [Moses] and put it on the seventy elders” (Num 11:25; Hebrew וַיָּאצֶל מִן־הָרוּחַ אֲשֶׁר עָלָיו וַיִּתֵּן עַל־שִׁבְעִים אִישׁ הַזְּקֵנִים vayya’tsel min-haruach asher ‘alav vayyitten ‘al-shiv‘im ish hazzeqenim), so that they prophesied.
  • The gifts the Spirit distributes. The genitive is subjective or descriptive: the varied spiritual gifts given by (or belonging to) the Spirit. Paul says there are “distributions” (diaireseis) of gifts, but the same Spirit, who “distributes to each one individually just as he wills” (1 Cor 12:4, 11).

The two views are not far apart, since the gifts are the manifestation of the Spirit’s presence (1 Cor 12:7). The phrase “according to his will” (κατὰ τὴν αὐτοῦ θέλησιν kata tēn autou thelēsin) most naturally refers to God, the subject of the whole clause, who is the one bearing witness. The point is that these gifts were not human achievements or performances on demand; God gave them as he chose. Either way, the readers had experienced the Spirit’s presence in their own community. Hebrews 6:4–5 will remind them that they had “become partakers of the Holy Spirit” and “tasted … the powers of the age to come”—the same word, dynameis, as “mighty works” here.

Why does the author list these things? His purpose is not to discuss miracles for their own sake, nor to settle later questions about which gifts continue in the church, but to strengthen the chain of testimony. The salvation the readers had received rested on a threefold foundation: the word of the Lord, the testimony of eyewitnesses, and the confirming witness of God. In the same way, Peter told the crowd at Pentecost that Jesus was “a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs” (Acts 2:22), and Paul described the “signs and wonders and mighty works” that accompanied his own ministry (2 Cor 12:12; Rom 15:18–19). The miracles were God’s seal on the message.

And here the full force of the warning becomes clear. If the readers neglected this salvation, they would not be ignoring an unverified human opinion. They would be ignoring a word spoken by the Lord, confirmed by witnesses, and countersigned by God himself. They had seen and experienced God’s testimony. To drift away now would be to treat that testimony as worthless. The question of verse 3 therefore stands with all its weight: how shall we escape?

Yet the passage is not only a threat. Its final words speak of God’s generous will in distributing his Spirit. The God who warns is the God who has given. The preacher’s aim is to draw his hearers back to the gifts they had already received, so that they will hold on to the Giver.

For Reflection

  1. The danger named in 2:1 is drifting, not open rebellion. What “currents” in your own life most easily carry you away from attentive hearing of God’s word, and what does it look like in practice to “pay far closer attention”?
  2. The author argues that the greater the speaker, the greater the responsibility of the hearer. How does seeing Jesus as the Son of chapter 1 change the way you hear the Gospels and the apostolic writings?
  3. The word parakoē, “disobedience,” is literally “hearing amiss.” In what ways is it possible to hear God’s word regularly and still fail to truly listen?
  4. Like the first readers, we receive the gospel through “those who heard.” Why is the reliability of the apostolic witness so important for faith, and how does 2:3–4 strengthen your confidence in it?
  5. The author includes himself in his warning (“we must,” “how shall we escape”). How might this example shape the way Christians warn and encourage one another (see 3:12–13)?
