Faith That Conquered and Faith That Suffered (11:30–38)

11:30

By faith the walls of Jericho fell, after they had been encircled for seven days.

Between 11:29 and 11:30 lie forty years. The author has passed silently over the wilderness wanderings, whose story he told in chapters 3 and 4 as a warning of what unbelief does. The next “by faith” belongs to a new generation, the children of those who fell in the desert, standing now on the far side of the Jordan before the first city of Canaan. The sentence is remarkable for whom it does not name. Joshua, the leader, is not mentioned (Hebrews has named him only once, in 4:8, to say that he did not give the people the final rest). Nor are the priests or the people named. The grammatical subject of the sentence is “the walls of Jericho” (τὰ τείχη Ἰεριχώ ta teichē Ierichō): they fell by faith. The faith, of course, was that of Joshua and the people who obeyed God’s strange command; but the sentence puts the spotlight on the result, as if to say that faith accomplished what siege engines could not.

The verb ἔπεσαν epesan, “they fell,” is a simple aorist. The participle κυκλωθέντα kyklōthenta, “having been encircled,” is an aorist passive from κυκλόω kykloō, “surround, go around,” and ἐπὶ ἑπτὰ ἡμέρας epi hepta hēmeras means “over a period of seven days.” The walls fell after the encircling had been completed, exactly as God had said.

Old Testament Text: Joshua 6:2–5, 20

Hebrew (Josh 6:2): וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־יְהוֹשֻׁעַ רְאֵה נָתַתִּי בְיָדְךָ אֶת־יְרִיחוֹ וְאֶת־מַלְכָּהּ גִּבּוֹרֵי הֶחָיִל — vayyomer YHWH el-Yehoshua‘ re’eh natatti veyadekha et-Yericho ve’et-malkah gibbore hechayil, “The Lord said to Joshua, ‘See, I have given Jericho into your hand, with its king and mighty men of valor.’”

Note the tense: “I have given.” God spoke of the victory as already accomplished before a single step was taken. Faith acts on what God has declared done, while it is still unseen (11:1).

The instructions (6:3–4) were that the men of war march around the city once a day for six days, with seven priests carrying seven ram’s-horn trumpets (שׁוֹפְרוֹת shoferot) before the ark, and on the seventh day march around seven times while the priests blew the trumpets.

Hebrew (Josh 6:20): וַיָּרִיעוּ הָעָם תְּרוּעָה גְדוֹלָה וַתִּפֹּל הַחוֹמָה תַּחְתֶּיהָ — vayyari‘u ha‘am teru‘ah gedolah vattippol hachomah tachteha, “and the people shouted a great shout, and the wall fell down in its place [literally, beneath itself].”

The faith displayed at Jericho was the faith of obedience to a command that made no military sense. No ancient general would have proposed taking a fortified city by walking around it in silence for a week behind a procession of priests and the ark of the covenant. The march was, in effect, a liturgy rather than a siege. Six days of silent circling, with nothing visible happening, tested whether the people would trust God’s word or their own judgment. Their persistence through six days of apparent futility is itself a picture of the endurance Hebrews calls for. The walls were not overthrown by human force but by God’s power, answering the people’s trust in his promise. The sevens that run through the narrative (seven priests, seven trumpets, seven days, seven circuits on the seventh day) mark the event as a sacred work of God, with the ark, the symbol of his throne and presence, at its center.

The fall of Jericho also anticipates an important pattern in Hebrews. The city that seemed impregnable fell at God’s word; Israel then received the first portion of the promised land. Yet Hebrews has already told the readers that Joshua did not give the people the ultimate rest (4:8): the conquest of Canaan was a real but partial fulfillment, a pledge of a greater inheritance. Faith that brought down the walls of Jericho is the same faith that now looks for “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (11:10), the city that, unlike Jericho, cannot be shaken (12:27–28).

11:31

By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who disobeyed, because she had received the spies with peace.

The last person to be introduced with the solemn “by faith” is, astonishingly, a Canaanite woman and a prostitute. The roll call began with Abel and Enoch, ran through Abraham and Moses, and reaches its final named individual in Rahab of Jericho. For a congregation proud of its descent from Abraham and its heritage in Moses, the inclusion of a foreign woman of disreputable profession is a deliberate reminder that faith, not ancestry or respectability, is what places a person among God’s people.

The author does not soften the description: Ῥαὰβ ἡ πόρνη Rhaab hē pornē, “Rahab the prostitute.” The Hebrew of Joshua 2:1 calls her אִשָּׁה זוֹנָה ishshah zonah, “a woman, a prostitute,” and Joshua 6:17, 25 repeats רָחָב הַזּוֹנָה Rachav hazzonah, “Rahab the prostitute.” Some later Jewish interpreters were uncomfortable with this; Josephus describes the spies as lodging at Rahab’s inn (Ant. 5.7–8), and the Aramaic Targum of Joshua renders zonah with a word meaning “innkeeper.” But the Hebrew word plainly means prostitute, and both Hebrews and James (Jas 2:25) retain the term. The gospel does not need to make its heroes respectable. Rahab’s past is named precisely so that the grace of God may be seen. Matthew includes her in the genealogy of Jesus as the mother of Boaz and so an ancestor of David and of the Messiah (Matt 1:5). Jesus himself told the religious leaders that “tax collectors and prostitutes are going into the kingdom of God before you,” because they believed (Matt 21:31–32).

Old Testament Text: Rahab’s Confession (Josh 2:9, 11)

Hebrew (Josh 2:9): יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־נָתַן יְהוָה לָכֶם אֶת־הָאָרֶץ — yada‘ti ki-natan YHWH lakhem et-ha’arets, “I know that the Lord has given you the land.”

Hebrew (Josh 2:11): כִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם הוּא אֱלֹהִים בַּשָּׁמַיִם מִמַּעַל וְעַל־הָאָרֶץ מִתָּחַת — ki YHWH Elohekhem hu Elohim bashshamayim mimma‘al ve‘al-ha’arets mittachat, “for the Lord your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth below.”

Rahab’s faith had content. She had heard how the Lord dried up the waters of the Sea of Reeds (Josh 2:10, the same event as Heb 11:29), and she drew the right conclusion: Israel’s God is the one true God of heaven and earth. Her confession uses language close to Moses’ own in Deuteronomy 4:39. She then asked the spies to swear “by the Lord” to show chesed, covenant kindness, to her family (Josh 2:12), and she bound the scarlet cord in her window as the sign of her protection (Josh 2:18), much as Israel had put blood on their doorposts in Egypt (11:28).

The main verb, οὐ συναπώλετο ou synapōleto, “she did not perish with,” uses a compound verb found only here in the New Testament: συναπόλλυμαι synapollymai, “to be destroyed together with.” The prefix syn, “with,” again marks solidarity, but in reverse: Moses chose to suffer with the people of God (11:25); Rahab, by faith, escaped perishing with the people of Jericho.

The people of Jericho are described as τοῖς ἀπειθήσασιν tois apeithēsasin, “those who disobeyed.” This exact form has appeared earlier in Hebrews, in the warning about the wilderness generation: “To whom did he swear that they would not enter his rest, if not to those who disobeyed (tois apeithēsasin)?” (3:18). The echo is surely intentional. The inhabitants of Jericho were not ignorant; as Rahab herself said, the whole city had heard what the Lord had done, and their hearts melted (Josh 2:10–11). They knew enough to fear, but they did not turn to the God they feared. Their unbelief expressed itself as disobedience, as unbelief always does (compare 3:18–19, where “disobeyed” and “unbelief” describe the same failure). So the Israelites of the wilderness and the Canaanites of Jericho fell under the same description, while a Canaanite prostitute was spared because she believed. Belonging to God’s people is a matter of faith, not birth.

The participle δεξαμένη dexamenē, “having received, welcomed,” gives the ground and evidence of her faith: she received the spies (κατασκόπους kataskopous, “scouts, spies”) μετ’ εἰρήνης met’ eirēnēs, “with peace,” that is, as friends rather than enemies, giving them safety at the risk of her life. In a world where hospitality toward strangers was a sacred duty and treachery toward guests a grave dishonor, Rahab’s reception of the spies was also an act of allegiance: she sided with Israel’s God against her own king (Josh 2:2–7). Hebrews will soon urge its readers not to neglect hospitality (13:2).

James uses Rahab to show that genuine faith produces action: “Was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out by another way?” (Jas 2:25). Hebrews says she was saved “by faith.” There is no contradiction. Hebrews shows the root, James the fruit. The faith that confessed “the Lord your God, he is God” is the faith that hid the spies. Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth near the end of the first century and clearly influenced by Hebrews, says that Rahab was saved “because of her faith and hospitality,” holding the two together (1 Clem 12:1), and he sees in the scarlet cord a sign that redemption would come through the blood of the Lord (1 Clem 12:7).

Joshua 6:25 reports the outcome: וַתֵּשֶׁב בְּקֶרֶב יִשְׂרָאֵל עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה vatteshev beqerev Yisra’el ‘ad hayyom hazzeh, “and she has lived in the midst of Israel to this day.” The outsider became an insider. That is exactly the movement Hebrews wants its readers to see: by faith, those who were far off are brought into the household of God, and by unbelief, those who were near can perish.

11:32

And what more shall I say? For time will fail me if I tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets,

With a rhetorical question, the preacher changes pace. Καὶ τί ἔτι λέγω; Kai ti eti legō? “And what more shall I say?” The verb legō here is best taken as a deliberative subjunctive (the present indicative and subjunctive have the same form): “What should I still say?” It signals that the speaker has more material than time, and that he will now summarize rather than narrate.

The next words confirm it: ἐπιλείψει με γὰρ διηγούμενον ὁ χρόνος epileipsei me gar diēgoumenon ho chronos, literally “for time will fail me telling,” that is, “if I go on to narrate.” This was a standard expression of Greek orators. Demosthenes, for example, says in his speech On the Crown that the day would fail him if he were to list the names of the traitors, and similar phrases occur in Isocrates and in Philo. The phrase tells us something about the character of Hebrews: it is a sermon (13:22, “a word of exhortation”), composed with the skill of a trained speaker and meant to be heard. It also reminds us that the list of faithful people is not complete. The Old Testament is full of examples; the author is not giving an exhaustive catalog but a representative selection.

A small grammatical detail is worth noting. The participle διηγούμενον diēgoumenon, “telling, narrating,” is masculine singular, agreeing with “me.” The author speaks of himself as a man. This is one of the few clues in the letter about the writer, and it tells against the occasional modern suggestion that Hebrews was written by a woman such as Priscilla.

Six names follow, and then “the prophets.” Four are judges (Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah), one is a king (David), and one is the last judge and first of the great prophets after Moses (Samuel). The order is curious. Chronologically, Barak (Judg 4–5) came before Gideon (Judg 6–8), Jephthah (Judg 11–12) before Samson (Judg 13–16), and Samuel before David. In each pair the author has reversed the historical order. Some suggest he names the better-known figure of each pair first; others note that 1 Samuel 12:11, in Samuel’s farewell speech, lists the judges as “Jerubbaal [Gideon], Bedan, Jephthah, and Samuel,” where the Greek translation reads “Barak” for “Bedan,” so that the author may have had that list in mind. The word τε te joined with kai (“both … and”) links David and Samuel closely, and Samuel naturally leads into “the prophets,” of whom he was the first great representative (Acts 3:24). In any case the list is brisk and impressionistic, suited to a speaker who has said that time is running out.

Historical Background: Faith Without Sinlessness

The names in this verse belong to some of the most troubled stories in the Old Testament. Gideon doubted and asked for repeated signs (Judg 6:15, 36–40), yet at God’s word he went against the vast Midianite army with three hundred men (Judg 7); afterward he made a golden ephod that “became a snare to Gideon and his house” (Judg 8:27). Barak would not go to battle unless Deborah went with him, so that the honor of the victory went to a woman (Judg 4:8–9); yet he went down from Mount Tabor at the word of the Lord against Sisera’s chariots (Judg 4:14). Samson was ruled by his appetites, broke his Nazirite vow, and was betrayed by Delilah; yet at the end, blinded and humiliated, he called on the Lord, “Remember me and strengthen me, I pray, only this once” (Judg 16:28), and God answered. Jephthah, the son of a prostitute, rejected by his brothers, appealed to the Lord as judge between Israel and Ammon (Judg 11:27); but he also made a rash vow concerning his daughter (Judg 11:30–40), whose outcome has been debated since antiquity and which Scripture nowhere commends. David trusted God against Goliath, coming “in the name of the Lord of hosts” (1 Sam 17:45), and was “a man after God’s own heart” (1 Sam 13:14; Acts 13:22); yet he committed adultery and murder (2 Sam 11). Samuel was faithful from boyhood, yet his sons did not walk in his ways (1 Sam 8:1–5).

Hebrews does not mention their failures, but it does not hide them either; the readers knew these stories. The point is plain: God commends faith, not sinlessness. These were flawed people who, at decisive moments, trusted the word of God and acted on it. Their inclusion is a great comfort to weak believers. The one perfect example of faith will come in 12:2, “Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith.” All others are sinners saved by grace who trusted the promise.

Jewish literature before Hebrews contains several catalogs of heroes. Sirach’s “Praise of the Fathers” (Sir 44–50) surveys Israel’s history from Enoch to the high priest Simon. In 1 Maccabees, the dying Mattathias urges his sons to remember the deeds of the fathers, naming Abraham, Joseph, Phinehas, Joshua, Caleb, David, Elijah, the three young men in the fire, and Daniel among the lions (1 Macc 2:51–60). Hebrews stands in this tradition, yet with a distinctive emphasis. Sirach praises the fathers for their glory and achievements; Hebrews praises them for their faith, a faith that looked beyond their own lifetime to what they did not receive (11:13, 39).

11:33

who through faith conquered kingdoms, practiced justice, obtained promises, shut the mouths of lions,

Having named names, the author now lists deeds. The style changes noticeably. Instead of the repeated “by faith” (πίστει pistei) at the head of each sentence, a single phrase, διὰ πίστεως dia pisteōs, “through faith,” governs a rapid series of short clauses joined without connecting words. Ancient rhetoric called this asyndeton, the omission of “and”; its effect is to create speed and accumulation, like a drumroll. Verses 33–34 contain nine such clauses, grouped loosely in threes. The deeds are not tied to particular names, and the reader is invited to recall the stories behind them. Many fit more than one person.

“Conquered kingdoms.” The verb κατηγωνίσαντο katēgōnisanto, from καταγωνίζομαι katagōnizomai, occurs only here in the New Testament. It contains the root ἀγών agōn, “contest, struggle,” the word used of athletic competition, which reappears in 12:1 (“let us run with endurance the race [agōna] set before us”). It means “to struggle against and overcome, to conquer.” Joshua subdued the kings of Canaan (Josh 12); the judges overthrew oppressors such as Jabin of Hazor (Judg 4) and the Midianites (Judg 7–8); David conquered the Philistines, Moabites, Arameans, Edomites, and Ammonites (2 Sam 8:1–14). Faith here is not passive resignation; it fights God’s battles.

“Practiced justice.” The phrase εἰργάσαντο δικαιοσύνην eirgasanto dikaiosynēn, “worked righteousness,” in this context almost certainly refers to the administration of justice by rulers and judges. The summary of David’s reign uses the corresponding Hebrew expression: וַיְהִי דָוִד עֹשֶׂה מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה לְכָל־עַמּוֹ vayhi David ‘oseh mishpat utsedaqah lekhol-‘ammo, “and David was doing justice and righteousness for all his people” (2 Sam 8:15). Samuel could appeal to the whole nation to testify that he had defrauded and oppressed no one (1 Sam 12:3–5). Faith in God expresses itself in just dealing with people. The judges and kings who trusted God were called to govern according to his righteousness, and when they did so, they were acting by faith.

“Obtained promises.” ἐπέτυχον ἐπαγγελιῶν epetychon epangeliōn, “attained promises” (the verb ἐπιτυγχάνω epitynchanō means “to obtain, reach, attain”). The noun is plural and without the article: “promises,” specific promises given in particular circumstances. Gideon was promised victory over Midian and received it (Judg 6:16; 7:7); David was promised the kingdom and a lasting dynasty (1 Sam 16; 2 Sam 7). Joshua’s summary of the conquest declares: לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר מִכֹּל הַדָּבָר הַטּוֹב אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה אֶל־בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל הַכֹּל בָּא lo-nafal davar mikkol haddavar hattov asher-dibber YHWH el-bet Yisra’el; hakkol ba, “Not one word of all the good word that the Lord had spoken to the house of Israel fell; all came to pass” (Josh 21:45). This must be read alongside 11:39, where we are told that all these “did not receive the promise” (singular, with the article). Both statements are true. They received many particular promises in their own lifetimes, fulfillments that were real and that confirmed God’s faithfulness; but they did not receive the promise, the final inheritance to which all the particular promises pointed. Hebrews made the same distinction with Abraham, who “obtained the promise” of a son after patient endurance (6:15) and yet died “not having received the promises” in their fullness (11:13).

“Shut the mouths of lions.” ἔφραξαν στόματα λεόντων ephraxan stomata leontōn; the verb φράσσω phrassō means “to stop up, shut, close.” The clearest reference is Daniel, who told King Darius from the lions’ den: “My God sent his angel and shut the lions’ mouths, and they have not harmed me, because I was found innocent before him” (Dan 6:22; the account says he was unharmed “because he had trusted in his God,” Dan 6:23). Mattathias’ catalog in 1 Maccabees likewise recalls that Daniel “was delivered from the mouth of lions” (1 Macc 2:60). Other lion stories may also be in the author’s mind: Samson tore apart a lion with his bare hands (Judg 14:5–6), David rescued sheep from a lion and a bear and drew from this confidence that God would deliver him from Goliath (1 Sam 17:34–37), and Benaiah, one of David’s mighty men, went down into a pit and killed a lion on a snowy day (2 Sam 23:20). But “shut the mouths” points most directly to Daniel, where the lions were not killed but restrained. In Daniel’s case, what shut the lions’ mouths was not his own strength but God’s answer to his faith. The faith of the believer is the instrument; the power belongs to God.

11:34

quenched the power of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, were made strong out of weakness, became mighty in war, routed the armies of foreigners.

The catalog continues with five more clauses.

“Quenched the power of fire.” ἔσβεσαν δύναμιν πυρός esbesan dynamin pyros; the verb σβέννυμι sbennymi means “extinguish, put out.” The phrase does not say they put out the fire itself, but its power: the fire still burned, but it had no power over them. This fits Daniel 3 exactly. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refused to bow to Nebuchadnezzar’s image, declaring that their God was able to deliver them, “but if not,” they still would not serve his gods (Dan 3:17–18). They were thrown into the furnace, which was so hot it killed the men who threw them in; yet they walked unharmed in the fire with a fourth figure “like a son of the gods,” and “the fire had no power over their bodies” (Dan 3:25, 27). Isaiah had promised: כִּי־תֵלֵךְ בְּמוֹ־אֵשׁ לֹא תִכָּוֶה וְלֶהָבָה לֹא תִבְעַר־בָּךְ ki-telekh bemo-esh lo tikkaveh velehavah lo tiv‘ar-bakh, “when you walk through fire you shall not be burned, and the flame shall not consume you” (Isa 43:2). The three young men’s “but if not” is worth pondering, because it anticipates the turn in 11:35b. Their faith did not depend on deliverance; they were ready to die. God chose to deliver them, but their faith would have been no less real had he not.

“Escaped the edge of the sword.” ἔφυγον στόματα μαχαίρης ephygon stomata machairēs, literally “fled the mouths of the sword.” The “mouth” of a sword is a Hebrew idiom for its edge, לְפִי־חָרֶב lefi-charev, “by the mouth of the sword” (Josh 6:21 and often): the sword is pictured as devouring. Notice the vivid parallel with the previous verse: the faithful shut the mouths of lions and escaped the mouths of the sword. David escaped Saul’s spear and sword many times (1 Sam 18:11; 19:10–12); Elijah escaped Jezebel’s threat (1 Kgs 19:1–3); Elisha was protected from the Aramean army (2 Kgs 6:15–23); Jeremiah and Baruch were hidden from King Jehoiakim: וַיַּסְתִּרֵם יְהוָה vayyastirem YHWH, “but the Lord hid them” (Jer 36:26). Readers of Hebrews may also have recalled that “the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-mouthed sword (μάχαιραν δίστομον machairan distomon)” (4:12). The one sword no one can escape is the word of God; the swords of men can be escaped when God wills.

“Were made strong out of weakness.” ἐδυναμώθησαν ἀπὸ ἀσθενείας edynamōthēsan apo astheneias. The verb is passive: “they were empowered,” that is, by God. The preposition apo, “from, out of,” marks weakness as the starting point. Gideon protested that his clan was the weakest in Manasseh and he the least in his father’s house (Judg 6:15); God made him strong. Samson, blinded and enslaved, prayed for strength “only this once” and received it (Judg 16:28–30). Hezekiah, sick unto death (Isa 38:1), was granted fifteen more years. Isaiah proclaims the principle: נֹתֵן לַיָּעֵף כֹּחַ וּלְאֵין אוֹנִים עָצְמָה יַרְבֶּה noten laya‘ef koach ul’en onim ‘otsmah yarbeh, “He gives power to the faint, and to the one who has no might he increases strength” (Isa 40:29). Paul learned the same truth: “My power is made perfect in weakness … for when I am weak, then I am strong” (2 Cor 12:9–10). The readers, whose hands were drooping and knees weak (12:12), needed to hear that weakness is where God’s strength begins its work.

“Became mighty in war.” ἐγενήθησαν ἰσχυροὶ ἐν πολέμῳ egenēthēsan ischyroi en polemō. David sang, “He trains my hands for war” (2 Sam 22:35; Ps 18:34). The judges and kings fought Israel’s wars in dependence on God.

“Routed the armies of foreigners.” παρεμβολὰς ἔκλιναν ἀλλοτρίων parembolas eklinan allotriōn. παρεμβολή parembolē means “encampment” or “army in battle line”; the same word appears in 13:11, 13 of the “camp” of Israel outside of which Jesus suffered. κλίνω klinō, “bend, cause to give way,” here means to turn an army to flight. Gideon’s three hundred threw the Midianite camp into panic so that they turned their swords on one another (Judg 7:21–22); Jonathan and his armor-bearer routed a Philistine garrison (1 Sam 14:6–15), Jonathan saying, “Nothing can hinder the Lord from saving by many or by few.” The word ἀλλότριος allotrios, “foreign, belonging to another,” marks these armies as aliens to the covenant.

It is possible that some of these clauses also reach into the Maccabean period, when Judas Maccabeus and his brothers, few in number, defeated much larger Seleucid armies (1 Macc 3–4), for the next verses clearly allude to that era. But the primary reference is to the canonical history from Joshua to the exile and beyond. The overarching point is that faith has repeatedly been the channel through which God has given his people victory. Yet the author is about to show that this is only half the story.

11:35

Women received back their dead by resurrection. But others were tortured, not accepting release, so that they might obtain a better resurrection.

This verse is the hinge of the paragraph. Its first half completes the list of triumphs with the greatest triumph of all, restoration from death; its second half begins the list of sufferings with a martyrdom endured in hope of resurrection. The word “resurrection” (ἀνάστασις anastasis) appears in both halves, and the contrast between them is carefully drawn.

“Women received back their dead by resurrection.” ἔλαβον γυναῖκες ἐξ ἀναστάσεως τοὺς νεκροὺς αὐτῶν elabon gynaikes ex anastaseōs tous nekrous autōn. Two Old Testament stories fit exactly. The widow of Zarephath, who had sheltered Elijah, lost her son to illness; Elijah stretched himself on the boy three times and cried to the Lord, and the child revived; Elijah “gave him to his mother,” and she confessed, “Now I know that you are a man of God, and that the word of the Lord in your mouth is truth” (1 Kgs 17:17–24). The Shunammite woman, who had provided a room for Elisha, lost the son God had given her in answer to the prophet’s word; she went straight to Elisha, who raised the boy, and “she took up her son and went out” (2 Kgs 4:18–37). Both women had shown hospitality to prophets, and both are women of faith. Jesus himself recalled the widow of Zarephath as an example of a foreigner who received God’s blessing (Luke 4:25–26), and he too gave back a widow’s son at Nain to his mother (Luke 7:11–17). Hebrews has already used the same idea of Abraham: he “received back” Isaac from the dead, figuratively speaking (11:19).

The Greek phrase ex anastaseōs, “out of resurrection, by resurrection,” describes these miracles as resurrections, though they were restorations to mortal life; the children would die again. That is precisely the point of the contrast that follows.

“But others were tortured.” The conjunction δέ de, “but,” and the pronoun ἄλλοι alloi, “others,” mark a sharp turn. From here to the end of verse 38, faith does not escape suffering; it endures it. The verb is ἐτυμπανίσθησαν etympanisthēsan, aorist passive of τυμπανίζω tympanizō.

Word Study: τυμπανίζω tympanizō

The verb comes from τύμπανον tympanon, “drum” (compare English “tympani” and “tympanum,” the eardrum). From the drum the word came to be used for a wooden frame or wheel on which a victim was stretched taut like a drumskin and beaten, sometimes to death, with clubs or rods. To be “tympanized” was to be tortured on this instrument, or beaten as a drum is beaten. The precise form of the device is not certain, but the meaning “to be tortured, broken by beating” is clear. Its most famous occurrence in Jewish literature is in 2 Maccabees 6, where the aged scribe Eleazar goes of his own accord to the tympanon rather than eat swine’s flesh (2 Macc 6:19, 28).

Historical Background: The Maccabean Martyrs

In 167 BC, the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who ruled Syria and Judea, attempted to force the Jews to abandon their ancestral religion. He outlawed circumcision and Sabbath observance, desecrated the temple in Jerusalem with pagan sacrifice, and compelled Jews to eat food forbidden by the law (1 Macc 1:41–64). Daniel had foreseen this crisis: a contemptible king would profane the sanctuary, “but the people who know their God shall stand firm and take action,” and some of the wise “shall fall by sword and flame, by captivity and plunder” (Dan 11:31–35).

Second Maccabees tells of two famous martyrdoms in this persecution. Eleazar, a scribe of ninety, was ordered to eat pork. Friends privately urged him to bring meat he was permitted to eat and only pretend to eat the sacrificial flesh, so that he might be released. He refused, lest the young be led astray by his pretense, and went to the tympanon and died under the blows, leaving his death “as an example of nobility” (2 Macc 6:18–31). Then seven brothers and their mother were arrested and tortured one by one before the king for refusing to eat swine’s flesh (2 Macc 7). As they died, they confessed their hope in the resurrection. The second brother declared that the King of the universe would raise up those who died for his laws to an everlasting renewal of life (7:9); the third, holding out his hands to be cut off, said he had received them from Heaven and hoped to receive them back from God (7:11); the fourth told the king that for him there would be no resurrection to life (7:14). The king offered the youngest wealth and high position if he would turn from the ways of his fathers (7:24), and he refused. The mother urged her last son to accept death so that, in God’s mercy, she might receive him back again with his brothers (7:29). The story was later retold at length in 4 Maccabees.

The connection with Hebrews is striking. These martyrs were offered release on condition of compromise and refused it; they died expressly in the hope of resurrection; and a mother expected to “receive back” her sons, not by a restoration to this life, as the women of 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 4 did, but in the resurrection. The author of Hebrews almost certainly has them in mind, and his readers would have recognized the allusion at once. These events were honored in Jewish memory; the Feast of Dedication, which commemorates the rededication of the temple after this persecution, is mentioned in John 10:22.

A Note on 2 Maccabees and Scripture

The allusion does not mean that Hebrews treats 2 Maccabees as Scripture. Hebrews refers to the Maccabean martyrs as history, as a true account of faithful people, without quoting the book or introducing it with any formula of divine speech such as “the Holy Spirit says” (3:7) or “he says” (1:6–7), as it does when citing the Old Testament. The New Testament elsewhere refers to events and traditions outside the canon without granting those sources canonical authority (for example, the names Jannes and Jambres in 2 Tim 3:8, and Moses’ Egyptian education in Acts 7:22). Second Maccabees itself makes no claim to inspiration: its author describes his work as an abridgment of a five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene (2 Macc 2:23) and ends by saying that if it is well written that is what he desired, and if poorly, that was the best he could do (2 Macc 15:38). The hope for which the martyrs died, the resurrection of the body, rests not on 2 Maccabees but on the canonical Scriptures (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2; compare Job 19:25–27), which the martyrs themselves believed. Hebrews honors their faith; it draws its doctrine from God’s word.

“Not accepting release.” οὐ προσδεξάμενοι τὴν ἀπολύτρωσιν ou prosdexamenoi tēn apolytrōsin. ἀπολύτρωσις apolytrōsis normally means “redemption, release (by payment of a ransom).” Hebrews used it in 9:15 of the redemption from transgressions secured by Christ’s death. Here it has the ordinary sense of release from torture and death, offered by the persecutors at the price of apostasy. To accept it would have meant buying one’s life with one’s faithfulness. The martyrs did “not accept” it. The verb προσδέχομαι prosdechomai, “accept, welcome,” is the same verb used in 10:34 of the readers themselves: “you accepted (προσεδέξασθε prosedexasthe) the plundering of your property with joy, knowing that you yourselves had a better and abiding possession.” The parallel is surely intended. The readers had accepted loss; the martyrs refused release. In both cases faith made the choice because it saw something “better.”

“So that they might obtain a better resurrection.” ἵνα κρείττονος ἀναστάσεως τύχωσιν hina kreittonos anastaseōs tychōsin. The purpose clause states their motive, and the comparative “better” (κρείττων kreittōn), one of the key words of Hebrews (1:4; 7:19, 22; 8:6; 9:23; 10:34; 11:16, 40; 12:24), explains the contrast with the first half of the verse. The women received their children back to mortal life; the martyrs looked for resurrection to everlasting life. The one was a restoration to the old order, the other an entrance into the new. Daniel had prophesied: “many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life” (Dan 12:2), and Isaiah: יִחְיוּ מֵתֶיךָ נְבֵלָתִי יְקוּמוּן הָקִיצוּ וְרַנְּנוּ שֹׁכְנֵי עָפָר yichyu metekha nevelati yequmun haqitsu veranenu shokhne ‘afar, “Your dead shall live; their bodies shall rise. Awake and sing for joy, you who dwell in the dust!” (Isa 26:19). The “better resurrection” may also be better in contrast with the resurrection of the wicked “to shame and everlasting contempt” (Dan 12:2; compare John 5:29); but the main contrast in the verse is with the temporary restorations of 11:35a.

This is the theological heart of the paragraph. Faith is not measured by whether God delivers from suffering. Some by faith escaped the sword (11:34); others by faith died by it (11:37). Some women by faith received their dead back; other believers by faith let themselves be killed rather than deny God. The same faith produces both outcomes, because faith rests not on the outcome but on God and his promise. Faith that trusts God only for deliverance in this life is not yet the faith of Hebrews 11, which “greeted the promises from afar” and died still trusting (11:13). For Christians, this hope has been secured by the resurrection of the one whom God “brought up from the dead” (13:20), the firstfruits of the better resurrection.

11:36

Still others experienced mockings and scourgings, and even chains and prison.

The list of sufferings continues with a new group: ἕτεροι δέ heteroi de, “and others,” “still others.” The idiom πεῖραν ἔλαβον peiran elabon, “they took a trial of,” that is, “they experienced,” is the same expression used in 11:29 of the Egyptians “attempting” the sea. The repetition may be deliberate: the Egyptians made trial of the sea and were destroyed; these believers made trial of suffering and were not destroyed in their faith.

Four forms of suffering are named, arranged in two pairs, with the second pair introduced by ἔτι δέ eti de, “and further, and even,” which intensifies the list.

“Mockings” (ἐμπαιγμῶν empaigmōn, from ἐμπαιγμός empaigmos, “derision, scornful ridicule”). Mockery attacks honor; in a culture built on honor and shame, it was a deeply wounding form of persecution. Elisha was jeered by youths (2 Kgs 2:23); the messengers of God were mocked and his prophets scoffed at, until there was no remedy (2 Chr 36:16); in 2 Maccabees the brothers were brought forward one after another to be made sport of before their torture (2 Macc 7:7, 10). The same word family is used repeatedly of the mocking of Jesus by the soldiers, the priests, and the passersby at his trial and crucifixion (Matt 27:29, 31, 41). The readers knew this too: they had been “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions,” literally “made a theater” (θεατριζόμενοι theatrizomenoi, 10:33).

“Scourgings” (μαστίγων mastigōn, from μάστιξ mastix, “whip, lash,” and so “flogging”). The seven brothers were first tortured with whips and thongs (2 Macc 7:1). Jeremiah was beaten by Pashhur (Jer 20:2) and later by the officials (Jer 37:15). Jesus foretold that his messengers would be flogged in the synagogues (Matt 10:17; 23:34), and Paul received the synagogue’s thirty-nine lashes five times (2 Cor 11:24).

“Chains and prison” (δεσμῶν καὶ φυλακῆς desmōn kai phylakēs). δεσμός desmos means “bond, fetter, chain”; φυλακή phylakē, “guard, prison.” Jeremiah is again the outstanding example.

Old Testament Text: Jeremiah 20:2 and 37:15

Hebrew (Jer 20:2): וַיַּכֶּה פַשְׁחוּר אֵת יִרְמְיָהוּ הַנָּבִיא וַיִּתֵּן אֹתוֹ עַל־הַמַּהְפֶּכֶת אֲשֶׁר בְּשַׁעַר בִּנְיָמִן הָעֶלְיוֹן אֲשֶׁר בְּבֵית יְהוָה — vayyakkeh Fashchur et Yirmeyahu hannavi vayyitten oto ‘al-hammahpekhet asher besha‘ar Binyamin ha‘elyon asher beveit YHWH, “Then Pashhur struck Jeremiah the prophet and put him in the stocks that were in the upper Benjamin Gate in the house of the Lord.” The word מַהְפֶּכֶת mahpekhet comes from a root meaning “to turn, twist”; it was a device that held the body in a cramped, distorted position, a public humiliation as well as a torment.

Hebrew (Jer 37:15): וַיִּקְצְפוּ הַשָּׂרִים עַל־יִרְמְיָהוּ וְהִכּוּ אֹתוֹ וְנָתְנוּ אוֹתוֹ בֵּית הָאֵסוּר בֵּית יְהוֹנָתָן הַסֹּפֵר כִּי־אֹתוֹ עָשׂוּ לְבֵית הַכֶּלֶא — vayyiqtsefu hassarim ‘al-Yirmeyahu vehikku oto venatenu oto bet ha’esur bet Yehonatan hassofer ki-oto ‘asu levet hakkele, “The officials were furious with Jeremiah, and they beat him and put him in the house of bonds, in the house of Jonathan the secretary, for they had made it into a prison.” Afterward he was lowered with ropes into a muddy cistern and sank in the mud (Jer 38:6).

Other prophets suffered the same. Micaiah son of Imlah was struck on the cheek and imprisoned on bread and water for prophesying truthfully against Ahab (1 Kgs 22:24–27), and the seer Hanani was put in the stocks by King Asa (2 Chr 16:10). The readers themselves had “sympathized with the prisoners” (10:34), and at the end of the sermon they will be told to “remember the prisoners as though bound with them” (13:3). Timothy had recently been in prison (13:23). Chains and prison were not ancient history for this congregation; they were a present reality and a looming threat. The preacher sets their experience within the long story of God’s faithful people. Prison is not a sign that God has abandoned his servants; it is the path many of the most faithful have walked, and the path of the apostles (Acts 5:18; 12:4; 16:23) and of the Lord himself.

11:37

They were stoned, they were sawn in two, they died by murder with the sword; they went about in sheepskins, in goatskins, destitute, afflicted, mistreated—

The sentences grow shorter and starker. Three aorist passive verbs, without connecting words, name three violent deaths; then the verse turns to the life of hardship of those who were hunted and homeless.

“They were stoned.” ἐλιθάσθησαν elithasthēsan, from λιθάζω lithazō, “stone (to death).” Stoning was the form of execution prescribed in the law for blasphemy and idolatry, and it was horribly ironic that it should be turned against God’s own messengers. The clearest Old Testament example is Zechariah son of Jehoiada the priest. When the Spirit of God came upon him and he rebuked the people for forsaking the Lord, “they conspired against him, and by the command of the king they stoned him with stones in the court of the house of the Lord.” As he died he said, “May the Lord see and avenge!” (2 Chr 24:20–22). Jesus referred to this martyrdom: “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah, whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar” (Matt 23:35; Luke 11:51). Since 2 Chronicles stood last in the traditional order of the Hebrew Bible, Jesus’ words span the Scriptures from the first murder to the last recorded in the canon. (Matthew calls him “son of Barachiah,” which raises a question of identification that need not detain us here; the reference to the temple court points to the Zechariah of 2 Chronicles 24.) Hebrews began the roll call with Abel (11:4); here it alludes to Zechariah, and the whole line of the righteous dead stands between them. Jesus lamented Jerusalem as the city “that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it” (Matt 23:37). Stephen would soon be stoned for the same witness (Acts 7:58–59), and Paul was stoned at Lystra and left for dead (Acts 14:19; 2 Cor 11:25). Jewish tradition outside Scripture also held that Jeremiah was stoned by his own people in Egypt, though Scripture does not record his death.

“They were sawn in two.” ἐπρίσθησαν epristhēsan, from πρίζω prizō, “saw, saw in two.” No Old Testament text records such a death. The reference is almost certainly to the tradition of the martyrdom of Isaiah under King Manasseh.

Historical Background: The Martyrdom of Isaiah

Isaiah prophesied, according to the opening of his book, in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (Isa 1:1). Hezekiah’s son Manasseh, who reigned for fifty-five years, was the most wicked of Judah’s kings, and Scripture says that he “shed very much innocent blood, until he had filled Jerusalem from one end to the other” (2 Kgs 21:16). Josephus adds that Manasseh did not spare even the prophets, but killed some of them every day (Ant. 10.38). A Jewish tradition, preserved in the writing known as the Ascension of Isaiah (whose earliest Jewish portion, the “Martyrdom of Isaiah,” may go back to the period before Christ, though the document as we have it includes later Christian additions), tells that Manasseh had Isaiah sawn in two with a wooden saw, and that the prophet neither cried out nor wept but spoke with the Holy Spirit until he was sawn apart (Ascension of Isaiah 5:1–14). Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, could reproach his Jewish dialogue partner with the claim that his people had sawn Isaiah asunder with a wooden saw (Dialogue with Trypho 120), and later rabbinic tradition also tells of Isaiah’s death by sawing during Manasseh’s reign. The tradition was evidently widespread among Jews and Christians of the first centuries.

This is tradition, not Scripture. Hebrews does not name Isaiah, and nothing in the argument depends on the details. But the tradition is early, consistent with what Scripture says about Manasseh’s bloodshed, and widely known, and it is likely that the author and his readers had Isaiah in mind. The prophet who saw the Lord “high and lifted up” (Isa 6:1) and foretold the Servant who “was pierced for our transgressions” (Isa 53:5) is remembered in this tradition as dying for his witness.

Textual Note: “They Were Tempted”

Many manuscripts include another verb in this list: ἐπειράσθησαν epeirasthēsan, “they were tempted” or “tested.” It appears in different positions in different manuscripts, sometimes before and sometimes after “they were sawn in two,” while some early witnesses, including the important papyrus known as P46 (about AD 200), and some early versions lack it altogether. The SBL Greek New Testament, followed in this study, omits it, as does the critical text used in most modern translations. Bruce Metzger’s Textual Commentary explains the reasoning: a word that wanders from place to place in the manuscript tradition is usually a later addition, and “they were tempted” is a weak and general term in a list of specific violent deaths. The most likely explanation is that it arose by accident, from a scribe’s repetition of the very similar-looking epristhēsan, “they were sawn,” which differs by only a few letters. Several scholars have also suggested that the original may have been ἐπρήσθησαν eprēsthēsan, “they were burned,” which would fit the Maccabean background (2 Macc 6:11; 7:3–5), but this is a conjecture without manuscript support. No point of doctrine is affected; the shorter reading is to be preferred.

“They died by murder with the sword.” ἐν φόνῳ μαχαίρης ἀπέθανον en phonō machairēs apethanon, literally “they died by the slaughter of the sword.” φόνος phonos means “killing, murder.” Elijah complained that Israel had “killed your prophets with the sword” (1 Kgs 19:10, 14), and Jezebel had cut off the prophets of the Lord (1 Kgs 18:4). Jeremiah records the death of a lesser-known prophet, Uriah son of Shemaiah, who prophesied the same message as Jeremiah, fled to Egypt, was brought back, and was executed by King Jehoiakim: וַיַּכֵּהוּ בֶּחָרֶב וַיַּשְׁלֵךְ אֶת־נִבְלָתוֹ אֶל־קִבְרֵי בְּנֵי הָעָם vayyakkehu becherev vayyashlekh et-nivlato el-qivre bene ha‘am, “and he struck him down with the sword and threw his dead body into the burial place of the common people” (Jer 26:23). The contrast with 11:34 is deliberate and moving: some “escaped the edge of the sword,” and some “died by the sword.” Both did so “through faith.” James the brother of John would die by the sword (Acts 12:2), while Peter, in the very same chapter, was delivered from prison (Acts 12:6–11). God’s purposes for his servants differ; their faith is the same.

“They went about in sheepskins, in goatskins.” The verse now turns from death to a life of deprivation. περιῆλθον periēlthon, “they went around, wandered about,” suggests homelessness. μηλωτή mēlōtē means “sheepskin” and was the word used in the Greek Old Testament for Elijah’s mantle, which he wrapped around his face at Horeb (1 Kgs 19:13), cast upon Elisha to call him (1 Kgs 19:19), and used to strike the Jordan before he was taken up (2 Kgs 2:8, 13–14). The Hebrew word for that mantle is אַדֶּרֶת adderet, a cloak; Elijah himself was known as “a hairy man” with a leather belt (2 Kgs 1:8). Zechariah speaks of the prophet’s hairy cloak as the recognized garb of the prophetic office: וְלֹא יִלְבְּשׁוּ אַדֶּרֶת שֵׂעָר לְמַעַן כַּחֵשׁ velo yilbeshu adderet se‘ar lema‘an kachesh, “and they will not put on a hairy cloak in order to deceive” (Zech 13:4). John the Baptist wore clothing of camel’s hair (Matt 3:4). “Goatskins” (αἰγείοις δέρμασιν aigeiois dermasin) adds a still rougher image. Such garments were the clothing of the poorest and of those living in the wild. Clement of Rome, echoing this verse, urges his readers to imitate “those who went about in goatskins and sheepskins, proclaiming the coming of Christ,” and names Elijah, Elisha, and Ezekiel (1 Clem 17:1).

Three present participles describe their condition: ὑστερούμενοι hysteroumenoi, “lacking, in want, destitute”; θλιβόμενοι thlibomenoi, “pressed, afflicted, oppressed”; κακουχούμενοι kakouchoumenoi, “mistreated, tormented.” The present tense portrays their sufferings as ongoing conditions. The last word picks up the verb Moses chose in 11:25 (“to be mistreated along with the people of God”) and anticipates 13:3 (“those who are mistreated”). The noun related to the second participle, θλῖψις thlipsis, “affliction,” was used of the readers’ own experience in 10:33. Elijah fed by ravens and by a poor widow (1 Kgs 17:2–16), the prophets hidden in caves and fed on bread and water (1 Kgs 18:4), the faithful in the Maccabean period fleeing to the wilderness: all fit this description.

11:38

people of whom the world was not worthy—wandering in deserts and mountains and caves and the holes of the earth.

Before completing his description of the homeless faithful, the author interrupts himself with a verdict: ὧν οὐκ ἦν ἄξιος ὁ κόσμος hōn ouk ēn axios ho kosmos, “of whom the world was not worthy.” The adjective ἄξιος axios originally meant “weighing as much as,” and so “worthy, of equal value.” The world thought these people unworthy of a place in it; it drove them out, clothed in skins, into the deserts. The author turns the judgment around: it was the world that was not worthy of them. On the scales of God, these destitute wanderers outweighed the whole world that rejected them.

The “world” (κόσμος kosmos) here means human society organized apart from God, as when Noah by his faith “condemned the world” (11:7). It is the same world that rejected Jesus (John 1:10; 15:18–19). The verdict echoes a thought found in the Wisdom of Solomon, which says of the righteous who were persecuted and seemed to perish that God tested them and found them worthy of himself (Wis 3:5). Hebrews has already said that God “is not ashamed to be called their God” (11:16). The people the world despised are the people God honors. This reversal of values lies at the heart of the gospel: the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone (Ps 118:22; Matt 21:42), and Jesus himself suffered “outside the gate” as one cast out by his own city (13:12).

The sentence then resumes with a final participle: ἐπὶ ἐρημίαις πλανώμενοι epi erēmiais planōmenoi, “wandering over deserts.” πλανάω planaō in the passive means “to wander, roam,” the restless movement of those who have no settled home. Four places are named: deserts (ἐρημίαι erēmiai, desolate places), mountains (ὄρη orē), caves (σπήλαια spēlaia), and “the holes of the earth” (ταῖς ὀπαῖς τῆς γῆς tais opais tēs gēs; ὀπή opē means an opening or hole, perhaps a crevice or den in the ground).

Scripture supplies many examples. When Jezebel was killing the prophets of the Lord, Obadiah, the steward of Ahab’s household, hid a hundred prophets, fifty to a cave, and fed them with bread and water (1 Kgs 18:4, 13). Elijah fled into the wilderness and then to a cave at Horeb, the mountain of God (1 Kgs 19:4, 9). David, hunted by Saul, hid in the cave of Adullam (1 Sam 22:1) and in the caves of En-gedi (1 Sam 24:3); two psalms bear superscriptions placing them there: לְדָוִד מִכְתָּם בְּבָרְחוֹ מִפְּנֵי־שָׁאוּל בַּמְּעָרָה leDavid mikhtam bevorcho mippene-Sha’ul bamme‘arah, “Of David, a miktam, when he fled from Saul, in the cave” (Ps 57, superscription; compare Ps 142). The author may also be thinking of the Maccabean era, when Mattathias and his sons fled to the mountains, leaving all they had in the city, and many who sought righteousness and justice went down to live in the wilderness with their children, wives, and livestock (1 Macc 2:28–31). Second Maccabees adds that Judas Maccabeus and his companions lived in the mountains like wild animals, eating what grew wild so as not to be defiled (2 Macc 5:27), and that others who had gathered in caves to keep the Sabbath secretly were betrayed and burned (2 Macc 6:11).

These lines complete the portrait of faith that Hebrews has been painting since Abraham: God’s people are “strangers and exiles on the earth” (11:13), seeking a homeland (11:14), with “no lasting city here” (13:14). The homeless prophets in the deserts are the extreme expression of that pilgrim identity. The author does not romanticize their suffering; deserts, caves, and holes in the ground are hard places. But he insists that their faith there was precious to God, and that the world which drove them out was the poorer for it. Jesus, who had “nowhere to lay his head” (Matt 8:20), walked the same road. For readers who might soon lose homes, property, and standing for Christ, these verses offered both realism and honor: if it comes to that, you will be in the best of company.
