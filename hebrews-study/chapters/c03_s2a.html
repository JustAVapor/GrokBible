Today, If You Hear His Voice (3:7–19)

This section (3:7–4:13) continues in chapter 4.

Translation

7Therefore, just as the Holy Spirit says,

“Today, if you hear his voice,

8do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion,

on the day of testing in the wilderness,

9where your fathers put me to the test by scrutiny

and saw my works

10for forty years.

Therefore I was angry with this generation,

and I said, ‘They always go astray in their heart,

and they have not known my ways.’

11So I swore in my wrath,

‘They shall not enter my rest.’”

12See to it, brothers, that there will not be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, shown in falling away from the living God. 13But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called “Today,” so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin. 14For we have become partakers of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end, 15as it is said,

“Today, if you hear his voice,

do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion.”

16For who were they who heard and rebelled? Was it not all those who came out of Egypt through Moses? 17And with whom was he angry for forty years? Was it not with those who sinned, whose corpses fell in the wilderness? 18And to whom did he swear that they would not enter his rest, if not to those who disobeyed? 19So we see that they were not able to enter because of unbelief.

Setting and Structure

The short exposition of Numbers 12:7 (3:1–6) ended with a condition: “his house is what we are, if we hold fast.” The author now turns that condition into a long, searching exhortation. It is built on Psalm 95:7–11 and runs from 3:7 to 4:13. This is the second of the sermon’s great warning passages (after 2:1–4). It is also its most sustained piece of Old Testament exposition so far. The section is best read as a unit. The half that falls in chapter 3 deals mainly with the psalm’s warning against hardening and unbelief. The half in chapter 4 deals mainly with its promise of rest and its word “Today.”

The link with 3:1–6 is natural. Moses was faithful in God’s house, but the generation Moses led out of Egypt was not. Psalm 95 tells their story. The faithful servant led an unfaithful people, and they died in the wilderness. The readers belong to a better house under a greater leader, but the same danger confronts them. They too have been redeemed, have heard God’s voice, and stand on the threshold of a promised inheritance. Will they, like the wilderness generation, harden their hearts and fall short?

The section in chapter 3 unfolds in three movements:

  • 3:7–11: The text. Psalm 95:7b–11 is quoted, introduced as the present speech of the Holy Spirit. The quotation follows the Greek version of the psalm closely, with a few small but significant differences (see 3:9 and 3:10).
  • 3:12–15: The application. The author draws out the psalm’s warning for the congregation. He warns against an evil heart of unbelief (3:12), commands daily mutual exhortation while it is still “Today” (3:13), restates the condition of 3:6 (3:14), and repeats the opening lines of the psalm (3:15).
  • 3:16–19: The history. Three pairs of questions and answers, drawn from Numbers 14, identify who rebelled, with whom God was angry, and to whom he swore. A conclusion follows: “they were not able to enter because of unbelief” (3:19).

Several word links show how carefully the passage is composed. The word “unbelief” (ἀπιστία apistia) appears in 3:12 and again in the last word of 3:19, framing the application and the history. The verb “see” (βλέπω blepō) does the same: “See to it” (3:12) and “we see” (3:19). The psalm’s key words, “today,” “hear,” “voice,” “harden,” “heart,” “forty years,” “swore,” “enter,” and “rest,” are taken up one after another in the commentary. This way of explaining a text by repeating and applying its own words, phrase by phrase, resembles the Jewish exposition later called midrash. Similar methods appear in the Dead Sea Scrolls, where biblical texts are applied phrase by phrase to the community’s own situation. Hebrews applies the psalm to the church, which lives in the last days that the psalm’s “Today” anticipated.

The historical background is the wilderness wandering, especially the decisive failure at Kadesh-barnea told in Numbers 13–14. Twelve spies were sent into Canaan. Ten of them reported that the land was good but its people too strong. The congregation wept, grumbled against Moses and Aaron, and proposed to choose a leader and return to Egypt (Num 14:1–4). Only Joshua and Caleb urged faith in the Lord. Then God swore that the generation that had seen his glory and his signs, and yet tested him “these ten times,” would not see the land, and that their bodies would fall in the wilderness over forty years (Num 14:20–35). The parallels with the readers of Hebrews are striking. Like Israel, they had been delivered and had seen God’s mighty works (2:4). Like Israel, they now faced hardship and opposition at the edge of the promise. Like Israel, they were tempted to look back to the familiar securities of the past. The author holds up the wilderness generation as a warning, as Paul also does (1 Cor 10:1–13): “these things happened to them as examples, and they were written down for our instruction, on whom the ends of the ages have come” (1 Cor 10:11).

Verse by Verse

3:7

Therefore, just as the Holy Spirit says, “Today, if you hear his voice,

“Therefore” (διό dio) draws the consequence of 3:1–6. Since we are God’s house only if we hold fast, we must heed the warning of the psalm. Grammatically, the “therefore” of 3:7 has no main verb until the command “See to it” in 3:12. The whole quotation (3:7b–11) is in effect an extended parenthesis: “Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says, … see to it, brothers.” Some interpreters attach “therefore” instead to the first command within the quotation, “do not harden your hearts” (3:8). Either way, the psalm is not quoted merely as an illustration. Its warnings are now addressed to the readers.

The quotation is introduced with the words “just as the Holy Spirit says” (καθὼς λέγει τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον kathōs legei to pneuma to hagion). Two features of this formula are important. First, the speaker is the Holy Spirit. The author knows the psalm has a human writer; in 4:7 he will say that God spoke “in David.” But he regards the Spirit as the true author of Scripture, who speaks through the human writer (compare 9:8; 10:15; 2 Pet 1:21; Acts 1:16; 4:25). Second, the verb is present tense: the Spirit says, not “said.” For Hebrews, Scripture is not a dead record of past speech. It is the living voice of God addressed to the present generation of hearers (compare 4:12: “the word of God is living and active”). The psalm addressed the worshipers of ancient Israel. Now, through the same words, the Spirit addresses the church.

The quotation begins with the word that will dominate the exposition: “Today” (σήμερον sēmeron). The word stands first for emphasis, as it does in the Hebrew (הַיּוֹם hayyom). In the psalm, “today” was the day when Israel gathered for worship and heard God’s word. The author will argue in 4:6–9 that the psalm’s “Today” shows that God’s offer of rest remained open long after Joshua’s conquest. It is still open now, as long as it is called “Today” (3:13). The readers live in that day of opportunity. The same word appeared in 1:5 (“today I have begotten you”), where it pointed to the decisive day of the Son’s enthronement. Since that day, God’s “Today” of invitation has been sounding.

“If you hear his voice” (ἐὰν τῆς φωνῆς αὐτοῦ ἀκούσητε ean tēs phōnēs autou akousēte). The Hebrew of Psalm 95:7, אִם־בְּקֹלוֹ תִשְׁמָעוּ im-beqolo tishma’u, can be read either as a condition (“if you listen to his voice”) or as a wish (“O that you would listen to his voice!”). The Greek uses the regular conditional form. In Hebrew idiom, to “listen to the voice” (shama’ beqol) means more than hearing sounds. It means to heed, to obey. That was the condition of the covenant at Sinai: “if you will truly listen to my voice and keep my covenant, you will be my treasured possession” (Exod 19:5). It was exactly what the wilderness generation failed to do: they “have not listened to my voice” (Num 14:22). In Hebrews, “his voice” is the voice of God speaking in his Son (1:2). The sermon ends with a warning that echoes this verse: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking… whose voice then shook the earth” (12:25–26).

Historical Background: Psalm 95 in Israel’s Worship

Psalm 95 begins as a joyful call to worship: “Come, let us sing for joy to the Lord; let us shout aloud to the rock of our salvation” (95:1). The worshipers are invited to bow down before their Maker, “for he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture and the flock of his hand” (95:6–7). Then, abruptly, the tone changes. A prophetic voice speaks in God’s name and warns the worshipers not to repeat the sin of their ancestors. The psalm thus combines praise and warning in a way that suits Hebrews exactly. The congregation that gathers to worship is the same congregation that must be warned against hardening.

The Hebrew psalm has no heading. The Greek version adds a superscription attributing it to David, which explains why Hebrews 4:7 can speak of God “saying in David, so long afterward” (that is, centuries after Joshua). Whether the author regarded David as the writer or simply referred to the Psalter by David’s name, his point stands. Long after Israel entered Canaan, God was still offering his rest and still warning against unbelief.

3:8

do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, on the day of testing in the wilderness,

The command is “do not harden your hearts” (μὴ σκληρύνητε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν mē sklērynēte tas kardias hymōn). The verb σκληρύνω sklērynō means to make hard, stiff, or unyielding. It comes from the adjective sklēros, “hard, rough” (compare the English “sclerosis,” a hardening of tissue). The Hebrew verb in Psalm 95:8, תַּקְשׁוּ taqshu (from qashah), is the same verb used when God says, “I will harden Pharaoh’s heart” (וַאֲנִי אַקְשֶׁה אֶת־לֵב פַּרְעֹה va’ani aqsheh et-lev par’oh, Exod 7:3). It is related to the description of Israel as a “stiff-necked” people (קְשֵׁה־עֹרֶף qesheh-oref, Exod 32:9), like an ox that will not bend its neck to the yoke. The irony is sharp. The people God delivered from a hard-hearted Pharaoh became hard-hearted themselves.

In biblical language the “heart” is not merely the seat of emotion. It is the center of the whole inner person: thought, will, desire, and moral choice. A hardened heart is one that has become insensitive to God’s word, so that the voice of God no longer penetrates or moves it. Notice that the psalm addresses the hearers’ responsibility. “Do not harden your hearts” is a command to people who are able to resist hardening by responding to God’s voice. Later the author will describe hardening as something that happens to a person through “the deceitfulness of sin” (3:13). Scripture holds both sides together: we harden ourselves when we refuse God’s word, and refusal makes us still harder.

The hardening took place “as in the rebellion, on the day of testing in the wilderness.” Here the Greek text quoted by Hebrews differs from the Hebrew in an instructive way.

Old Testament Text: Psalm 95:7–8

Hebrew: הַיּוֹם אִם־בְּקֹלוֹ תִשְׁמָעוּ׃ אַל־תַּקְשׁוּ לְבַבְכֶם כִּמְרִיבָה כְּיוֹם מַסָּה בַּמִּדְבָּר׃ — hayyom im-beqolo tishma’u. al-taqshu levavkhem kimrivah, keyom massah bammidbar, “Today, if you would listen to his voice! Do not harden your heart as at Meribah, as on the day of Massah in the wilderness.”

In the Hebrew, Meribah and Massah are place names. They mean “Quarreling” (from riv, “to contend, dispute, bring a lawsuit”) and “Testing” (from nasah, “to test, try”). The names come from the incident at Rephidim early in the wilderness journey, when the people demanded water and Moses struck the rock. “He called the name of the place Massah and Meribah, because of the quarreling of the sons of Israel and because they tested the Lord, saying, ‘Is the Lord among us or not?’” (וַיִּקְרָא שֵׁם הַמָּקוֹם מַסָּה וּמְרִיבָה vayyiqra shem hammaqom massah umerivah, Exod 17:7). A second incident near the end of the forty years, at Kadesh, also produced “the waters of Meribah” (מֵי מְרִיבָה mei merivah, Num 20:13). That second incident was when Moses himself failed and was barred from entering the land. The two place names thus mark the beginning and the end of the wilderness years, and both stand for Israel’s quarrelsome distrust.

The Greek version of the psalm, followed by Hebrews, translates the place names as ordinary nouns: “as in the rebellion” (ὡς ἐν τῷ παραπικρασμῷ hōs en tō parapikrasmō) and “on the day of testing” (κατὰ τὴν ἡμέραν τοῦ πειρασμοῦ kata tēn hēmeran tou peirasmou). Peirasmos, “testing,” is a fair rendering of Massah. Parapikrasmos is a rarer word. It is related to the verb parapikrainō, “to embitter, provoke,” which the Greek Old Testament often uses for Israel’s rebellion against God (for example, Ps 78:17, 40, 56 [Greek Ps 77]). It is not an exact translation of “quarreling,” but it captures the inner meaning of the event. Israel’s quarrel with Moses was really a provocation of God.

How does this affect the meaning? In the Hebrew the psalm points to specific places on the map. In the Greek it points to a pattern of behavior: rebellion and testing. That broader rendering suits the author’s purpose well. When he explains the psalm in 3:16–19, he does not discuss Massah and Meribah at all. He goes straight to the rebellion at Kadesh-barnea in Numbers 14, where the oath of exclusion was sworn. The Greek rendering does not change the sense of the Hebrew. It generalizes it, gathering all the wilderness provocations, from the first at Massah to the decisive one at Kadesh, under the heading “the rebellion.” The two are fully compatible. The specific incidents in the Hebrew are examples of the persistent rebellion the Greek names.

One more feature deserves notice. The Hebrew says “your heart” (singular, levavkhem), treating the people as one body with one heart. The Greek says “your hearts” (plural). Hebrews will apply the warning both to the congregation as a whole and to “any one of you” (3:12, 13). Hardening can begin with a single member and spread through the body (compare 12:15: “that no root of bitterness springing up causes trouble, and by it many are defiled”). That is why mutual care matters so much in the verses that follow.

3:9

where your fathers put me to the test by scrutiny and saw my works

The word “where” (οὗ hou) refers back to “the wilderness.” It was there that “your fathers,” the generation of the exodus, “put me to the test” (ἐπείρασαν epeirasan, from peirazō). The speaker is now God himself, speaking in the first person through the psalm. To “test” God in this sense is not to seek honestly to know him. It is to challenge him, to demand that he prove himself on our terms, and to make our trust conditional on his meeting our demands. At Massah the people asked, “Is the Lord among us or not?” (Exod 17:7). Moses had warned them, “Why do you test the Lord?” (Exod 17:2). The law later forbade it: “You shall not test the Lord your God, as you tested him at Massah” (לֹא תְנַסּוּ אֶת־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם כַּאֲשֶׁר נִסִּיתֶם בַּמַּסָּה lo tenassu et-YHWH eloheikhem ka’asher nissitem bammassah, Deut 6:16). Jesus quoted that very command when the devil urged him to throw himself from the temple (Matt 4:7). Where Israel tested God in the wilderness, the Son, tested in the wilderness, refused to test God and trusted him. He is the faithful one where the fathers were faithless (compare 2:18; 4:15).

The phrase “by scrutiny” translates ἐν δοκιμασίᾳ en dokimasia. The noun dokimasia means examination, testing to establish whether something is genuine, the kind of scrutiny applied to metals or to candidates for public office. In the Greek version of the psalm the line reads with a verb, “they tried me” (edokimasan), matching the Hebrew בְּחָנוּנִי bechanuni, “they examined me.” Hebrews has the noun instead, “in examination” or “by scrutiny.” The meaning is unchanged. The people subjected God to suspicious examination, as if he were on trial and had to prove his trustworthiness to them. Hebrews’ text also lacks an explicit object “me” after “tested.” The object is clearly implied by the context, since God is the speaker, and I have supplied it in the translation.

And they “saw my works” (εἶδον τὰ ἔργα μου eidon ta erga mou). This is the heart of their guilt. They did not test God for lack of evidence. They had seen the plagues on Egypt, the parting of the sea, the pillar of cloud and fire, the manna each morning, and water from the rock. The Hebrew can be translated “even though they had seen my work” (גַּם־רָאוּ פָעֳלִי gam-ra’u fo’oli). In Numbers 14:22 God himself made the same point: these are “the men who have seen my glory and my signs, which I did in Egypt and in the wilderness, and yet have tested me these ten times and have not listened to my voice.” The more God had shown them, the more inexcusable their distrust.

The readers of Hebrews were in the same position. God had confirmed the gospel to them “by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit” (2:4). They had “tasted the heavenly gift” and “the powers of the age to come” (6:4–5). To see God’s works and still demand that he prove himself is the hallmark of the hardened heart.

Textual Note

Many later manuscripts conform Hebrews 3:9 to the wording of the Greek psalm, reading “where your fathers tested me, they tried me” (adding “me” and replacing the noun “by scrutiny” with the verb “they tried”). The earliest witnesses, including the papyrus P46 and Codex Vaticanus, read “tested by scrutiny” without “me.” Scribes more often harmonized New Testament quotations with the familiar Old Testament text than the reverse, so the reading that differs from the psalm is probably original. The sense is the same in both.

3:10

for forty years. Therefore I was angry with this generation, and I said, ‘They always go astray in their heart, and they have not known my ways.’

This verse contains the most interesting difference between the quotation in Hebrews and the psalm as it stands in both Hebrew and Greek. In the psalm, “forty years” belongs with what follows: “For forty years I loathed that generation.” In Hebrews, the author has inserted a single word, “therefore” (διό dio), after “forty years.” The phrase “forty years” is thereby attached to what precedes: the fathers “saw my works for forty years. Therefore I was angry with this generation.”

Old Testament Text: Psalm 95:9–11

Hebrew: אֲשֶׁר נִסּוּנִי אֲבוֹתֵיכֶם בְּחָנוּנִי גַּם־רָאוּ פָעֳלִי׃ אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה אָקוּט בְּדוֹר וָאֹמַר עַם תֹּעֵי לֵבָב הֵם וְהֵם לֹא־יָדְעוּ דְרָכָי׃ אֲשֶׁר־נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי בְאַפִּי אִם־יְבֹאוּן אֶל־מְנוּחָתִי׃

Transliteration: asher nissuni avoteikhem, bechanuni, gam-ra’u fo’oli. arba’im shanah aqut bedor va’omar, am to’ei levav hem, vehem lo-yade’u derakhai. asher-nishba’ti ve’appi, im-yevo’un el-menuchati.

Translation: “when your fathers tested me; they examined me, though they had seen my work. Forty years I loathed a generation, and I said, ‘They are a people who go astray in heart, and they have not known my ways.’ So I swore in my anger, ‘They shall not enter my rest.’”

Several features of the Hebrew should be noted. (1) The verb אָקוּט aqut (from qut) is strong. It means to feel loathing or disgust. The Greek renders it with προσοχθίζω prosochthizō, “to be angry, be offended, be provoked,” which Hebrews follows. (2) The Hebrew says “a generation” (bedor) without a demonstrative. The Greek psalm has “that generation,” and Hebrews has “this generation” (see below). (3) Where the Hebrew reads “a people who go astray in heart” (am to’ei levav), the Greek has “they always go astray in heart.” The sense is very similar. In both, Israel’s problem was a persistent inner wandering. (4) In the Hebrew, as in the Greek psalm, “forty years” stands at the head of the clause about God’s loathing. Hebrews’ inserted “therefore” moves it to the previous clause about seeing God’s works.

Why did the author make this change? He was not ignorant of the psalm’s wording. In 3:17 he asks, “with whom was he angry for forty years?”, joining “forty years” to God’s anger, exactly as the psalm does. So he knows that both statements are true. For forty years Israel saw God’s works, and for forty years God was angry with them. The two overlapped. Throughout the whole period of judgment, God kept on feeding, guiding, and protecting the very people who were under his displeasure. In 3:10 the author highlights the first truth for his hearers. The fathers did not sin after a brief glimpse of God’s power. They lived with it day after day for forty years, and their unbelief persisted. The added “therefore” makes the logic explicit. God’s anger was the consequence of their persistent unbelief in the face of persistent grace.

Some interpreters have wondered whether the author also saw a parallel in the timing of his own day. If Hebrews was written in the 60s, nearly forty years had passed since Jesus’ ministry, death, and resurrection around AD 30. A later rabbinic opinion recorded in the Babylonian Talmud (b. Sanh. 99a) held that the days of the Messiah would last forty years, appealing to this very verse. We cannot know whether such ideas were in the author’s mind, and he does not say so. The text itself makes the point that matters. Like the fathers, the readers had seen God’s works over a long period, and like the fathers, they must not let familiarity breed unbelief.

“Therefore I was angry with this generation” (διὸ προσώχθισα τῇ γενεᾷ ταύτῃ dio prosōchthisa tē genea tautē). Hebrews reads “this generation,” where the Greek psalm has “that generation” (ekeinē). Later manuscripts of Hebrews conform to the psalm, but “this” is well attested in the earliest manuscripts. The change may be slight, but it suits the author’s purpose of bringing the psalm into the present. Jesus used the same phrase, “this generation,” of his own unbelieving contemporaries (Matt 11:16; 12:39–45; Luke 11:29–32), and he called them, echoing Moses (Deut 32:5, 20), a “faithless and perverse generation” (Matt 17:17).

God’s verdict is twofold. First, “They always go astray in their heart” (ἀεὶ πλανῶνται τῇ καρδίᾳ aei planōntai tē kardia). The verb πλανάω planaō means to wander, to go astray, to be led astray. It is the root of the English “planet,” which in ancient terms meant a “wandering star.” There is a tragic fitness here. The generation whose hearts wandered from God was condemned to wander in the wilderness for forty years. Their outward wandering mirrored their inner state. The present tense and the word “always” stress the habitual character of their straying.

Second, “they have not known my ways” (αὐτοὶ δὲ οὐκ ἔγνωσαν τὰς ὁδούς μου autoi de ouk egnōsan tas hodous mou). In the Bible, to “know” God’s ways is not merely to know facts about him. It is to understand his purposes and character and to walk in them. Psalm 103:7 draws a revealing contrast: “He made known his ways to Moses, his deeds to the sons of Israel” (יוֹדִיעַ דְּרָכָיו לְמֹשֶׁה לִבְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל עֲלִילוֹתָיו yodia derakhav leMosheh, livnei Yisra’el alilotav). Moses had prayed, “Make known to me your ways, that I may know you” (Exod 33:13). The people saw God’s deeds, but they never came to know his ways. They saw what God did without understanding who he was. They witnessed his power without learning to trust his heart. That distinction remains searching. It is possible to see God at work, even to benefit from his works, without ever knowing him.

3:11

So I swore in my wrath, ‘They shall not enter my rest.’”

The quotation ends with God’s oath. The connecting word ὡς hōs, here “so” or “accordingly,” renders the Hebrew asher, which in this context marks the result: their persistent straying led to God’s solemn decree. “I swore in my wrath” (ὤμοσα ἐν τῇ ὀργῇ μου ōmosa en tē orgē mou). The Hebrew בְאַפִּי ve’appi means literally “in my nose,” a vivid Hebrew idiom for anger, drawn from the flared nostrils and heavy breathing of an angry person. God’s wrath in Scripture is not a loss of temper. It is his settled, holy opposition to sin, and it is the other side of his covenant love. He is angry because he is grieved by the faithlessness of his people.

The oath itself is phrased in a way that sounds strange in English. Literally it reads, “If they shall enter my rest!” (Εἰ εἰσελεύσονται εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσίν μου Ei eiseleusontai eis tēn katapausin mou). This is a Hebrew oath formula carried over word for word into Greek. In Hebrew, solemn oaths were often expressed as a self-curse with the curse left unspoken: “[May such-and-such happen to me] if they enter my rest.” A positive “if” clause thus amounts to an emphatic negative, “They shall certainly not enter.” The Hebrew אִם־יְבֹאוּן אֶל־מְנוּחָתִי im-yevo’un el-menuchati uses exactly this form, and so does God’s oath in Numbers 14:23: אִם־יִרְאוּ אֶת־הָאָרֶץ im-yir’u et-ha’arets, “[I swear that] they shall not see the land.” For the reader, the point is that God’s word here is irrevocable. When God swears, the matter is settled (compare 6:13–18; 7:20–22).

Historical Background: The Oath at Kadesh-barnea

The oath the psalm recalls was sworn at Kadesh-barnea, in the wilderness south of Canaan, in the second year after the exodus (Num 10:11; 13:26; 14:20–35; compare Deut 1:19–46). After the spies’ report, the people wept all night and said, “If only we had died in the land of Egypt, or in this wilderness, if only we had died!” They spoke of appointing a leader and returning to Egypt (Num 14:2–4). When Joshua and Caleb pleaded with them not to rebel against the Lord, the congregation talked of stoning them (Num 14:6–10).

God’s response took the form of an oath: “As I live,” declares the Lord, “what you have spoken in my hearing, so I will do to you. In this wilderness your corpses shall fall” (Num 14:28–29). Every one counted in the census, from twenty years old and upward, except Caleb and Joshua, would die in the desert. Their children would be “shepherds in the wilderness forty years,” “a year for each day” of the forty days the spies had spent exploring the land (Num 14:33–34). Deuteronomy records the outcome. Thirty-eight years passed between Kadesh-barnea and the crossing of the brook Zered, “until the whole generation, the men of war, had perished from the camp, as the Lord had sworn to them” (Deut 2:14).

There is a further detail in this story that Hebrews’ readers would have found painfully relevant. When the people heard God’s sentence, they changed their minds and tried to go up into the land on their own, without the ark and without Moses. They were defeated by the Amalekites and Canaanites (Num 14:39–45). They learned too late that it is not possible to enter God’s rest on one’s own terms after refusing his word. The moment of opportunity had passed. This is part of the background of the warning that the “Today” of opportunity will not last forever (3:13; compare 12:17).

The object of the oath is “my rest” (τὴν κατάπαυσίν μου tēn katapausin mou). The Greek κατάπαυσις katapausis, “rest,” renders the Hebrew מְנוּחָה menuchah, “resting place, rest.” In the Pentateuch and Joshua, the “rest” was first of all the land of Canaan, where Israel would live in peace and security with God dwelling among them. Moses told Israel on the plains of Moab, “you have not yet come to the rest and the inheritance that the Lord your God is giving you” (כִּי לֹא־בָּאתֶם עַד־עָתָּה אֶל־הַמְּנוּחָה וְאֶל־הַנַּחֲלָה ki lo-ba’tem ad-attah el-hammenuchah ve’el-hannachalah, Deut 12:9). Later, the same word was used of the temple as God’s resting place: “Arise, Lord, to your resting place” (Ps 132:8, 14).

But notice the possessive. God calls it “my rest.” It is not only Israel’s rest in the land but God’s own rest, the rest he shares with his people. In chapter 4 the author will develop this. He will link “my rest” to God’s own Sabbath rest after creation (Gen 2:2), and he will argue that since David still spoke of the rest centuries after Joshua, the rest offered in the psalm must be something greater than Canaan. It is a “Sabbath-rest” that “remains for the people of God” (4:8–9). For now, the reader is left with the solemn words of the oath ringing in the ears: “They shall not enter my rest.” The rest of chapter 3 explains who “they” were and why they failed, so that the readers will not follow them.
