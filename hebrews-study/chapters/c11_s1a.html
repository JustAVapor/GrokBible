The Faith That Pleases God (11:1–40)

Translation

1Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen. 2For by it the elders received testimony.

3By faith we understand that the ages were fashioned by the word of God, so that what is seen has not come into being out of things that appear.

4By faith Abel offered to God a better sacrifice than Cain, through which he received testimony that he was righteous, God bearing witness over his gifts; and through it, though he died, he still speaks. 5By faith Enoch was taken up so that he should not see death, and “he was not found, because God had taken him up.” For before his being taken up he had received testimony that he had pleased God. 6And without faith it is impossible to please him, for the one who draws near to God must believe that he exists and that he becomes a rewarder of those who seek him out. 7By faith Noah, having been warned by God concerning things not yet seen, in reverent fear constructed an ark for the salvation of his household; through this he condemned the world and became heir of the righteousness that is according to faith.

8By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed by going out to a place that he was going to receive as an inheritance; and he went out, not knowing where he was going. 9By faith he lived as a sojourner in the land of promise as in a foreign land, dwelling in tents with Isaac and Jacob, the fellow heirs of the same promise; 10for he was waiting for the city that has the foundations, whose designer and builder is God. 11By faith—together with Sarah herself—he received power to beget offspring, even beyond the season of life, since he considered faithful the one who had promised. 12Therefore from one man, and him as good as dead, there were born descendants as many as the stars of heaven in multitude and as the innumerable sand that is by the shore of the sea.

13In faith these all died, not having received the promises, but having seen them from afar and greeted them, and having confessed that they were strangers and exiles on the earth. 14For those who say such things make it plain that they are seeking a homeland. 15If they had been mindful of that land from which they had gone out, they would have had opportunity to return. 16But as it is, they long for a better one, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God; for he has prepared for them a city.

17By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac; he who had received the promises was offering up his only son, 18of whom it had been said,

“In Isaac your offspring shall be called,”

19for he reckoned that God was able to raise even from the dead; from there, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back. 20By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau, even concerning things to come. 21By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, and “worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff.” 22By faith Joseph, at his end, made mention of the exodus of the sons of Israel and gave instructions concerning his bones.

23By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden for three months by his parents, because they saw that the child was beautiful, and they were not afraid of the king’s edict. 24By faith Moses, when he had grown up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter, 25choosing rather to be mistreated with the people of God than to have the fleeting enjoyment of sin, 26considering the reproach of the Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt; for he was looking away to the reward. 27By faith he left Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king; for he endured as seeing him who is invisible. 28By faith he kept the Passover and the pouring of the blood, so that the destroyer of the firstborn would not touch them.

29By faith they passed through the Red Sea as through dry land, but the Egyptians, when they attempted it, were swallowed up. 30By faith the walls of Jericho fell after they had been encircled for seven days. 31By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had received the spies in peace.

32And what more shall I say? For time will fail me if I tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets, 33who through faith conquered kingdoms, worked righteousness, obtained promises, shut the mouths of lions, 34quenched the power of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, were made strong out of weakness, became mighty in war, put foreign armies to flight. 35Women received back their dead by resurrection. But others were tortured, not accepting release, so that they might obtain a better resurrection. 36Still others received the trial of mockings and floggings, and further of chains and imprisonment. 37They were stoned, they were sawn in two, they died by murder with the sword; they went about in sheepskins, in goatskins, in want, afflicted, mistreated— 38men of whom the world was not worthy—wandering in deserts and mountains and caves and the holes of the earth.

39And all these, though they received testimony through their faith, did not receive what was promised, 40since God had foreseen something better for us, so that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

Setting and Structure

Hebrews 11 is a single, carefully built unit framed by a deliberate “bookend” (what scholars call an inclusio). It opens, “For by it [faith] the elders received testimony” (ἐμαρτυρήθησαν emartyrēthēsan, 11:2), and it closes, “all these, though they received testimony (μαρτυρηθέντες martyrēthentes) through their faith, did not receive what was promised” (11:39). Everything between the two is an illustration of that one sentence: God has borne witness to people who trusted him. The same verb appears within the chapter at Abel (11:4, twice) and Enoch (11:5), and its noun-cousin “witnesses” launches the application in 12:1. The chapter is therefore not merely a gallery of heroes; it is a record of God’s verdicts.

The flow of the chapter can be traced as follows:

  • 11:1–3 — What faith is. A description of faith (v. 1), the thesis that God commended the elders for it (v. 2), and a first example drawn from “us,” the believing community: by faith we understand creation by the word of God (v. 3).
  • 11:4–7 — Faith before the flood. Abel, Enoch, and Noah, with a comment on the necessity of faith inserted in v. 6.
  • 11:8–22 — Abraham and the patriarchs. Abraham’s call, sojourning, and the gift of a son (vv. 8–12); a reflective pause on the patriarchs as pilgrims seeking a heavenly homeland (vv. 13–16); Abraham’s supreme test, the offering of Isaac (vv. 17–19); and three deathbed scenes in which Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph look forward to what God would do after their deaths (vv. 20–22).
  • 11:23–31 — Moses and the exodus generation. Moses’ parents, Moses’ choice to share the reproach of God’s people, the Passover, the Red Sea, Jericho, and Rahab.
  • 11:32–38 — A rapid survey. The preacher runs out of time and sweeps through the judges, David, Samuel, and the prophets, first in triumph (vv. 33–35a) and then in suffering, torture, and death (vv. 35b–38).
  • 11:39–40 — Conclusion. All received God’s testimony, none received the promise; God has planned something better that includes “us.”

Two features of this outline deserve notice. First, the preacher pauses twice to comment on his examples (v. 6 and vv. 13–16), and both pauses state principles the hearers must apply to themselves: faith is indispensable for pleasing God, and faith makes believers pilgrims who seek a better country. Second, the movement of the examples is not only chronological but also emotional. The early examples end in deliverance (Noah’s household saved, Isaac received back, Israel brought through the sea); the last ones end in chains, saws, and caves. The sequence teaches that faith is not measured by visible success. Some by faith “escaped the edge of the sword” (v. 34); others by the same faith “died by murder with the sword” (v. 37). Both are commended.

The examples are drawn almost entirely from the Old Testament, following the order of the biblical story from Genesis through Joshua, then Judges, Samuel, Kings, and the prophets. The sufferings in vv. 35b–38 probably reach beyond the Hebrew canon into the Maccabean period, when faithful Jews were tortured for refusing to abandon the law of God (2 Macc 6–7). The author draws on the Scriptures mostly in their Greek form, the Septuagint, the Bible his hearers used, and on two occasions (vv. 5 and 21) he reproduces the Septuagint’s wording outright. Where the Greek differs from the Hebrew, the verse notes below explain how.

The cultural setting sharpens the chapter’s force. In the Greco-Roman world a person’s worth was established by public reputation, the testimony of others. The hearers had lost that. They had been “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:33), had seen their property seized (10:34), and were tempted to retreat to safer ground. David deSilva has shown how thoroughly Hebrews addresses this world of honor and shame. Chapter 11 answers it by pointing to a higher court. The people it lists were often despised, displaced, and destitute; the world counted them nobodies, yet “the world was not worthy” of them (v. 38), and God himself testified to them and was “not ashamed to be called their God” (v. 16). To persevere in faith, then, is to prefer God’s verdict to the world’s.

Finally, the chapter must be read in the light of what surrounds it. It illustrates the righteous one who “shall live by faith” and does not “shrink back” (10:38–39), and it prepares for the call to “run with endurance” while “looking to Jesus” (12:1–2). Faith in Hebrews is never a mere opinion; it is trust in God’s promise that holds on, obeys, and keeps moving toward a future it cannot yet see. Every example in the chapter is a portrait of that kind of faith, and every portrait is meant to say to the weary hearer: you are not the first to walk this road, and the God who kept his word to them will keep it to you.

Verse by Verse

What Faith Is (11:1–3)

11:1

Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen.

The chapter opens with one of the most quoted sentences in the New Testament. In Greek it is a compact, almost proverbial line of eleven words: Ἔστιν δὲ πίστις ἐλπιζομένων ὑπόστασις, πραγμάτων ἔλεγχος οὐ βλεπομένων estin de pistis elpizomenōn hypostasis, pragmatōn elenchos ou blepomenōn. The verb “is” stands first, which is unusual and emphatic: “Faith is this.” The little word δέ de, “now,” links the sentence to what has just been said. The preacher has quoted Habakkuk, “my righteous one shall live by faith” (10:38), and has declared that “we are … of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul” (10:39). Now he explains what this faith is and what it does.

It is important to notice what kind of statement this is. It is not a full theological definition of saving faith. It does not mention Christ, forgiveness, or justification, all of which Hebrews treats elsewhere. It describes faith from one angle: its relation to the future and to the unseen. That is the angle the hearers needed, because their problem was not doctrinal ignorance but weariness. They had believed; they had suffered; and the reward had not yet arrived (10:32–36). The verse tells them that faith by its very nature deals with what is not yet in hand and not yet in view. Faith does not wait until the promise is visible; it lays hold of it before then. That is why every example that follows involves someone acting on a word from God about something they could not see.

The sentence has two balanced halves. Faith is (1) the ὑπόστασις hypostasis of things hoped for, and (2) the ἔλεγχος elenchos of things not seen. The two objects overlap. “Things hoped for” are future: the promised inheritance, the city, the resurrection (11:10, 16, 35). “Things not seen” include those future realities but also realities that are invisible because they belong to God’s realm, or lie in the past: the creation of the world by God’s word (11:3), the coming flood (11:7), and above all God himself, “him who is invisible” (11:27). Both participles are present tense (“things being hoped for,” “things not being seen”), describing the ongoing situation of believers in this age. The Greek word order is also artful: the phrase “of things … not seen” is split in two (pragmatōn … ou blepomenōn), wrapping itself around the word elenchos, so that the sentence ends on the decisive words “not seen.”

Word Study: ὑπόστασις hypostasis

The word is built from hypo, “under,” and stasis, “a standing,” and its root idea is “that which stands underneath”: a foundation, a support, the solid substance of a thing as opposed to its appearance. In ordinary Greek it could mean the sediment at the bottom of a liquid, the foundation of a building, the substance or reality of something, a plan or undertaking, and, in the papyri, documents bearing on a person’s property. It appears three times in Hebrews, and the other two uses frame the discussion. In 1:3 the Son is “the exact imprint of his essential being (hypostasis),” where the word plainly means God’s objective reality. In 3:14 believers are partners of Christ “if we hold the beginning of our confidence (hypostasis) firm to the end,” where most translators understand a subjective attitude, firm confidence. In the Greek Old Testament the word can stand for the Hebrew words for “hope.” In Psalm 39:8 (Hebrew numbering; English 39:7), where the psalmist says תּוֹחַלְתִּי לְךָ הִיא tochalti lekha hi, “my hope—it is in you,” the Septuagint renders “my hope” with hypostasis: my ground of expectation is in you.

Interpretive Options: Faith as “Assurance,” “Substance,” or “Title Deed”

  • Subjective: “assurance, confidence.” Faith is the believer’s firm inner confidence about things hoped for. This fits the use in 3:14 and the Septuagint’s association of the word with hope. Luther’s German Bible rendered it “a sure confidence,” and most modern English versions say “assurance” or “confidence.” The weakness of this view, taken alone, is that it can make faith sound like a strong feeling, which is not the author’s emphasis; his examples are not people who felt certain but people who acted on God’s word.
  • Objective: “substance, reality.” Faith gives present reality to future things; it is the means by which what is hoped for already “stands” in the life of the believer. John Chrysostom explained the verse this way: the resurrection has not yet happened and has no visible existence, but faith gives it substance in the soul. The Latin Vulgate translated substantia, followed by the King James Version’s “substance.” In the twentieth century Helmut Köster, in an influential dictionary article, argued that the word means “reality” throughout Hebrews. This view matches 1:3 and the objective tone of the second half of the verse (“proof”). Its weakness is that faith is a human response, not the reality itself; the things hoped for are real because God promised them, not because we believe them.
  • Legal: “title deed, guarantee.” Early in the twentieth century J. H. Moulton and George Milligan, studying the Greek papyri from Egypt, noted that hypostasis was used for the documents filed in public archives that established a person’s claim to property, and they suggested that faith is “the title deed” of things hoped for. The image is vivid and has been popular in preaching: the believer holds the deed to an estate not yet occupied. Many scholars, however, think the papyri do not quite support so specific a meaning, and the author gives no hint of a legal metaphor here.

Weighing these from the text: the word in 11:1 is set in parallel with elenchos, “proof, conviction,” which points beyond a mere mood to something that gives firm ground. The whole chapter shows faith grasping what God has promised so firmly that people stake their lives on it. Yet faith is not the thing hoped for; it is the believer’s grip on it. Calvin captured the balance when he took the word to mean the prop or foundation on which the soul rests and plants its foot. The rendering “assurance” is best, provided it is understood not as a feeling but as a well-founded certainty: faith is the God-given firmness by which future realities become solid ground for present life. The ground of that assurance lies outside the believer, in the God who “promised” and “is faithful” (10:23; 11:11). Faith is sure because its object is sure.

Word Study: ἔλεγχος elenchos

This noun occurs only here in the New Testament. In Greek usage it means a “test” or “proof,” the act of bringing something to light and demonstrating it, often in a courtroom or in philosophical argument (the Socratic method of cross-examination was called the elenchus). The related verb ἐλέγχω elenchō means “expose, convict, reprove” (Heb 12:5; John 16:8). Applied to faith, the word suggests that faith functions like evidence that convinces. The Vulgate rendered it argumentum, “proof.” Many English versions say “conviction,” which can mean either the proof that convinces (objective) or the state of being convinced (subjective), and both are present. Faith is the inner persuasion, created by God’s word, that things unseen are real; and that persuasion itself serves as proof, since it moves the believer to act as though the unseen were already visible. Moses “endured as seeing him who is invisible” (11:27); that is elenchos in action.

Scripture elsewhere confirms this orientation of faith toward the unseen. Paul writes, “we walk by faith, not by sight” (2 Cor 5:7), and, “hope that is seen is not hope; for who hopes for what he sees?” (Rom 8:24). He tells the Corinthians that “the things that are seen are temporary, but the things that are not seen are eternal” (2 Cor 4:18). Peter writes to believers who love Christ “though you have not seen him” (1 Pet 1:8). Jesus himself pronounced blessed “those who have not seen and yet have believed” (John 20:29). Hebrews 11:1 states the principle that unites all these sayings.

It is worth guarding against a common misunderstanding. Hebrews does not mean that faith creates its own reality, or that believing hard enough makes something so. The things hoped for are not wishes; they are promises, and promises made by a God who cannot lie (6:18). The hope Hebrews commends is “the hope set before us” by God (6:18), an “anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast” that enters behind the curtain where Jesus has gone (6:19–20). Faith does not manufacture the future; it receives God’s word about the future and stands on it. Nor is faith opposed to evidence. The believers of chapter 11 had heard God speak; they had seen his acts in the past. Faith is trust in a God who has spoken, extended into the territory where he has not yet acted.

For the first hearers the point was immediate. They had been told that they had “a better possession and an abiding one” (10:34), that Jesus would come “in a very little while” (10:37), that there was a “rest” remaining for the people of God (4:9), and a city to come (13:14). None of it could be seen. Their neighbors could see what they had lost; they could not see what they had gained. Faith, says the preacher, is precisely the capacity to treat God’s promised future as more solid than the visible present. That is not escapism; it is realism, if God is who he says he is.

11:2

For by it the elders received testimony.

This short sentence is the thesis of the whole chapter. The word “for” (γάρ gar) shows that what follows is the proof of what has just been said: we know that faith is the assurance of things hoped for, because this is the quality for which God commended his people in former times. The phrase “by it” (ἐν ταύτῃ en tautē, literally “in this”) refers to faith. The preposition can express the means (“by means of faith”) or the sphere and ground (“in the matter of faith,” “on account of faith”). Either way, faith is what God noticed and approved.

“The elders” (οἱ πρεσβύτεροι hoi presbyteroi) here does not mean church officers, as it often does elsewhere in the New Testament (Acts 14:23; 1 Tim 5:17), but “the people of old,” the ancestors of Israel. Jewish usage spoke the same way of the forefathers whose teaching was handed down (“the tradition of the elders,” Mark 7:3, 5). They are the same people Hebrews calls “the fathers” to whom God spoke through the prophets (1:1). Implied in the word is honor: these are the venerable ones whose memory the community cherished.

Word Study: ἐμαρτυρήθησαν emartyrēthēsan

The verb is μαρτυρέω martyreō, “to bear witness, to testify,” from which English has derived “martyr.” Here it is in the passive voice and the aorist tense: “they were testified to,” “they received testimony,” and hence “they were approved, commended.” Who gave the testimony? The passive leaves the subject unnamed, but the chapter makes it plain: it is God (11:4, “God bearing witness over his gifts”). Grammarians call this kind of construction a “divine passive,” where God is the unnamed agent. And the place where God gave this testimony is Scripture. Hebrews uses the same verb in 7:8 and 7:17 to introduce what Scripture says (“it is testified” of Melchizedek; “for it is testified, ‘You are a priest forever’”), and in 10:15 “the Holy Spirit also bears witness to us” through the words of Jeremiah. To be “testified to” is to have God’s approving verdict written into the sacred record.

The verb is a key word of the chapter. It recurs in 11:4 (twice, of Abel), 11:5 (of Enoch, in the perfect tense: “he has been attested,” the testimony still standing in Scripture), and 11:39, where “all these, though they received testimony through their faith” rounds off the chapter and closes the frame begun here. Then, in 12:1, the people who received God’s testimony become themselves a “cloud of witnesses” (μαρτύρων martyrōn), whose lives testify to us what faith can do.

The point carries real weight for the hearers. In their world, honor was a matter of public testimony: what people said about you determined your standing. The hearers had lost that standing for the sake of Christ (10:33). The preacher reminds them that the testimony that finally matters is God’s. The patriarchs were often obscure, homeless, or persecuted; yet their names are in the book that God himself wrote, and his verdict on them has outlasted every empire that ignored them. Jesus rebuked those who “receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God” (John 5:44). Paul, judged by others, answered, “it is the Lord who judges me,” and looked to the day when “each one’s commendation will come from God” (1 Cor 4:4–5). That is the commendation Hebrews 11 sets before its readers. The question the verse quietly asks is: whose testimony are you living for?

11:3

By faith we understand that the ages were fashioned by the word of God, so that what is seen has not come into being out of things that appear.

The first “by faith” of the chapter introduces not an ancient hero but “we.” Before he turns to Abel, the preacher points to something every believer does: we believe that God made the world by his word. There is good reason to begin here. Creation is the first “thing not seen” (11:1) that faith grasps, since no human being was present to watch it (Job 38:4, “Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?”). And it is the foundational act of the God in whom all the heroes of the chapter trust. The God who called the visible world into being by a word, out of nothing visible, is the God who can call life out of a barren womb (11:11–12) and raise the dead (11:19, 35). As Paul says of Abraham’s faith, he believed in “God who gives life to the dead and calls into being the things that do not exist” (Rom 4:17). Faith in creation and faith in resurrection are the same faith.

The verb “we understand” (νοοῦμεν nooumen, from νοέω noeō) means to perceive with the mind, to grasp intellectually. Faith is not the opposite of understanding; it is the way to understanding of things that lie beyond the reach of sight. Paul uses the same verb in Romans 1:20, where God’s invisible attributes “are perceived with the mind” (nooumena) through the things he has made. There Paul says that creation reveals that there is a Creator; here Hebrews says that faith, receiving God’s word, understands how the world came to be.

Word Study: κατηρτίσθαι τοὺς αἰῶνας katērtisthai tous aiōnas

The verb καταρτίζω katartizō means “to put in order, to fit together, to equip, to make complete.” It is used of mending nets (Mark 1:19), of restoring a fallen believer (Gal 6:1), and in Hebrews of the body God “prepared” for the Son (10:5) and of the God of peace who will “equip” believers for every good work (13:21). Applied to creation, it pictures the world not only brought into existence but set in order, fitted out, and made complete, as in Genesis 2:1, “Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all their host.” The form is a perfect infinitive, expressing a completed act whose results remain: the world was fashioned and stands fashioned.

The object is τοὺς αἰῶνας tous aiōnas, literally “the ages.” Hebrews used the same expression in 1:2: God made “the ages” through the Son. The Greek word aiōn can mean an age or era, and also the world that exists through that era. It corresponds to the Hebrew עוֹלָם olam, which in later Hebrew came to mean both “age” and “world.” The plural views the universe in the full sweep of its existence, its space and its time, all the ages of history. Many translations render “the universe” or “the worlds”; “the ages” preserves the author’s wording and its link with 1:2.

The instrument of creation is “the word of God,” and the noun is ῥῆμα rhēma, which emphasizes a word actually spoken, an utterance. Hebrews used it in 1:3, where the Son “upholds all things by the word (rhēma) of his power.” The same spoken word that made the world sustains it. The background is Genesis 1, where each act of creation begins with the formula וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים vayyomer elohim, “And God said,” and follows with its result: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי־אוֹר vayyomer elohim yehi or, vayhi-or, “And God said, ‘Let there be light,’ and there was light” (Gen 1:3). The phrase “And God said” occurs ten times in that chapter, and the Mishnah later summarized the teaching by saying that the world was created “by ten sayings” (m. Avot 5:1).

Old Testament Text: Psalm 33:6, 9

Hebrew: בִּדְבַר יְהוָה שָׁמַיִם נַעֲשׂוּ וּבְרוּחַ פִּיו כָּל־צְבָאָם — bidvar YHWH shamayim naʿasu, uveruach piv kol-tseva’am, “By the word of the Lord the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host.”

Hebrew: כִּי הוּא אָמַר וַיֶּהִי הוּא־צִוָּה וַיַּעֲמֹד — ki hu amar vayyehi, hu-tsivvah vayyaʿamod, “For he spoke, and it came to be; he commanded, and it stood firm.”

The psalm puts into poetry what Genesis narrates. Nothing intervenes between God’s word and its effect: no struggle with rival powers, as in the creation myths of Israel’s neighbors, and no pre-existing material that God has to work around. He speaks, and what he speaks exists. The psalm draws the practical conclusion directly: “Let all the earth fear the Lord” (Ps 33:8), and, further on, “Our soul waits for the Lord” (33:20). The God whose word made the world is the God whose word can be trusted. That is exactly the move Hebrews 11 makes.

The second half of the verse states the result of creation by the word: εἰς τὸ μὴ ἐκ φαινομένων τὸ βλεπόμενον γεγονέναι eis to mē ek phainomenōn to blepomenon gegonenai. The construction eis to with an infinitive can express purpose (“in order that”) or result (“so that”); here result is meant: because the world was made by God’s word, it follows that the visible world did not come from visible things. “What is seen” (τὸ βλεπόμενον to blepomenon) is singular, the whole visible order taken together, and it echoes the “things not seen” of verse 1. The perfect infinitive “has come into being” (γεγονέναι gegonenai) again stresses a past event with an abiding result.

Interpretive Options: “Not out of things that appear”

The difficulty lies in the placement of the negative μή mē, “not.” It stands before the phrase ek phainomenōn, “out of things that appear.” Does it negate the whole statement, or only the participle “appearing”?

  • The negative governs the whole clause: “so that what is seen did not come into being out of things that appear.” In Greek, the negative mē is the normal negative for an infinitive, and Greek writers often placed it early in a clause, before a prepositional phrase, while it still negated the verb. On this reading the verse says that the visible world does not derive from any visible source. Its origin lies entirely outside the material order, in the word of God. This is the reading of most modern commentators and of translations such as the King James Version (“were not made of things which do appear”).
  • The negative goes with the participle: “so that what is seen came into being out of things that do not appear,” as if the Greek were ek mē phainomenōn. The Latin Vulgate took it this way (“so that visible things might be made out of invisible ones”). Some have then connected the verse with ideas found in the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria and in Platonic thought, where the visible world is fashioned from invisible patterns or from formless matter (compare Wis 11:17, which speaks of God creating the world “out of formless matter”).

The first reading is the more natural Greek. If the author had meant “out of invisible things,” he could easily have placed the negative directly before the participle. More important, the context decides the matter. The verse sets God’s spoken word over against any visible source: the world exists because God spoke, not because something already existing was rearranged. Even on the second reading, the “invisible” source would most naturally be the word and power of God himself, not some eternal matter alongside him, for Hebrews knows of nothing that exists apart from God’s creating act (1:2, 10–12; 2:10, “for whom and through whom all things exist”).

Does the verse teach creation out of nothing (creatio ex nihilo)? It does not use that technical language, and its emphasis falls on the positive point that the word of God is the cause of the visible world. But it excludes any visible pre-existing material, and when it is read with the rest of Scripture its implication is clear. Genesis 1:1 makes the heavens and the earth, the totality of created things, the object of God’s creating. John says that “all things came into being through him, and apart from him not one thing came into being that has come into being” (John 1:3). Paul says that God “calls into being the things that do not exist” (Rom 4:17) and that in Christ “all things were created, in the heavens and on the earth, visible and invisible” (Col 1:16). The heavenly worshipers sing, “by your will they existed and were created” (Rev 4:11). Already in the second century BC, the mother of the Maccabean martyrs urged her son to look at heaven and earth and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed (2 Macc 7:28). Hebrews 11:3 stands in this stream. Nothing, visible or invisible, stands beside God as a co-eternal raw material; everything owes its being to his word.

Why does this belong at the head of a chapter about endurance? Because the whole chapter depends on the conviction that God’s word is effective, that what God says becomes reality. The world itself is the proof. The things the hearers hoped for were as yet invisible, but so was everything before God spoke. To believe in the Creator is to believe that the invisible word is more fundamental than the visible world, and therefore that a promise from God is more solid than anything the eye can see. Those who understand by faith that the world came from God’s word will not find it incredible that the same word can bring a city out of heaven, a people out of a dead womb, and a Savior out of the grave (13:20).
