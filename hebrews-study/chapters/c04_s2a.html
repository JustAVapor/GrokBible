Our Great High Priest (4:14–16)

This section (4:14–5:10) continues in chapter 5.

Translation

14Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast the confession. 15For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been tempted in every respect in the same way as we are, yet without sin. 16Let us then draw near with boldness to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace for help in time of need.

Setting and Structure

With 4:14 the sermon reaches a hinge. The preacher has already introduced the theme of Jesus’ priesthood in brief flashes. In 1:3 the Son “made purification for sins” before sitting down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. In 2:17–18 he became like his brothers in every respect “so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the things pertaining to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people,” and because he suffered when tempted, “he is able to help those who are being tempted.” In 3:1 the hearers were told to consider “Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession.” But since 3:1 the preacher has been occupied with the comparison between Jesus and Moses (3:1–6) and with the long exposition of Psalm 95 (3:7–4:13). Now he returns to the priestly theme and opens the great central part of the sermon, which runs from 4:14 to 10:18 and sets out the high priesthood of Christ after the order of Melchizedek.

These three verses work as a bridge. Looking back, they gather up what has been said: the word “confession” recalls 3:1; the mention of temptation and help recalls 2:17–18; the call to draw near answers the solemn warning of 4:12–13. Looking forward, they announce the subject of the chapters to come: a great high priest, Jesus the Son of God, who has passed through the heavens, who sympathizes with weakness, and through whom we may approach the throne of grace. Students of the structure of Hebrews, notably George Guthrie, have observed that 4:14–16 is closely matched by 10:19–23, where the same elements reappear: “since we have … a great priest over the house of God, let us draw near … let us hold fast the confession of our hope.” The two passages act like bookends around the central exposition of Christ’s priesthood (5:1–10:18). The preacher opens the subject with an exhortation and closes it with the same exhortation, now enriched by everything he has explained in between.

The section that begins here continues through 5:10. Its structure is clear:

  1. Exhortation (4:14–16). Since we have a great high priest, let us hold fast our confession and draw near to the throne of grace.
  2. The qualifications of every high priest (5:1–4). A high priest is taken from among men and appointed on their behalf in things pertaining to God; he can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward because he is himself beset with weakness; and he does not take the honor on himself but is called by God, as Aaron was.
  3. Christ meets these qualifications (5:5–10). He did not glorify himself but was appointed by God, who addressed him in Psalm 2:7 and Psalm 110:4; in the days of his flesh he offered prayers with loud cries and tears, learned obedience through what he suffered, and, having been made perfect, became the source of eternal salvation, designated by God a high priest after the order of Melchizedek.

Many interpreters have noticed that 5:5–10 takes up the qualifications of 5:1–4 in roughly reverse order, forming a chiasm (an A–B–B′–A′ pattern). That material belongs to the study of chapter 5. For now it is enough to see that 4:14–16 states the conclusion first, as a preacher often does, and then the following verses lay its foundation.

The historical background is the high priesthood of Israel, which the hearers knew well. According to the law of Moses, the high priest was the one priest set over his brothers (Lev 21:10), anointed and clothed in garments “for glory and for beauty” (Exod 28:2). His supreme duty was to enter the Most Holy Place once a year, on the Day of Atonement, to make atonement for himself and the people (Lev 16). He alone could pass through the curtain into the presence of God, and only with blood and a cloud of incense lest he die (Lev 16:2, 12–14). In the first century the high priest was still the most prominent figure in Jewish religious life. Yet the office had been badly compromised. Since the Hasmonean era it had been bound up with politics, and under Herod and the Roman governors high priests were appointed and removed at will; Josephus records a rapid succession of them, often drawn from a few powerful families. The Gospels show Annas and Caiaphas presiding at the trial of Jesus. For Jewish Christians tempted to look back to the temple, the preacher’s message was that they had not lost a high priest by following Jesus. They had gained the true and final one.

Notice also the tone of these verses. After the sobering words of 4:12–13, the preacher speaks with warmth. The hearers are not left naked and exposed before the eyes of God with no defense. They have a high priest who stands before God for them, who has been where they are, and who invites them to come near. The section is one of the most comforting passages in the New Testament, and it has been a refuge to tempted and struggling believers in every age.

Verse by Verse

4:14

Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast the confession.

The sentence begins with a participle: Ἔχοντες οὖν Echontes oun, “having, then,” or “since then we have.” The participle gives the ground for the exhortation that follows. The preacher does not first tell the hearers what to do and then offer them help. He begins with what they already possess. The word “then” (oun) resumes the thread of 2:17–3:1, where Jesus was first called high priest, and also answers 4:12–13: since we must give account to God, it is good news indeed that we have a high priest.

The emphasis falls on possession. “We have” (echontes; compare 4:15 “we do not have,” 6:19 “we have this as an anchor,” 8:1 “we have such a high priest,” 10:19 “having boldness,” 13:10 “we have an altar”) is one of the sermon’s recurring expressions. The hearers may have felt that by confessing Jesus they had lost a great deal: the temple, the priesthood, the sacrifices, their standing in the synagogue. The preacher insists that they have not lost but gained. They already have what the old priesthood only pictured.

Old Testament Background: “The Great Priest”

The expression “great high priest” (ἀρχιερέα μέγαν archierea megan) is emphatic, since archiereus already means “chief priest, high priest.” The Hebrew Bible usually calls the high priest הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל hakkohen haggadol, literally “the great priest.” Leviticus 21:10 describes him as וְהַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל מֵאֶחָיו wehakkohen haggadol me’echaw, “the priest who is great above his brothers,” on whose head the anointing oil was poured. The same title is given to Joshua (Yehoshua) the son of Jehozadak, high priest after the exile: יְהוֹשֻׁעַ הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל yehoshua‘ hakkohen haggadol, “Joshua the high priest” (Zech 3:1; Hag 1:1). The Greek writer of 1 Maccabees speaks of “Simon the great high priest” (1 Macc 13:42), using the same combination of words as Hebrews.

By adding “great” to “high priest,” the preacher signals that Jesus is not simply one more holder of the office. He is high priest in a supreme and unique sense. There is a suggestive contrast with 4:8. The first Joshua (Iēsous) could not give God’s people rest; a later Joshua, the high priest of Zechariah 3, was clothed in clean garments as a sign that God would remove iniquity “in a single day” (Zech 3:9). Now Jesus (Iēsous), the great high priest, does what neither could do.

This high priest “has passed through the heavens” (διεληλυθότα τοὺς οὐρανούς dielēlythota tous ouranous). The participle is in the perfect tense: he has passed through, and he remains where he has arrived. The verb διέρχομαι dierchomai means “to go through, to pass all the way across.” The picture is drawn from the Day of Atonement. Once a year the high priest of Israel passed through the court, through the holy place, and through the curtain into the Most Holy Place, where God was enthroned above the ark (Lev 16:2, 12–15). Jesus has made the true journey of which that was a copy. He has passed not through curtains of fabric but through the heavens themselves, into the very presence of God. Hebrews will say this plainly later: “Christ entered not into holy places made with hands, copies of the true things, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf” (9:24). He is “exalted above the heavens” (7:26). Paul says similarly that he “ascended far above all the heavens” (Eph 4:10).

The plural “heavens” reflects a common biblical way of speaking. Moses says, הֵן לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ הַשָּׁמַיִם וּשְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמָיִם hen layhwh eloheikha hashamayim ushemei hashamayim, “Behold, to the Lord your God belong heaven and the heaven of heavens” (Deut 10:14). Solomon confessed that “heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you” (1 Kgs 8:27), and Paul spoke of being caught up to “the third heaven” (2 Cor 12:2). Hebrews does not offer a map of the heavens. Its point is simply that Jesus has gone through every created realm, beyond the reach of every earthly priest, into the presence of the Creator. Angels are “ministering spirits” sent out to serve (1:14); Moses was a faithful servant in God’s house (3:5); Joshua led Israel into Canaan (4:8). None of them passed through the heavens to sit at God’s right hand. Jesus alone has done that (1:3, 13).

The high priest is then named: “Jesus the Son of God” (Ἰησοῦν τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ Iēsoun ton huion tou theou). The two names together sum up the first two chapters of the sermon. Chapter 1 presented him as the Son: heir of all things, radiance of God’s glory, exact imprint of his essential being, addressed as God and Lord, superior to angels. Chapter 2 presented him as Jesus: the man made for a little while lower than the angels, who shared flesh and blood with his brothers, suffered, was tempted, and tasted death for everyone (2:9, 14–18). Both are necessary for his priesthood. Because he is the Son, his priesthood is eternal, his access to God is unhindered, and his sacrifice has infinite worth. Because he is Jesus, truly human, he can represent human beings and sympathize with them. The name “Jesus” in Hebrews often stands at an emphatic point in the sentence to draw attention to his humanity and his earthly suffering (2:9; 6:20; 12:2; 13:12). Here it is joined to the title “Son of God,” which was the heart of the church’s confession.

The exhortation follows: “let us hold fast the confession” (κρατῶμεν τῆς ὁμολογίας kratōmen tēs homologias). The verb κρατέω krateō means “to grasp, to hold firmly, to take hold of with strength.” It is in the present tense, calling for a continuing grip, not a single act. Earlier the preacher urged them to “hold firm” (katechō) their boldness and confidence (3:6, 14); later he speaks of those who have fled for refuge “to seize” (krateō) the hope set before them (6:18), and he urges, “let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering” (10:23).

Word Study: ὁμολογία homologia

The noun comes from homo- (“same”) and logos (“word”): literally, “saying the same thing,” and so “agreement, acknowledgment, confession.” In the New Testament it describes the public declaration of faith by which believers acknowledge Jesus. Paul reminds Timothy that he “made the good confession in the presence of many witnesses,” just as Christ Jesus “made the good confession” before Pontius Pilate (1 Tim 6:12–13). In Romans 10:9–10, “if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.” Jesus himself said, “Everyone who confesses me before men, I also will confess before my Father who is in heaven” (Matt 10:32).

In Hebrews “the confession” (3:1; 4:14; 10:23) probably refers to the church’s shared declaration of faith, which included the acknowledgment of Jesus as the Son of God and very likely was spoken publicly at baptism and in worship. The article “the” suggests something known and fixed: not a private feeling but the common confession of the people of God. To hold it fast meant more than keeping an inner conviction. It meant continuing to identify openly with Jesus and his people when that identification was costly. The hearers had already suffered for it (10:32–34), and some were tempted to let it go quietly. The preacher’s answer is not merely to try harder, but to look again at whom they confess: a great high priest who has passed through the heavens. The greatness of the one confessed is the strength to keep confessing.

There is a profound pastoral wisdom in the order of this verse. The command to hold fast is grounded in the fact that we have a high priest. Perseverance is not achieved by clenching our fists alone. It is the response of those who know that Christ holds them, intercedes for them (7:25), and has gone ahead of them into the presence of God (6:19–20). We hold fast because we are held.
