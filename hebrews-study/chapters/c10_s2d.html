Remember, Endure, Live by Faith (10:32–39)

10:32

But keep remembering the former days, in which, after you had been enlightened, you endured a great contest of sufferings,

The little word “but” (δέ de) marks a sudden change of tone. The thunder of 10:26–31 gives way to warmth and encouragement — just as, after the warning of 6:4–8, the author wrote, “But we are persuaded of better things concerning you, beloved” (6:9). This pattern is pastoral wisdom. A preacher who only warns may drive people to despair; a preacher who only comforts may lull them into complacency. The author does both, and he does them in this order: first the danger, then the evidence of grace already at work in them.

The verb Ἀναμιμνῄσκεσθε Anamimnēskesthe is a present imperative: “keep remembering,” “call to mind again and again.” Memory is a great biblical discipline. Israel was commanded, “You shall remember all the way by which the Lord your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness” (Deut 8:2). The psalmist in his distress said, “I will call to mind the deeds of the Lord; yes, I will remember your wonder of old” (Ps 77:11). The readers are to remember not God’s deeds in Israel’s history but God’s grace in their own history — how he had sustained them in their early days as Christians.

“The former days” (τὰς πρότερον ἡμέρας tas proteron hēmeras) were the days “in which, after you had been enlightened” (φωτισθέντες phōtisthentes). This aorist passive participle is the same word used in 6:4, “those who have once been enlightened.” It describes conversion: the moment when the light of the gospel shone into their hearts (compare 2 Cor 4:4–6; Eph 1:18). The early church soon came to use “enlightenment” as a name for baptism, but here the reference is to the whole experience of coming to know Christ. The aorist tense points to a definite past event. Soon after they came to faith — perhaps because they came to faith — trouble came.

Word Study: ἄθλησις athlēsis

The noun ἄθλησιν athlēsin, found only here in the New Testament, means a contest, a struggle, an athletic competition. It comes from the world of the games — the races, wrestling, and boxing that were central to Greek and Roman public life. The related verb appears in 2 Tim 2:5: “if anyone competes as an athlete, he is not crowned unless he competes lawfully.” Jewish writers of the period applied this imagery to the sufferings of the faithful; 4 Maccabees, for instance, describes the martyrdom of the aged Eleazar and the mother with her seven sons as a noble athletic contest in which virtue awarded the prize. Hebrews uses the same imagery here and will develop it fully in 12:1–4: “let us run with endurance the race that lies before us,” and “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood in your struggle against sin.” The readers’ early sufferings were a contest in which they had competed well. The point is that they had already proved, by God’s grace, that they could endure. Their present weariness should not make them forget their past victory.

They “endured” (ὑπεμείνατε hypemeinate) — the verb from which the noun “endurance” (hypomonē) comes, the key word of 10:36 and 12:1–3. It means to remain under a load without collapsing, to stand firm under pressure. The author will use the same verb of Jesus, who “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2), and “endured such opposition from sinners against himself” (12:3). The readers’ endurance in the former days was a reflection, however small, of their Lord’s.

The contest was “of sufferings” (παθημάτων pathēmatōn), the same word used of Christ’s “sufferings” in 2:9–10. As the following verses show, these sufferings included public abuse, imprisonment, and loss of property. The author does not minimize them: they were a “great” contest (πολλήν pollēn, literally “much”).

10:33

sometimes being made a public spectacle by reproaches and afflictions, and sometimes becoming partners with those who were treated so.

The author divides the readers’ sufferings into two kinds, using a balanced Greek construction, τοῦτο μέν … τοῦτο δέ touto men … touto de, “on the one hand … on the other,” or “partly … partly,” “sometimes … sometimes.” Some of them suffered directly; others suffered by standing with those who suffered. Both are counted as part of the same contest.

Word Study: θεατρίζω theatrizō

The present passive participle θεατριζόμενοι theatrizomenoi means “being made a spectacle,” “being exposed publicly.” It is built on the word theatron, “theater,” and appears only here in the New Testament. In the Roman world the theater and the amphitheater were places not only of entertainment but of public humiliation and punishment; criminals and enemies of the state could be exhibited there for the crowd’s jeering. The cognate noun appears in Paul’s description of the apostles’ lot: “we have become a spectacle (theatron) to the world, to angels and to men” (1 Cor 4:9). The word does not necessarily mean the readers were literally dragged into a theater; it means that their suffering was public, exposed to the gaze and ridicule of others. Their shame was on display.

The means of this exposure were “reproaches and afflictions” (ὀνειδισμοῖς τε καὶ θλίψεσιν oneidismois te kai thlipsesin). “Reproaches” are insults, verbal abuse, public scorn. The same word appears in 11:26, where Moses considered “the reproach of Christ” greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, and in 13:13, where the readers are called to go out to Jesus outside the camp, “bearing his reproach.” “Afflictions” are pressures, hardships, persecutions of various kinds — the word suggests being squeezed or crushed.

Historical Background: Honor, Shame, and Public Exposure

To understand why “reproach” and public exposure were so painful, we must remember that the Roman world was an honor-and-shame culture. A person’s standing in the community — their reputation, their family name, their place in the social order — was among their most valuable possessions. To be publicly insulted, mocked, or humiliated was not merely unpleasant; it could cost a person his livelihood, his friendships, his family ties, and his place in society. David deSilva, who has done much to illuminate this side of Hebrews, emphasizes that the readers’ temptation to drift away was largely a temptation to regain the honor and acceptance their society had stripped from them. The author’s answer throughout the letter is to redefine honor: Jesus “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2), and now sits at God’s right hand. The verdict that matters is God’s, not the crowd’s. Those whom the world despised are the ones of whom “the world was not worthy” (11:38).

The second group became “partners with those who were treated so” (κοινωνοὶ τῶν οὕτως ἀναστρεφομένων γενηθέντες koinōnoi tōn houtōs anastrephomenōn genēthentes). The noun koinōnos means a partner, a sharer, one who has something in common with another. These believers were not themselves arrested or publicly abused, but they chose to identify with those who were. They visited them, supported them, and refused to distance themselves from them. In a culture where association with the disgraced brought disgrace, this was costly. It meant accepting the same shame. Paul thanked the Philippians in similar terms: “you did well to share in my affliction” (Phil 4:14), and he praised Onesiphorus, who “often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain” (2 Tim 1:16).

This verse teaches an important truth about Christian suffering. It is not only those who bear the blows directly who suffer for Christ. Those who stand beside them, at cost to themselves, also share in the contest. The church is a body: “if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it” (1 Cor 12:26).

10:34

For you indeed shared the sufferings of the prisoners, and you accepted with joy the plundering of your possessions, knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one.

The author now gives two concrete examples, explaining (“for indeed,” καὶ γάρ kai gar) the two kinds of suffering in 10:33, though in reverse order: first their solidarity with others, then their own losses.

“You shared the sufferings of the prisoners” (τοῖς δεσμίοις συνεπαθήσατε tois desmiois synepathēsate). The verb sympatheō means “to suffer with,” “to share the feelings of,” “to sympathize” — not merely to feel pity from a distance, but to enter into another’s suffering and act on it. It is the very verb the author used of Jesus in 4:15: “we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize (συμπαθῆσαι sympathēsai) with our weaknesses.” What their great High Priest does for them, they did for their imprisoned brothers and sisters. In loving the prisoners they were imitating Christ. Jesus himself said, “I was in prison, and you came to me … inasmuch as you did it to one of these my brothers, the least, you did it to me” (Matt 25:36, 40). In 13:3 the author will urge them to keep doing what they once did: “Remember the prisoners as though bound with them.”

Textual Note: “The Prisoners” or “My Bonds”?

The manuscripts differ here. The reading adopted in this study, τοῖς δεσμίοις tois desmiois, “the prisoners,” is supported by a strong and early group of witnesses. Many later manuscripts read tois desmois mou, “my bonds,” which lies behind the older English rendering “you had compassion on me in my bonds.” Some witnesses read simply tois desmois, “the bonds.” Bruce Metzger explains in his Textual Commentary that the editors preferred “the prisoners” because it is well attested and fits the context, and he suggests a plausible history for the other readings: “bonds” could have arisen when a scribe accidentally dropped a single letter, after which “my” was added to make sense of the phrase — and the resulting “my bonds” then seemed to support the idea that Paul, the famous prisoner, wrote the letter. The parallel in 13:3, where the same word τῶν δεσμίων tōn desmiōn, “the prisoners,” appears without variation, strongly supports the reading here. The point of the verse is not the author’s own imprisonment but the congregation’s care for its imprisoned members.

A second, smaller variant appears at the end of the verse: many later manuscripts add “in heaven” after “a better possession.” The addition is a correct interpretation, but it is not part of the earliest text.

Historical Background: Roman Prisons

Roman prisons were not, for the most part, places of long-term punishment. They held people awaiting trial, sentencing, or execution. Conditions were notoriously harsh: dark, crowded, filthy, often with prisoners in chains. The state generally did not provide adequate food, clothing, or care; prisoners depended on family and friends to bring them what they needed. The New Testament reflects this: when Paul was held at Caesarea, the governor allowed “his friends to attend to his needs” (Acts 24:23); Paul in Rome depended on visitors like Onesiphorus and on gifts brought by Epaphroditus (2 Tim 1:16–17; Phil 2:25; 4:18); in his last imprisonment he asked Timothy to bring his cloak (2 Tim 4:13). The second-century satirist Lucian, no friend of Christians, describes how, when a man named Peregrinus was imprisoned as a Christian, believers attended him from early morning, brought him meals, and spent their money to help him (The Passing of Peregrinus 12–13). Visiting prisoners was costly and risky. It meant being publicly identified with someone the authorities regarded as a criminal, and it could lead to one’s own arrest. This is the kind of solidarity the readers had shown.

Their own losses are described next: “you accepted with joy the plundering of your possessions” (τὴν ἁρπαγὴν τῶν ὑπαρχόντων ὑμῶν μετὰ χαρᾶς προσεδέξασθε tēn harpagēn tōn hyparchontōn hymōn meta charas prosedexasthe). The noun harpagē means seizure, robbery, plunder. Jesus used it of the “greed” or “plunder” inside the cups of the Pharisees (Matt 23:25; Luke 11:39). It could describe official confiscation of property, which often accompanied banishment or legal condemnation, or the looting of homes and shops by mobs. In the ancient world, when a community fell into disfavor, its houses could be broken into and its goods carried off while the authorities looked the other way; Philo of Alexandria, for example, describes how Jewish homes and shops in Alexandria were plundered during the riots of AD 38 (Against Flaccus). Whether by official action or mob violence, the readers had lost property, perhaps their homes and livelihoods.

Historical Background: When Were “the Former Days”?

The author does not tell us when or where these sufferings occurred, and we should hold any reconstruction loosely. But if, as the greeting from “those from Italy” (13:24) suggests, the congregation lived in or near Rome, one event fits the description remarkably well. The Roman biographer Suetonius, writing early in the second century, records of the emperor Claudius: “Since the Jews were constantly causing disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome” (Claudius 25.4). Most historians think “Chrestus” is a garbled form of Christus, “Christ” (the two words sounded nearly alike in the Greek of the time), and that the “disturbances” were conflicts within Rome’s Jewish community over the claim that Jesus was the Messiah. Luke confirms the expulsion: in Corinth Paul met “a certain Jew named Aquila … who had recently come from Italy, and Priscilla his wife, because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome” (Acts 18:2). The fifth-century Christian historian Orosius dates the edict to AD 49; that date is widely accepted, though not certain.

An expulsion of this kind would explain much in Hebrews 10:32–34. Jewish believers forced to leave the city would have suffered public disgrace; some may have been held in custody; and property left behind — homes, shops, goods — could easily be confiscated or looted. Those not expelled (Gentile believers, for instance) could have shown solidarity by caring for those who were. Yet no one, it seems, had been killed: the author can say, “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood” (12:4). That detail is important for dating. In AD 64, after the great fire of Rome, Nero made the Christians scapegoats; Tacitus records that a great multitude were put to death with mockery — some dressed in the skins of wild animals and torn apart by dogs, some crucified, some set on fire to serve as torches by night — in a public spectacle in Nero’s gardens (Annals 15.44). It would be hard to say to a Roman congregation after that slaughter, “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood.” If the readers were in Rome, then, the letter was most likely written in the 60s before Nero’s violence fell on them (or at least on this house church), with “the former days” lying some fifteen years in the past — long enough for first enthusiasm to cool and for a new generation to grow weary. The alternative is that these sufferings belong to another time and place altogether; but the Claudian expulsion remains the most attractive suggestion, and it fits well with the letter’s date before AD 70.

Astonishingly, they “accepted” this “with joy” (μετὰ χαρᾶς meta charas). The verb prosdechomai means to welcome, receive, accept. This was not grim resignation but glad acceptance. The joy was not in the loss itself but in what the loss signified and what it could not touch. Jesus had said, “Blessed are you when they insult you and persecute you … because of me. Rejoice and be glad, because your reward is great in the heavens” (Matt 5:11–12). The apostles left the council “rejoicing that they had been counted worthy to be dishonored for the Name” (Acts 5:41). James and Peter both urge believers to rejoice in trials (Jas 1:2; 1 Pet 4:13).

The reason for their joy: “knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one” (γινώσκοντες ἔχειν ἑαυτοὺς κρείττονα ὕπαρξιν καὶ μένουσαν ginōskontes echein heautous kreittona hyparxin kai menousan). There is a play on words in the Greek: they lost their hyparchonta, “possessions,” but they have a better hyparxis, “possession” — the two words share the same root. The world could take their goods, but not their true wealth. The reflexive pronoun “yourselves” (heautous) is emphatic: whatever others seized, you yourselves still possess something far greater.

This possession is “better” (κρείττονα kreittona) — the great key word of Hebrews, used of the better hope (7:19), the better covenant (7:22; 8:6), the better promises (8:6), the better sacrifices (9:23), the better country (11:16), and the better resurrection (11:35). And it is “abiding” (μένουσαν menousan), “lasting,” “remaining.” The same word appears in 12:27 of the things that cannot be shaken, which “remain,” and in 13:14: “for here we do not have an abiding (menousan) city, but we are seeking the one that is to come.” Earthly goods are temporary and can be taken away; the inheritance of God’s people is “imperishable and undefiled and unfading, kept in the heavens” (1 Pet 1:4). Jesus taught his disciples to lay up treasures in heaven, “where neither moth nor rust consumes and where thieves do not dig through and steal” (Matt 6:19–20). The readers had done exactly that, and their joy in loss was the proof.

10:35

Do not, therefore, throw away your boldness, which has a great reward.

Having reminded them of their past faithfulness, the author draws the conclusion (“therefore,” οὖν oun): μὴ ἀποβάλητε mē apobalēte, “do not throw away.” The verb means to throw off, cast aside, discard — as Bartimaeus threw off his cloak when Jesus called him (Mark 10:50). The aorist subjunctive with “not” forbids the action altogether: do not take that step. The image is of someone discarding something valuable as if it were a burden — the way a fleeing soldier might drop whatever slows him down. After all they had endured, it would be tragic folly to throw away now what they had held so firmly then.

What they must not throw away is “your boldness” (τὴν παρρησίαν ὑμῶν tēn parrēsian hymōn). This is the same word with which the section began (10:19). There, boldness was the God-given right and confidence to enter the holy places by Jesus’ blood. Here it includes also the outward, public confidence that confesses Christ before a hostile world — the boldness the readers had shown in the former days. The two belong together: those who have boldness before God can have boldness before people. The word also links back to 3:6: “we are his house, if we hold fast our boldness.” To throw away boldness is to stop drawing near to God and to stop confessing Christ before others.

This boldness “has a great reward” (ἥτις ἔχει μεγάλην μισθαποδοσίαν hētis echei megalēn misthapodosian). The noun misthapodosia, “payment of wages,” “recompense,” is a word distinctive to Hebrews in the New Testament. In 2:2 it described the just “recompense” (punishment) for disobedience under the old covenant. In 11:26 it describes the “reward” Moses looked to when he chose the reproach of Christ. A related noun in 11:6 describes God as the “rewarder” of those who seek him. The present tense “has” is significant: the reward is not uncertain; it is attached to faithful boldness as surely as wages to work, though it is entirely God’s gracious gift.

Some are uneasy with the idea of reward, fearing it undermines grace. But Scripture speaks freely of reward (Matt 5:12; 6:4; 1 Cor 3:14; Col 3:24; Rev 22:12). The reward is not earned in the sense of a debt owed; it is the gracious inheritance God has promised to those who trust him. Faith itself, Hebrews says, must believe “that he is a rewarder of those who seek him” (11:6). To look forward to God’s reward is not mercenary; it is to take God at his word. The great reward is ultimately God himself and the fulfillment of all his promises — the “better possession,” the “abiding city,” the unshakable kingdom (10:34; 12:28; 13:14).

10:36

For you have need of endurance, so that, having done the will of God, you may receive the promise.

The author now names exactly what his readers need: ὑπομονῆς γὰρ ἔχετε χρείαν hypomonēs gar echete chreian, “for of endurance you have need.” The word “endurance” stands first in the Greek for emphasis. They do not need a new teaching, a new experience, or a new religion; they need to keep going. The noun hypomonē picks up the verb of 10:32 (“you endured”): what they did then, they must do now. It also prepares for 12:1–3, where they are told to “run with endurance the race that lies before us,” looking to Jesus, who “endured the cross.” Endurance in Scripture is not passive resignation but active, steadfast perseverance under pressure — the quality of the runner who refuses to stop before the finish line.

The purpose: “so that, having done the will of God, you may receive the promise” (ἵνα τὸ θέλημα τοῦ θεοῦ ποιήσαντες κομίσησθε τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν hina to thelēma tou theou poiēsantes komisēsthe tēn epangelian). The phrase “the will of God” is a deliberate echo. Only a few verses earlier the author quoted Psalm 40 as the words of Christ entering the world: “Behold, I have come to do your will, O God” (10:7, 9; compare Ps 40:8, Hebrew 40:9, לַעֲשׂוֹת־רְצוֹנְךָ laʿasot-retsonkha, “to do your will”). And he added, “By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10). Christ did the will of God perfectly, to the point of death, and so secured our salvation. Now his people are called to do the will of God in their turn — not to earn salvation, which he has already accomplished, but as the path of faithful obedience that leads to the promised inheritance. The aorist participle “having done” views their whole life of obedience as a completed whole, seen from the end. In the context, doing God’s will means above all continuing to trust and confess Christ in the face of pressure — to persevere.

Then they will “receive” (κομίσησθε komisēsthe) “the promise.” The verb komizō means to receive, obtain, carry off as one’s own. Hebrews uses it for the reception of what God has promised (11:13, 19, 39). The heroes of faith in chapter 11 “did not receive the promises, but saw them from a distance and greeted them” (11:13); “all these, though they received a good testimony through their faith, did not receive the promise, since God had foreseen something better for us” (11:39–40). The “promise” here is the whole inheritance of God’s people — the eternal inheritance (9:15), the rest of God (4:1), the city to come (13:14). Those who “through faith and patience inherit the promises” (6:12) are the models the readers are to imitate.

This verse should not be misread as teaching salvation by works. The author has spent ten chapters showing that salvation rests entirely on Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice. But Scripture consistently teaches that the faith that saves is a faith that endures and obeys. Jesus said, “the one who has endured to the end — he will be saved” (Matt 24:13). Paul spoke of “the obedience of faith” (Rom 1:5). The endurance of the believer is itself God’s gift and work: “he who began a good work in you will carry it through to completion” (Phil 1:6). But it is also something the believer must actively do. Hebrews holds both together.

10:37

For yet “a very little while” — “the Coming One will come and will not delay;

To strengthen the call to endurance, the author turns once more to Scripture. He weaves together two prophetic texts: a phrase from Isaiah 26:20 and a longer quotation from Habakkuk 2:3–4. The “for” explains why endurance is needed and why it is possible: the waiting will not be long.

“For yet a very little while” (ἔτι γὰρ μικρὸν ὅσον ὅσον eti gar mikron hoson hoson). The repeated hoson hoson is an idiom intensifying “little”: “a little, how very little,” “a very, very little while.” The expression comes from the Greek of Isaiah 26:20.

Old Testament Text: Isaiah 26:20

Hebrew: “Go, my people, enter your inner rooms and shut your doors behind you; hide for a brief moment (חֲבִי כִמְעַט־רֶגַע chavi kimʿat-regaʿ) until the indignation passes over.” The Greek translation renders the phrase “for a little moment” with the same doubled idiom Hebrews uses: “hide yourself for a very little while (μικρὸν ὅσον ὅσον mikron hoson hoson), until the wrath of the Lord passes by.”

The context is rich. Isaiah 26 is the song of the redeemed people in the day of God’s salvation (26:1). It has just declared, “Your dead shall live; their corpses shall rise; awake and shout for joy, dwellers in the dust” (26:19), and it goes on: “For look, the Lord is going out from his place to visit the iniquity of the earth’s inhabitants upon them” (26:21). God’s people are to wait in shelter for a short time while his judgment passes over the world, and then the Lord comes. This is exactly the situation of Hebrews’ readers: a brief time of waiting and endurance before the Lord’s coming in judgment and salvation. The author has already borrowed language from Isaiah 26:11 in 10:27; now he draws from the same chapter again, in a way that would remind attentive hearers of its promise of resurrection (compare 11:35).

Then begins the quotation from Habakkuk 2:3: “the Coming One will come and will not delay” (ὁ ἐρχόμενος ἥξει καὶ οὐ χρονίσει ho erchomenos hēxei kai ou chronisei). The Hebrew and Greek forms of this verse differ in a way that matters, and Hebrews makes a further small but significant change.

Old Testament Text: Habakkuk 2:3 — Hebrew, Greek, and Hebrews

The Hebrew text. Habakkuk had complained that God was using the wicked Babylonians to punish Judah, and he took his stand to wait for God’s answer (Hab 1:12–2:1). God told him to write down the vision plainly (2:2), and said: “For the vision is yet for the appointed time (כִּי עוֹד חָזוֹן לַמּוֹעֵד ki ʿod chazon lammoʿed); it pants toward the end and will not lie. If it tarries, wait for it, for it will surely come; it will not be late (אִם־יִתְמַהְמָהּ חַכֵּה־לוֹ כִּי־בֹא יָבֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר im-yitmahmah chakkeh-lo ki-vo yavo lo yeʾacher)” (Hab 2:3). In the Hebrew, the subject is most naturally “the vision” (chazon): the revelation God has given will certainly be fulfilled at its appointed time. The doubled verb bo yavo, literally “coming, it will come,” is a Hebrew way of expressing certainty: “it will surely come.”

The Greek translation. The Greek Old Testament reads the second half of the verse personally: “If he delays, wait for him, for a coming one will come and will not delay.” Because the Greek word for “vision” is feminine while the pronoun “him” is masculine, the Greek reader naturally understands that a person is coming. The Hebrew allows this shift (the Hebrew pronoun in “wait for it” can equally mean “wait for him”), since the fulfillment of God’s vision is, in the end, the coming of God himself to judge and save.

Hebrews. The author adds the definite article: not “a coming one” but “the Coming One” (ὁ ἐρχόμενος ho erchomenos). In the early Christian vocabulary “the Coming One” was a messianic title. John the Baptist sent to ask Jesus, “Are you the Coming One, or shall we look for another?” (Matt 11:3), and the crowds welcomed Jesus with the words of Ps 118:26, “Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord” (Matt 21:9). By adding the article, Hebrews makes explicit what the Greek text implied: the fulfillment of Habakkuk’s vision is the coming of Christ. Christ, who “has been manifested once at the consummation of the ages” (9:26), “will appear a second time, apart from sin, for salvation to those who eagerly await him” (9:28).

Is this a distortion of Habakkuk? Not at all. In the Hebrew the vision is God’s promise of judgment on the wicked and salvation for his people, and its fulfillment is certain at the appointed time. The Greek translators drew out the personal reality behind the vision — the one who comes to fulfill it. Hebrews identifies that person as Jesus. The essential message is the same in all three: God’s promised intervention is certain; it may seem slow, but it will not be late; therefore wait for it in faith.

“And will not delay” (καὶ οὐ χρονίσει kai ou chronisei). For readers tempted to give up because the Lord had not yet returned, this was a crucial word. From the human point of view the waiting may seem long. But from God’s point of view the time is short — “a very little while” — and the Lord will come exactly on time. Peter answered similar doubts: “The Lord is not slow about his promise, as some reckon slowness, but is patient toward you, not wanting any to perish” (2 Pet 3:9). James urged, “Be patient, then, brothers, until the Lord’s coming … because the Lord’s coming has drawn near” (Jas 5:7–8). The church in every generation lives in this tension, and the call is the same: endure, for he is coming.

10:38

but my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul takes no pleasure in him.”

The quotation continues with Habakkuk 2:4, one of the most important verses in the Old Testament for the New Testament’s understanding of faith. Paul quotes it twice (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11). Hebrews uses it here as the bridge to its great chapter on faith.

Old Testament Text: Habakkuk 2:4 — Hebrew, Greek, and Hebrews

The Hebrew text: הִנֵּה עֻפְּלָה לֹא־יָשְׁרָה נַפְשׁוֹ בּוֹ וְצַדִּיק בֶּאֱמוּנָתוֹ יִחְיֶה hinneh ʿuppelah lo-yashrah nafsho bo, wetsaddiq beʾemunato yichyeh, “Look — swollen, not upright, is his soul within him; but the righteous one will live by his faithfulness.” The first half describes the arrogant (probably the proud Babylonian oppressor, compare 2:5); the second describes the righteous, who will live — survive the coming judgment and enjoy God’s favor — by his ʾemunah. That word means firmness, steadiness, faithfulness, trustworthiness; when Moses’ hands were held up during the battle with Amalek, “his hands were steadiness (אֱמוּנָה ʾemunah) until the sun went down” (Exod 17:12). It comes from the same root as the verb in Gen 15:6, “Abram trusted (וְהֶאֱמִן weheʾemin) in the Lord, and he reckoned it to him as righteousness.” In Habakkuk it describes the steadfast trust in God that holds on while waiting for the vision to be fulfilled — faith that perseveres, or faithfulness rooted in faith.

The Greek translation differs in two ways. First, its rendering of the first half is quite different from the Hebrew: “if he shrinks back, my soul takes no pleasure in him.” The Greek translators apparently read or understood the Hebrew consonants differently; how exactly they arrived at this rendering is debated. Second, in the second half most Greek manuscripts read “the righteous one shall live by my faithfulness” — that is, by God’s faithfulness — while at least one important manuscript reads “my righteous one shall live by faith.” So the Greek order is: (a) the coming one will come; (b) if he shrinks back, my soul takes no pleasure in him; (c) the righteous shall live by faith(fulness).

Hebrews reads “my righteous one shall live by faith” (ὁ δὲ δίκαιός μου ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται ho de dikaios mou ek pisteōs zēsetai), agreeing with the form of the Greek text that attaches “my” to “righteous one.” (Some later manuscripts of Hebrews drop “my,” probably to match Paul’s quotation in Rom 1:17 and Gal 3:11, which has no “my”; the earliest and best witnesses include it.) And Hebrews inverts the order of the two clauses, placing “my righteous one shall live by faith” first and “if he shrinks back …” second.

Why does the author reverse the clauses? Three reasons emerge from the text.

First, to make the subject clear. In the Greek order of Habakkuk, the clause “if he shrinks back” follows directly after “a coming one will come and will not delay.” The natural subject of “shrinks back” would then be the Coming One — which, once the author has identified the Coming One as Christ, would be unthinkable. By putting “my righteous one shall live by faith” first, the author makes “my righteous one” the subject of “if he shrinks back.” Christ will certainly come and will not hold back; the question is whether his people will hold back.

Second, to sharpen the warning. The arrangement now confronts the righteous person with two possibilities: he will live by faith, or he will shrink back, and then God will take no pleasure in him. The warning of 10:26–31 is restated in the language of prophecy. The “righteous one” is called to live by faith; if he draws back, he forfeits God’s pleasure. As with the earlier warnings, the author does not soften the alternatives: there is no safe middle ground between faith and shrinking back.

Third, to prepare for his own conclusion and for chapter 11. The new order creates a pattern that 10:39 then reverses, forming a small chiasm: faith (10:38a) — shrinking back (10:38b) — shrinking back (10:39a) — faith (10:39b). The last word is faith, and the next chapter will begin, “Now faith is …” (11:1).

The phrase “my righteous one” (ὁ δίκαιός μου ho dikaios mou) is tender. God claims the righteous person as his own. The person who lives by faith belongs to God. “Shall live” (ζήσεται zēsetai, future) points to life in the full sense — preservation through judgment and the enjoyment of eternal life with God. “By faith” (ἐκ πίστεως ek pisteōs) — literally “out of faith” — names faith as the source and ground of this life.

The verb “shrinks back” (ὑποστείληται hyposteilētai, aorist middle subjunctive of hypostellō) means to draw back, hold back, withdraw, shrink from something out of fear. Paul used it twice in his farewell to the Ephesian elders: “I did not shrink back from announcing to you anything that was profitable … I did not shrink back from announcing to you the whole purpose of God” (Acts 20:20, 27). He used it also of Peter at Antioch, who “began to draw back and separate himself” from the Gentiles, “fearing those of the circumcision” (Gal 2:12). The word describes timid withdrawal under pressure — exactly the danger facing Hebrews’ readers. “My soul takes no pleasure in him” (οὐκ εὐδοκεῖ ἡ ψυχή μου ἐν αὐτῷ ouk eudokei hē psychē mou en autō) is a Hebrew idiom for God himself: “I take no pleasure in him.” The verb eudokeō is the word the Father used at Jesus’ baptism: “This is my Son, the beloved, in whom I have taken delight” (Matt 3:17). In 10:6 and 10:8 God took “no pleasure” in the Levitical sacrifices; here he takes no pleasure in one who shrinks back from the one true sacrifice.

How does Hebrews’ use of Habakkuk 2:4 relate to Paul’s? Paul emphasizes the first part of the phrase: the one who is righteous by faith — that is, justified through faith in Christ rather than by works of the law — shall live (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11). Hebrews emphasizes the continuing life of faith: the righteous one keeps living by faith, without shrinking back, until the Coming One arrives. These are not two competing doctrines but two sides of one truth. The faith by which a person is first counted righteous is the same faith by which he goes on living and persevering. Abraham, Paul’s great example of justifying faith (Rom 4; Gen 15:6), is also Hebrews’ great example of persevering faith (11:8–19). Both Paul and Hebrews are faithful to Habakkuk, whose ʾemunah is precisely the steady trust in God that holds on through the long wait.

10:39

But we are not of those who shrink back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul.

The section ends not with a warning but with a confident declaration. The emphatic pronoun ἡμεῖς hēmeis, “we,” stands first: “But we …” The author includes himself and his readers together. Having warned them solemnly, he now expresses his conviction about them, just as he did in 6:9: “But we are persuaded of better things concerning you, beloved, things bound up with salvation, even though we speak in this way.”

The Greek is compact and vivid: οὐκ ἐσμὲν ὑποστολῆς εἰς ἀπώλειαν, ἀλλὰ πίστεως εἰς περιποίησιν ψυχῆς ouk esmen hypostolēs eis apōleian, alla pisteōs eis peripoiēsin psychēs. Literally: “we are not of shrinking-back unto destruction, but of faith unto the preserving of the soul.” The genitive case (“of shrinking back,” “of faith”) describes what characterizes a person or what he belongs to. We do not belong to the company defined by shrinking back; we belong to the company defined by faith.

Word Study: ὑποστολή hypostolē and περιποίησις peripoiēsis

Hypostolē, found only here in the New Testament, is the noun from the verb “shrink back” in 10:38. It means a drawing back, timidity, withdrawal from a commitment through fear. The author picks up the word from the quotation and sets it in contrast with faith: these are the two possible directions, and they lead to two opposite destinations.

Peripoiēsis means acquiring, gaining possession of, preserving, keeping safe. Paul uses it of “obtaining salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thess 5:9) and of “the redemption of God’s own possession” (Eph 1:14); Peter uses it of God’s people as “a people for God’s possession” (1 Pet 2:9). The related verb appears in Jesus’ saying, “Whoever seeks to keep his life safe will lose it, but whoever loses it will keep it alive” (Luke 17:33). There is a profound irony here. Those who shrink back do so to preserve their lives, their property, their reputations — and they end in destruction. Those who hold to faith, even at the cost of property and honor (10:34), gain the preservation of the soul. Jesus said, “By your endurance you will gain possession of your souls” (Luke 21:19).

The two destinations are stark: “destruction” (ἀπώλειαν apōleian) or “the preserving of the soul.” Apōleia is the New Testament’s regular word for final ruin, eternal loss (Matt 7:13; Phil 1:28; 3:19; 2 Pet 3:7). It corresponds to the “fearful expectation of judgment” of 10:27. The “soul” (ψυχῆς psychēs) here means the whole person, the self, the life — not merely an immaterial part. To “preserve the soul” is to be saved in the fullest sense, brought safely through judgment into eternal life. The word “soul” also links back to the quotation: if the righteous one shrinks back, God’s “soul” takes no pleasure in him (10:38); but those of faith gain their own souls.

This verse is the author’s answer to the question raised by the warning of 10:26–31. He does not leave his readers wondering whether they will fall. He declares, with pastoral confidence, that they belong to faith. This confidence is not based on their strength but on God’s faithfulness (10:23), Christ’s perfect sacrifice (10:14), and the evidence of grace already visible in their lives (10:32–34; compare 6:10). Yet the confidence is expressed in a way that calls for a response. To say “we are of faith” is to call them to live as people of faith — to draw near, hold fast, and endure. The declaration both assures and summons. Scripture regularly holds the two together: “with fear and trembling work out your own salvation, for it is God who is at work in you, both the willing and the working, for his good pleasure” (Phil 2:12–13).

And with that word “faith,” the author is ready to describe what such faith looks like. Chapter 11 will show, through a long line of witnesses from Abel to the prophets and martyrs, people who did not shrink back but lived by faith, “of whom the world was not worthy” (11:38). The readers are being invited to join that company.

For Reflection

  1. The author grounds every exhortation in what believers already “have” through Christ: boldness to enter, a new and living way, and a great priest over God’s house (10:19–21). How does knowing that the way into God’s presence was opened by Jesus’ flesh and blood — not by your own worthiness — change the way you pray and worship?
  2. Faith, hope, and love are tied in 10:22–24 to drawing near to God, holding fast the confession, and stirring one another up. Which of these three is weakest in your life right now, and what practical step could you take to strengthen it — especially within the gathered church?
  3. The warning of 10:26–31 concerns not every deliberate sin but the settled repudiation of the Son, his blood, and his Spirit. Why does Scripture take this sin so seriously? How can the warning keep a believer from drifting without leading a tender conscience into despair?
  4. The readers had once accepted the loss of their property with joy, “knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one” (10:34). What “possessions” — material, social, or reputational — would be hardest for you to lose for Christ’s sake? What does it look like to hold them loosely because of the better and abiding possession?
  5. The author urges his readers to remember “the former days” of their early faithfulness (10:32). What past evidences of God’s grace in your life, or in your congregation’s life, can strengthen your endurance today?
  6. Habakkuk’s word, “my righteous one shall live by faith,” is used by Paul for how we are counted righteous and by Hebrews for how we keep going. How do these two uses together shape your understanding of what it means to trust Christ — at the beginning, through the long wait, and to the end?
