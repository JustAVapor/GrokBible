Remember, Endure, Live by Faith (10:32–39)

10:32

But keep remembering the former days, in which, after you had been enlightened, you endured a great contest of sufferings,

The little word “but” (δέ de) marks a sudden change of tone. The thunder of 10:26–31 gives way to warmth and encouragement — just as, after the warning of 6:4–8, the author wrote, “But we are persuaded of better things concerning you, beloved” (6:9). This pattern is pastoral wisdom. A preacher who only warns may drive people to despair; a preacher who only comforts may lull them into complacency. The author does both, and he does them in this order: first the danger, then the evidence of grace already at work in them.

The verb Ἀναμιμνῄσκεσθε Anamimnēskesthe is a present imperative: “keep remembering,” “call to mind again and again.” Memory is a great biblical discipline. Israel was commanded, “You shall remember the whole way that the Lord your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness” (Deut 8:2). The psalmist in his distress said, “I will remember the deeds of the Lord; yes, I will remember your wonders of old” (Ps 77:11). The readers are to remember not God’s deeds in Israel’s history but God’s grace in their own history — how he had sustained them in their early days as Christians.

“The former days” (τὰς πρότερον ἡμέρας tas proteron hēmeras) were the days “in which, after you had been enlightened” (φωτισθέντες phōtisthentes). This aorist passive participle is the same word used in 6:4, “those who have once been enlightened.” It describes conversion: the moment when the light of the gospel shone into their hearts (compare 2 Cor 4:4–6; Eph 1:18). The early church soon came to use “enlightenment” as a name for baptism, but here the reference is to the whole experience of coming to know Christ. The aorist tense points to a definite past event. Soon after they came to faith — perhaps because they came to faith — trouble came.

Word Study: ἄθλησις athlēsis

The noun ἄθλησιν athlēsin, found only here in the New Testament, means a contest, a struggle, an athletic competition. It comes from the world of the games — the races, wrestling, and boxing that were central to Greek and Roman public life. The related verb appears in 2 Tim 2:5: “an athlete is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules.” Jewish writers of the period applied this imagery to the sufferings of the faithful; 4 Maccabees, for instance, describes the martyrdom of the aged Eleazar and the mother with her seven sons as a noble athletic contest in which virtue awarded the prize. Hebrews uses the same imagery here and will develop it fully in 12:1–4: “let us run with endurance the race set before us,” and “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood in your struggle against sin.” The readers’ early sufferings were a contest in which they had competed well. The point is that they had already proved, by God’s grace, that they could endure. Their present weariness should not make them forget their past victory.

They “endured” (ὑπεμείνατε hypemeinate) — the verb from which the noun “endurance” (hypomonē) comes, the key word of 10:36 and 12:1–3. It means to remain under a load without collapsing, to stand firm under pressure. The author will use the same verb of Jesus, who “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2), and “endured such hostility from sinners against himself” (12:3). The readers’ endurance in the former days was a reflection, however small, of their Lord’s.

The contest was “of sufferings” (παθημάτων pathēmatōn), the same word used of Christ’s “sufferings” in 2:9–10. As the following verses show, these sufferings included public abuse, imprisonment, and loss of property. The author does not minimize them: they were a “great” contest (πολλήν pollēn, literally “much”).

10:33

sometimes being made a public spectacle by reproaches and afflictions, and sometimes becoming partners with those who were treated so.

The author divides the readers’ sufferings into two kinds, using a balanced Greek construction, τοῦτο μέν … τοῦτο δέ touto men … touto de, “on the one hand … on the other,” or “partly … partly,” “sometimes … sometimes.” Some of them suffered directly; others suffered by standing with those who suffered. Both are counted as part of the same contest.

Word Study: θεατρίζω theatrizō

The present passive participle θεατριζόμενοι theatrizomenoi means “being made a spectacle,” “being exposed publicly.” It is built on the word theatron, “theater,” and appears only here in the New Testament. In the Roman world the theater and the amphitheater were places not only of entertainment but of public humiliation and punishment; criminals and enemies of the state could be exhibited there for the crowd’s jeering. The cognate noun appears in Paul’s description of the apostles’ lot: “we have become a spectacle (theatron) to the world, to angels and to men” (1 Cor 4:9). The word does not necessarily mean the readers were literally dragged into a theater; it means that their suffering was public, exposed to the gaze and ridicule of others. Their shame was on display.

The means of this exposure were “reproaches and afflictions” (ὀνειδισμοῖς τε καὶ θλίψεσιν oneidismois te kai thlipsesin). “Reproaches” are insults, verbal abuse, public scorn. The same word appears in 11:26, where Moses considered “the reproach of Christ” greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, and in 13:13, where the readers are called to go out to Jesus outside the camp, “bearing his reproach.” “Afflictions” are pressures, hardships, persecutions of various kinds — the word suggests being squeezed or crushed.

Historical Background: Honor, Shame, and Public Exposure

To understand why “reproach” and public exposure were so painful, we must remember that the Roman world was an honor-and-shame culture. A person’s standing in the community — their reputation, their family name, their place in the social order — was among their most valuable possessions. To be publicly insulted, mocked, or humiliated was not merely unpleasant; it could cost a person his livelihood, his friendships, his family ties, and his place in society. David deSilva, who has done much to illuminate this side of Hebrews, emphasizes that the readers’ temptation to drift away was largely a temptation to regain the honor and acceptance their society had stripped from them. The author’s answer throughout the letter is to redefine honor: Jesus “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2), and now sits at God’s right hand. The verdict that matters is God’s, not the crowd’s. Those whom the world despised are the ones of whom “the world was not worthy” (11:38).

The second group became “partners with those who were treated so” (κοινωνοὶ τῶν οὕτως ἀναστρεφομένων γενηθέντες koinōnoi tōn houtōs anastrephomenōn genēthentes). The noun koinōnos means a partner, a sharer, one who has something in common with another. These believers were not themselves arrested or publicly abused, but they chose to identify with those who were. They visited them, supported them, and refused to distance themselves from them. In a culture where association with the disgraced brought disgrace, this was costly. It meant accepting the same shame. Paul thanked the Philippians in similar terms: “you did well to share in my affliction” (Phil 4:14), and he praised Onesiphorus, who “often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chain” (2 Tim 1:16).

This verse teaches an important truth about Christian suffering. It is not only those who bear the blows directly who suffer for Christ. Those who stand beside them, at cost to themselves, also share in the contest. The church is a body: “if one member suffers, all suffer together” (1 Cor 12:26).

10:34

For you indeed shared the sufferings of the prisoners, and you accepted with joy the plundering of your possessions, knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one.

The author now gives two concrete examples, explaining (“for indeed,” καὶ γάρ kai gar) the two kinds of suffering in 10:33, though in reverse order: first their solidarity with others, then their own losses.

“You shared the sufferings of the prisoners” (τοῖς δεσμίοις συνεπαθήσατε tois desmiois synepathēsate). The verb sympatheō means “to suffer with,” “to share the feelings of,” “to sympathize” — not merely to feel pity from a distance, but to enter into another’s suffering and act on it. It is the very verb the author used of Jesus in 4:15: “we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize (συμπαθῆσαι sympathēsai) with our weaknesses.” What their great High Priest does for them, they did for their imprisoned brothers and sisters. In loving the prisoners they were imitating Christ. Jesus himself said, “I was in prison and you came to me … as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me” (Matt 25:36, 40). In 13:3 the author will urge them to keep doing what they once did: “Remember the prisoners as though bound with them.”

Textual Note: “The Prisoners” or “My Bonds”?

The manuscripts differ here. The reading adopted in this study, τοῖς δεσμίοις tois desmiois, “the prisoners,” is supported by a strong and early group of witnesses. Many later manuscripts read tois desmois mou, “my bonds,” which lies behind the older English rendering “you had compassion on me in my bonds.” Some witnesses read simply tois desmois, “the bonds.” Bruce Metzger explains in his Textual Commentary that the editors preferred “the prisoners” because it is well attested and fits the context, and he suggests a plausible history for the other readings: “bonds” could have arisen when a scribe accidentally dropped a single letter, after which “my” was added to make sense of the phrase — and the resulting “my bonds” then seemed to support the idea that Paul, the famous prisoner, wrote the letter. The parallel in 13:3, where the same word τῶν δεσμίων tōn desmiōn, “the prisoners,” appears without variation, strongly supports the reading here. The point of the verse is not the author’s own imprisonment but the congregation’s care for its imprisoned members.

A second, smaller variant appears at the end of the verse: many later manuscripts add “in heaven” after “a better possession.” The addition is a correct interpretation, but it is not part of the earliest text.

Historical Background: Roman Prisons

Roman prisons were not, for the most part, places of long-term punishment. They held people awaiting trial, sentencing, or execution. Conditions were notoriously harsh: dark, crowded, filthy, often with prisoners in chains. The state generally did not provide adequate food, clothing, or care; prisoners depended on family and friends to bring them what they needed. The New Testament reflects this: when Paul was held at Caesarea, the governor allowed “his friends to attend to his needs” (Acts 24:23); Paul in Rome depended on visitors like Onesiphorus and on gifts brought by Epaphroditus (2 Tim 1:16–17; Phil 2:25; 4:18); in his last imprisonment he asked Timothy to bring his cloak (2 Tim 4:13). The second-century satirist Lucian, no friend of Christians, describes how, when a man named Peregrinus was imprisoned as a Christian, believers attended him from early morning, brought him meals, and spent their money to help him (The Passing of Peregrinus 12–13). Visiting prisoners was costly and risky. It meant being publicly identified with someone the authorities regarded as a criminal, and it could lead to one’s own arrest. This is the kind of solidarity the readers had shown.

Their own losses are described next: “you accepted with joy the plundering of your possessions” (τὴν ἁρπαγὴν τῶν ὑπαρχόντων ὑμῶν μετὰ χαρᾶς προσεδέξασθε tēn harpagēn tōn hyparchontōn hymōn meta charas prosedexasthe). The noun harpagē means seizure, robbery, plunder. Jesus used it of the “greed” or “plunder” inside the cups of the Pharisees (Matt 23:25; Luke 11:39). It could describe official confiscation of property, which often accompanied banishment or legal condemnation, or the looting of homes and shops by mobs. In the ancient world, when a community fell into disfavor, its houses could be broken into and its goods carried off while the authorities looked the other way; Philo of Alexandria, for example, describes how Jewish homes and shops in Alexandria were plundered during the riots of AD 38 (Against Flaccus). Whether by official action or mob violence, the readers had lost property, perhaps their homes and livelihoods.

Historical Background: When Were “the Former Days”?

The author does not tell us when or where these sufferings occurred, and we should hold any reconstruction loosely. But if, as the greeting from “those from Italy” (13:24) suggests, the congregation lived in or near Rome, one event fits the description remarkably well. The Roman biographer Suetonius, writing early in the second century, records of the emperor Claudius: “Since the Jews were constantly causing disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome” (Claudius 25.4). Most historians think “Chrestus” is a garbled form of Christus, “Christ” (the two words sounded nearly alike in the Greek of the time), and that the “disturbances” were conflicts within Rome’s Jewish community over the claim that Jesus was the Messiah. Luke confirms the expulsion: in Corinth Paul met “a Jew named Aquila … recently come from Italy with Priscilla his wife, because Claudius had ordered all the Jews to leave Rome” (Acts 18:2). The fifth-century Christian historian Orosius dates the edict to AD 49; that date is widely accepted, though not certain.

An expulsion of this kind would explain much in Hebrews 10:32–34. Jewish believers forced to leave the city would have suffered public disgrace; some may have been held in custody; and property left behind — homes, shops, goods — could easily be confiscated or looted. Those not expelled (Gentile believers, for instance) could have shown solidarity by caring for those who were. Yet no one, it seems, had been killed: the author can say, “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood” (12:4). That detail is important for dating. In AD 64, after the great fire of Rome, Nero made the Christians scapegoats; Tacitus records that a great multitude were put to death with mockery — some dressed in the skins of wild animals and torn apart by dogs, some crucified, some set on fire to serve as torches by night — in a public spectacle in Nero’s gardens (Annals 15.44). It would be hard to say to a Roman congregation after that slaughter, “you have not yet resisted to the point of blood.” If the readers were in Rome, then, the letter was most likely written in the 60s before Nero’s violence fell on them (or at least on this house church), with “the former days” lying some fifteen years in the past — long enough for first enthusiasm to cool and for a new generation to grow weary. The alternative is that these sufferings belong to another time and place altogether; but the Claudian expulsion remains the most attractive suggestion, and it fits well with the letter’s date before AD 70.

Astonishingly, they “accepted” this “with joy” (μετὰ χαρᾶς meta charas). The verb prosdechomai means to welcome, receive, accept. This was not grim resignation but glad acceptance. The joy was not in the loss itself but in what the loss signified and what it could not touch. Jesus had said, “Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you … on my account. Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven” (Matt 5:11–12). The apostles left the council “rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name” (Acts 5:41). James and Peter both urge believers to rejoice in trials (Jas 1:2; 1 Pet 4:13).

The reason for their joy: “knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, and an abiding one” (γινώσκοντες ἔχειν ἑαυτοὺς κρείττονα ὕπαρξιν καὶ μένουσαν ginōskontes echein heautous kreittona hyparxin kai menousan). There is a play on words in the Greek: they lost their hyparchonta, “possessions,” but they have a better hyparxis, “possession” — the two words share the same root. The world could take their goods, but not their true wealth. The reflexive pronoun “yourselves” (heautous) is emphatic: whatever others seized, you yourselves still possess something far greater.

This possession is “better” (κρείττονα kreittona) — the great key word of Hebrews, used of the better hope (7:19), the better covenant (7:22; 8:6), the better promises (8:6), the better sacrifices (9:23), the better country (11:16), and the better resurrection (11:35). And it is “abiding” (μένουσαν menousan), “lasting,” “remaining.” The same word appears in 12:27 of the things that cannot be shaken, which “remain,” and in 13:14: “for here we have no lasting (menousan) city, but we seek the city that is to come.” Earthly goods are temporary and can be taken away; the inheritance of God’s people is “imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven” (1 Pet 1:4). Jesus taught his disciples to lay up treasures in heaven, “where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not break in and steal” (Matt 6:19–20). The readers had done exactly that, and their joy in loss was the proof.

10:35

Do not, therefore, throw away your boldness, which has a great reward.

Having reminded them of their past faithfulness, the author draws the conclusion (“therefore,” οὖν oun): μὴ ἀποβάλητε mē apobalēte, “do not throw away.” The verb means to throw off, cast aside, discard — as Bartimaeus threw off his cloak when Jesus called him (Mark 10:50). The aorist subjunctive with “not” forbids the action altogether: do not take that step. The image is of someone discarding something valuable as if it were a burden — the way a fleeing soldier might drop whatever slows him down. After all they had endured, it would be tragic folly to throw away now what they had held so firmly then.

What they must not throw away is “your boldness” (τὴν παρρησίαν ὑμῶν tēn parrēsian hymōn). This is the same word with which the section began (10:19). There, boldness was the God-given right and confidence to enter the holy places by Jesus’ blood. Here it includes also the outward, public confidence that confesses Christ before a hostile world — the boldness the readers had shown in the former days. The two belong together: those who have boldness before God can have boldness before people. The word also links back to 3:6: “we are his house, if we hold fast our boldness.” To throw away boldness is to stop drawing near to God and to stop confessing Christ before others.

This boldness “has a great reward” (ἥτις ἔχει μεγάλην μισθαποδοσίαν hētis echei megalēn misthapodosian). The noun misthapodosia, “payment of wages,” “recompense,” is a word distinctive to Hebrews in the New Testament. In 2:2 it described the just “recompense” (punishment) for disobedience under the old covenant. In 11:26 it describes the “reward” Moses looked to when he chose the reproach of Christ. A related noun in 11:6 describes God as the “rewarder” of those who seek him. The present tense “has” is significant: the reward is not uncertain; it is attached to faithful boldness as surely as wages to work, though it is entirely God’s gracious gift.

Some are uneasy with the idea of reward, fearing it undermines grace. But Scripture speaks freely of reward (Matt 5:12; 6:4; 1 Cor 3:14; Col 3:24; Rev 22:12). The reward is not earned in the sense of a debt owed; it is the gracious inheritance God has promised to those who trust him. Faith itself, Hebrews says, must believe “that he is a rewarder of those who seek him” (11:6). To look forward to God’s reward is not mercenary; it is to take God at his word. The great reward is ultimately God himself and the fulfillment of all his promises — the “better possession,” the “abiding city,” the unshakable kingdom (10:34; 12:28; 13:14).
