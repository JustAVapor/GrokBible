Hebrews 7

A Priest Forever after the Order of Melchizedek

Hebrews 7 is the hinge of the whole sermon. Three times the preacher has quoted or echoed Psalm 110:4, “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (5:6, 10; 6:20). Three times he has raised the name and then set it down. At 5:11 he paused because his hearers had become “sluggish” (νωθροί nōthroi) in hearing, and he spent the long warning and encouragement of 5:11–6:20 waking them up. Now, with the anchor of hope fixed “behind the curtain” where Jesus has entered as forerunner, “having become a high priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (6:19–20), he finally opens the subject he called “hard to explain” (5:11). Chapter 7 is the “solid food” of 5:14.

The chapter is a sustained piece of biblical reasoning built on just two Old Testament texts: the brief narrative of Genesis 14:17–20, the only story in the Law about Melchizedek, and the single oath of Psalm 110:4, the only other place in the Hebrew Bible where his name appears. The author reads the psalm in the light of the narrative and the narrative in the light of the psalm. The method is careful and Jewish in texture: he weighs what Scripture says, what it does not say, the order in which God said things, and the significance of an oath. His conclusion is breathtaking for a first-century Jewish reader: the Levitical priesthood, which God himself established at Sinai, was never meant to be final. God had already announced, in a psalm written centuries after Moses, a different priest of a different order, appointed in a different way, with a different kind of life.

The chapter falls into two movements:

  • 7:1–10: The greatness of Melchizedek. The author retells Genesis 14, interprets Melchizedek’s name and title, draws out the significance of what Genesis omits about him, and then argues from two actions—Abraham gave him a tenth, and he blessed Abraham—that Melchizedek is greater than Abraham and therefore greater than Abraham’s descendant Levi, the father of Israel’s priests.
  • 7:11–28: The superiority of the priest “according to the order of Melchizedek.” Here Melchizedek himself recedes and Jesus comes forward. The author argues that (1) the promise of a new priest proves the old priesthood could not bring “perfection” (7:11–19); (2) the new priest was installed by a divine oath, the old ones without one (7:20–22); (3) the old priests were many because they died, but this priest lives forever and so saves completely (7:23–25); and (4) he is exactly the high priest sinners need—holy, exalted, and having offered once for all the sacrifice of himself (7:26–28).

Key words bind the chapter together. Watch for μένω menō, “remain” (7:3, 24), and the cluster of words for permanence: εἰς τὸ διηνεκές eis to diēnekes, “perpetually” (7:3), εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα eis ton aiōna, “forever” (7:17, 21, 24, 28), πάντοτε pantote, “always” (7:25). Against these stand the words for mortality and weakness: “dying men” (7:8), “prevented by death” (7:23), “weakness” (7:18, 28). The chapter also introduces two great themes that will dominate chapters 8–10: τελείωσις teleiōsis, “perfection” (7:11; cf. 7:19, 28), meaning the bringing of worshipers to the goal of real, unhindered fellowship with God; and διαθήκη diathēkē, “covenant,” which appears for the first time in 7:22 and becomes the subject of chapter 8. Notice also the language of “drawing near” to God (7:19, 25), the priestly privilege that the new priest opens to all his people.

Two cautions help the reader. First, the author is not interested in Melchizedek for his own sake. He never mentions the bread and wine of Genesis 14:18, and he does not speculate about Melchizedek’s heavenly origins as some Jewish writers of the period did. His single concern is what Scripture’s portrait of Melchizedek reveals about the priesthood of the Son. Melchizedek is “made like the Son of God” (7:3), not the other way round. Second, the argument about the law in 7:11–19 must be read precisely. The author does not say the law was evil or that God made a mistake at Sinai. He says that the law, centered on a priesthood of mortal and sinful men, could not bring anyone to the goal God intended, and that God himself, in the Scriptures, announced its replacement. The whole chapter is an exercise in letting the Old Testament interpret itself.

For a congregation tempted to drift back toward the visible, venerable, and socially secure institutions of the old covenant, the chapter delivers both a warning and a magnificent comfort. To go back is to exchange a living priest for dying ones, an oath-sworn guarantee for a provisional arrangement, a once-for-all sacrifice for an endless round of offerings. To hold fast is to have a high priest who “always lives to intercede” for those who draw near to God through him (7:25).
