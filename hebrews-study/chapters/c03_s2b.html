3:12

See to it, brothers, that there will not be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, shown in falling away from the living God.

Now the author applies the psalm directly. The first word is an imperative, “See to it” or “Watch out” (βλέπετε blepete, from βλέπω blepō, “see, look, beware”). It is present tense, calling for continuing vigilance. The same verb returns at 12:25: “See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking.” The tender address “brothers” (ἀδελφοί adelphoi) recalls “holy brothers” in 3:1. The warning comes from a member of the family to the family. It does not come from an outsider passing judgment.

The construction that follows is unusual. μήποτε mēpote, “lest, that … not,” is followed here by a future indicative verb, ἔσται estai, “there will be.” In clauses of warning, Greek normally uses the subjunctive mood, which presents the danger as a possibility. The future indicative makes the danger more concrete and more pressing. It is as though the author says, “Watch out that there will not turn out to be….” He is not imagining a far-fetched case. He sees a real threat.

The warning is directed at “any of you” (ἔν τινι ὑμῶν en tini hymōn). The author is concerned not only for the congregation as a whole but for each individual member. One wandering heart is one too many. This individual concern runs through Hebrews’ exhortations: “that none of you may be hardened” (3:13); “lest any of you should seem to have fallen short” (4:1); “that no one may fall” (4:11); “that each of you show the same earnestness” (6:11); “see to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God” (12:15).

The danger is “an evil heart of unbelief” (καρδία πονηρὰ ἀπιστίας kardia ponēra apistias). The word “heart” picks up the psalm’s language (“do not harden your hearts,” “they always go astray in their heart”). The genitive “of unbelief” describes the kind of heart in view: a heart characterized by unbelief and producing it. It is “evil” (πονηρός ponēros), morally bad, wicked. Hebrews does not treat unbelief as an intellectual misfortune or an excusable weakness. It is a moral evil because it treats God as untrustworthy. To refuse to believe God’s promise is to call him a liar (compare 1 John 5:10). The word ἀπιστία apistia, “unbelief,” is the negative of pistis, “faith,” and it stands in deliberate contrast with Jesus, the “faithful” one (pistos, 3:2, 5). Moses called the wilderness generation “children in whom there is no faithfulness” (בָּנִים לֹא־אֵמֻן בָּם banim lo-emun bam, Deut 32:20). The danger for the readers was that they would become like them.

Unbelief shows itself “in falling away from the living God” (ἐν τῷ ἀποστῆναι ἀπὸ θεοῦ ζῶντος en tō apostēnai apo theou zōntos). The construction is an infinitive with the article, governed by the preposition “in” (en). It describes the way in which an evil heart of unbelief expresses itself. The verb ἀφίσταμαι aphistamai (here in the aorist infinitive apostēnai) means to go away, withdraw, depart, desert. It is the source of the English word “apostasy,” which means a deliberate turning away from God. Jesus used it in the parable of the sower of those who “believe for a while, and in a time of testing fall away” (Luke 8:13), and Paul used it of those who “will depart from the faith” in later times (1 Tim 4:1). The idea in Hebrews is not of a slip or a lapse but of a settled departure, turning one’s back on God.

The wilderness story supplies the background. When the people wanted to return to Egypt, Joshua and Caleb pleaded with them: “Only do not rebel against the Lord” (אַךְ בַּיהוָה אַל־תִּמְרֹדוּ akh bYHWH al-timrodu, Num 14:9). The Greek translation of Numbers renders this plea with the language of apostasy: in effect, “do not become apostates (apostatai) from the Lord.” Moses later warned them that they had “turned back from following the Lord” (Num 14:43). For the readers of Hebrews, the danger of falling away likely took the form of drifting back from open confession of Christ into a form of Judaism that did not acknowledge him. The author’s point is striking. To leave Christ for the synagogue would not be a return to the God of Israel. It would be apostasy “from the living God,” the very God who spoke to the fathers and has now spoken in his Son.

The title “the living God” (θεὸς ζῶν theos zōn) appears four times in Hebrews (3:12; 9:14; 10:31; 12:22). It is an Old Testament title that sets the true God against lifeless idols. It often appears where God speaks and acts with power. Moses asked, “Who of all flesh has heard the voice of the living God speaking from the midst of the fire, as we have, and lived?” (קוֹל אֱלֹהִים חַיִּים qol elohim chayyim, Deut 5:26). Joshua told Israel at the Jordan, “By this you will know that the living God is among you, and he will surely drive out before you the Canaanites” (Josh 3:10). The living God speaks, and the living God brings his people into the land. To fall away from him is to turn from the only source of life. It is also to fall into the hands of the one whose judgment is “a fearful thing” (10:31).

3:13

But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called “Today,” so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin.

The remedy for the danger of 3:12 is not, first of all, private introspection. It is the life of the congregation. “But exhort one another” (ἀλλὰ παρακαλεῖτε ἑαυτούς alla parakaleite heautous). The verb παρακαλέω parakaleō has a wide range: to urge, exhort, appeal, encourage, comfort. Its noun, παράκλησις paraklēsis, is the word the author uses to describe his whole sermon, “the word of exhortation” (13:22). Exhortation in the New Testament is neither nagging nor scolding. It is coming alongside a brother or sister with the truth of God’s word, to strengthen, to warn, and to encourage them to hold on. The pronoun ἑαυτούς heautous is literally “yourselves,” but here it has a reciprocal sense, “one another.” The members of the congregation are to do for each other what the author is doing for them in this sermon. Every believer is called to this ministry, not just the leaders.

Such exhortation is to happen “every day” (καθ’ ἑκάστην ἡμέραν kath’ hekastēn hēmeran). The present imperative also implies ongoing, habitual action. Hardening is not usually a sudden event. It happens by degrees, through small daily compromises and neglect, so the remedy must also be daily. The wording suggests that the members of the church were meant to be in frequent contact with one another, not just on the day of assembly. In 10:24–25 the author returns to this: “let us consider one another to stir up love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as some are in the habit of doing, but exhorting one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near.”

The time for exhortation is limited: “as long as it is called ‘Today’” (ἄχρις οὗ τὸ Σήμερον καλεῖται achris hou to Sēmeron kaleitai). The author takes the psalm’s word “Today” and treats it almost as a title, with the article: “the ‘Today.’” He means the present time of opportunity, the time during which God’s voice is still sounding and his invitation is still open. That time runs throughout the present age, until the coming “Day” (10:25) when Christ will appear a second time (9:28). But the phrase also carries a note of urgency for each hearer. The wilderness generation found that their “today” came to an end at Kadesh. No one can presume on an unlimited supply of tomorrows (compare 2 Cor 6:2: “Behold, now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation”).

The purpose of the exhortation is “so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin.” The verb is passive (σκληρυνθῇ sklērynthē, “be hardened”). In 3:8 the psalm commanded, “do not harden your hearts,” placing responsibility on the hearers. Here the author shows how hardening happens: a person is hardened “by the deceitfulness of sin” (ἀπάτῃ τῆς ἁμαρτίας apatē tēs hamartias). The noun ἀπάτη apatē means deception, deceit, or a seductive allure. Sin does not present itself honestly. It promises life and delivers death; it promises freedom and enslaves. It began that way in Eden (“The serpent deceived me,” Gen 3:13). Paul says that sin “deceived me and through it killed me” (Rom 7:11) and speaks of “the deceitful desires” of the old self (Eph 4:22). Jeremiah says, “The heart is more deceitful than all else” (עָקֹב הַלֵּב מִכֹּל aqov hallev mikkol, Jer 17:9). The word aqov means crooked or treacherous, from the same root as the name Jacob, “the one who grasps the heel,” who supplanted his brother by deceit.

How did sin deceive the wilderness generation? It made Egypt look safe and Canaan look impossible. It magnified the giants and shrank God. It made slavery seem attractive (“we remember the fish we ate in Egypt for nothing,” Num 11:5) and God’s promise seem a trap (“Why is the Lord bringing us into this land to fall by the sword?” Num 14:3). For the readers of Hebrews, sin’s deceit may have whispered that the old securities were safer than the reproach of Christ, and that the cost of confessing Jesus was too high. John Owen, the Puritan theologian who wrote a vast commentary on Hebrews, also wrote a treatise on the deceitfulness of indwelling sin. He traced how sin draws the mind away from vigilance, entices the affections, and gradually leads the will to consent. The remedy Hebrews offers is the voice of fellow believers who see what we cannot see in ourselves and who speak God’s truth to us before the hardening sets in.

3:14

For we have become partakers of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end,

The word “For” (γάρ gar) explains why such vigilance and mutual exhortation are needed. The stakes are high. Everything depends on continuing in what has begun. This verse closely parallels 3:6. Both sentences state a present reality and attach a condition of perseverance:

  • 3:6: “his house is what we are, if we hold fast our boldness and the boast of our hope.”
  • 3:14: “we have become partakers of Christ, if indeed we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end.”

The first clause is “we have become partakers of Christ” (μέτοχοι γὰρ τοῦ Χριστοῦ γεγόναμεν metochoi gar tou Christou gegonamen). The word metochoi appeared in 3:1 (“partakers of a heavenly calling”) and in 1:9, where the Son was anointed “beyond your companions.” What does it mean to be “partakers of Christ”? Interpreters take it in two main ways.

  • Sharers in Christ. Believers participate in Christ himself, sharing his life through union with him. This is close to Paul’s language of being “in Christ.”
  • Partners or companions of Christ. Believers are the Messiah’s companions, his associates, who share with him in his inheritance and kingdom. This reading draws on the use of the same word in 1:9 (“your companions”) and on the common Greek use of metochos for a business partner (compare Luke 5:7, where the fishermen beckon to their “partners”). The article with “Christ” (tou Christou, “the Christ”) may favor this reading, presenting him as the Messiah whose companions we are.

The two readings overlap. In 2:10–13 the author spoke of Jesus bringing “many sons to glory” and calling them his “brothers.” Believers share in Christ’s destiny because they belong to him. Whether the emphasis falls on sharing his life or sharing his inheritance, the point is the same: our hope rests entirely on our connection to him.

The verb γεγόναμεν gegonamen, “we have become,” is in the perfect tense. The perfect describes a past action whose results continue into the present. “We became partakers of Christ, and we are partakers still.” This tense is important for understanding the condition that follows.

The condition is introduced by ἐάνπερ eanper, a strengthened form of “if,” meaning “if indeed” or “provided that.” What must be held is “the beginning of our confidence” (τὴν ἀρχὴν τῆς ὑποστάσεως tēn archēn tēs hypostaseōs). The “beginning” is the confidence the readers had when they first believed. The author will remind them of it in 10:32–34, when “after you were enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings” and “joyfully accepted the plundering of your property.” They are to hold that early confidence “firm to the end” (μέχρι τέλους βεβαίαν mechri telous bebaian). The adjective βέβαιος bebaios means firm, steadfast, reliable. It is used in 2:2 of the message spoken through angels that “proved firm” and in 6:19 of hope as an anchor “sure and firm.” Beginning and end (archē and telos) frame the Christian life like the start and finish of a race (compare 12:1–2). The goal is not merely a good start but faithfulness to the finish.

Word Study: ὑπόστασις hypostasis

This word is used three times in Hebrews, each time with a different nuance. Literally it means “that which stands under,” a foundation, a substructure, an underlying reality. In 1:3 it refers to God’s “essential being,” the underlying reality of who God is, of which the Son is the exact imprint. In 11:1 it describes faith as the “assurance” of things hoped for. Here in 3:14 it means “confidence”: the steady, well-founded conviction that stands firm under pressure.

The development from “foundation” to “confidence” is natural. A person’s confidence is what he stands on. The Greek Old Testament can use the word where the Hebrew speaks of hope, as in Naomi’s words, “if I should say I have hope” (Ruth 1:12), and the psalmist’s “my hope is in you” (Ps 39:7 [Greek 38:8]). In 3:14 the word stands close to “boldness” and “hope” in the parallel verse 3:6. The readers’ “confidence” is not self-confidence. It is the firm conviction, grounded in God’s word and Christ’s work, with which they first embraced the gospel. They must not let it erode.

How should the relationship between the two clauses be understood? The question touches one of the most discussed issues in Hebrews, the relation between assurance and perseverance. It must be handled carefully, from the text.

Interpretive Options: “We have become … if we hold”

  • Perseverance as a condition for keeping salvation. Some interpreters, especially in the Wesleyan-Arminian tradition, read the verse to mean that believers truly share in Christ now but can lose that share if they fail to persevere. On this view the condition is a requirement for remaining in Christ.
  • Perseverance as the evidence of genuine participation. Others, including John Owen and many interpreters in the Reformed tradition, stress the perfect tense: “we have become partakers.” The condition does not say, “we will become partakers if we hold fast,” nor “we will remain partakers if we hold fast,” but “we have become partakers if we hold fast.” Holding fast to the end is the proof that the past event was real. Those who persevere show that they truly became partakers of Christ. Those who finally fall away show that, whatever their outward profession, they never truly shared in him.
  • A hypothetical warning. A third view treats the condition as hypothetical, describing something that cannot actually happen to true believers but is stated to motivate them. This view sometimes downplays the seriousness of the warnings, which the author clearly means his readers to take with full seriousness.

The grammar of the verse favors the second reading. The author does not make present membership in Christ depend on future perseverance in the sense of earning or retaining it. He makes future perseverance the evidence of present membership. That reading coheres with the wider teaching of Scripture. John writes of those who left the church: “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us” (1 John 2:19). Jesus taught that true disciples are those who “abide in my word” (John 8:31). Paul writes that God “will present you holy … if indeed you continue in the faith, stable and steadfast” (Col 1:22–23), and also that the one “who began a good work in you will bring it to completion” (Phil 1:6). Jesus promises that no one will snatch his sheep from his hand (John 10:28–29).

Yet the second reading must not be used to blunt the warning. The author does not tell his readers to sit back and examine whether they are among the elect. He tells them to hold fast, to exhort one another daily, to watch out. In Scripture, God’s preserving grace and the call to persevere do not compete. God keeps his people precisely through such warnings, exhortations, and the mutual care of the church. Hebrews itself expresses confidence about its readers (“we are sure of better things in your case, things that belong to salvation,” 6:9) while warning them in the strongest terms. The proper response to 3:14 is neither anxiety nor complacency. It is to hold fast to Christ, who holds us, and to help one another do the same.

3:15

as it is said, “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion.”

The author now repeats the opening lines of the psalm (Ps 95:7b–8a), introduced with the phrase “as it is said” (ἐν τῷ λέγεσθαι en tō legesthai, literally “in the being said”). This is another articular infinitive with “in” (compare 3:12), here in the present passive. It suggests that the psalm is being said, still sounding in the present. The repetition brings the three key ideas back before the hearers: “Today,” “his voice,” and “do not harden your hearts.” It also prepares for the questions of 3:16–18, which will explain “the rebellion” in the words of the psalm.

How this verse connects to its context is debated, because the phrase “as it is said” can be attached in more than one direction:

  • Some connect it with 3:14: we must hold our confidence firm to the end, in keeping with the psalm’s call not to harden our hearts.
  • Some connect it with 3:13, treating 3:14 as a parenthesis: exhort one another daily … in keeping with the word, “Today, if you hear his voice.”
  • Some begin a new sentence with 3:15 and connect it with the questions of 3:16: “When it says, ‘Today … do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion,’ who were they who heard and rebelled?” The word “for” (gar) at the start of 3:16 makes this reading somewhat awkward, though not impossible.

The first two options are closest to the flow of the Greek, and they amount to much the same thing. The psalm’s “Today” provides both the urgency and the framework for the whole exhortation of 3:12–14. What the hearers must do (watch, exhort, hold fast) is exactly what the psalm demands when it says, “do not harden your hearts.” Whatever the precise syntax, the effect of the repetition is plain. The author wants the voice of the psalm to ring in his hearers’ ears as the Spirit’s living word to them, not a recollection of what God once said to others.

Hearing God’s voice “today” is a gift as well as a responsibility. The very fact that God is still speaking means the door has not yet closed. The wilderness generation heard God’s voice and hardened their hearts. The readers of Hebrews hear God’s voice in his Son, a far greater revelation (1:1–2; 2:1–4). It is an act of mercy that he is still calling.

3:16

For who were they who heard and rebelled? Was it not all those who came out of Egypt through Moses?

The author now turns from application to history, using a rhetorical device common in ancient preaching and teaching. He poses a series of questions, each answered by another question. There are three pairs (3:16, 17, 18). Each takes up a phrase from the psalm and identifies its referent from the account in Numbers. The first concerns the psalm’s phrase “as in the rebellion”: “who were they who heard and rebelled?” (τίνες γὰρ ἀκούσαντες παρεπίκραναν; tines gar akousantes parepikranan?). The verb παραπικραίνω parapikrainō, “rebel, provoke, embitter,” is the verb related to the noun parapikrasmos, “rebellion,” in 3:8 and 3:15. The participle “having heard” (akousantes) is significant. These were people who heard God’s voice, just as the readers have heard it. Hearing was not the problem; responding was.

The answer: “Was it not all those who came out of Egypt through Moses?” The form of the question (ἀλλ’ οὐ all’ ou, “but [was it] not…?”) expects the answer “yes.” The rebels were not a fringe group of obvious unbelievers. They were the people of the exodus, the generation that had seen the plagues, eaten the Passover, walked through the sea on dry ground, and sung the song of Moses. They had experienced the greatest act of redemption in the Old Testament.

And they came out “through Moses” (διὰ Μωϋσέως dia Mōuseōs). This phrase links the section firmly to 3:1–6. The faithful servant led them out, but his faithfulness could not substitute for their faith. Having the best of leaders and the most wonderful of beginnings did not guarantee arrival. Paul makes the same point in 1 Corinthians 10:1–5. All the fathers were under the cloud, all passed through the sea, all were baptized into Moses, all ate the same spiritual food and drank the same spiritual drink, “nevertheless, with most of them God was not pleased, for they were struck down in the wilderness.” Jude also reminds his readers that “the Lord, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe” (Jude 5).

The word “all” (πάντες pantes) is a rhetorical generalization, not a statistical one. The author knows that Joshua and Caleb were exceptions (Num 14:24, 30, 38; 26:65), and he refers to Joshua in 4:8. But the exceptions were so few that the rebellion is rightly described as the act of the whole generation.

Interpretive Options: Question or Statement?

The earliest Greek manuscripts were written without accents, so the first word of 3:16, tines, could be read in two ways. With an accent on the first syllable (τίνες), it is the interrogative “who?” Without an accent, or with an accent on the second syllable, it is the indefinite pronoun “some.”

  • As a statement. Taking the word as “some,” the verse would read: “For some, when they had heard, rebelled, though not all who came out of Egypt through Moses.” This was the reading of the King James Version. It emphasizes that not everyone rebelled, since Joshua and Caleb were faithful.
  • As a question. Taking the word as “who?” the verse reads: “For who were they who heard and rebelled? Was it not all those who came out of Egypt through Moses?” This is the reading of nearly all modern editions and translations.

The context decides clearly in favor of the question. Verses 17 and 18 both begin with the same interrogative pronoun in another case (τίσιν tisin, “with whom?” “to whom?”), and each is answered with another question. Verse 16 is evidently the first of three parallel question-and-answer pairs. Moreover, the author’s point is not that a few rebelled and most were faithful. It is the reverse. Almost the whole redeemed generation rebelled, which is exactly what makes the warning so sobering.

3:17

And with whom was he angry for forty years? Was it not with those who sinned, whose corpses fell in the wilderness?

The second question takes up another phrase of the psalm: “for forty years … I was angry” (3:10). “With whom was he angry for forty years?” (τίσιν δὲ προσώχθισεν τεσσεράκοντα ἔτη; tisin de prosōchthisen tesserakonta etē?). Here, as noted at 3:10, the author joins “forty years” to God’s anger, in line with the original wording of the psalm. Taken together with 3:9–10, this shows that the forty years were both a time of God’s works and a time of God’s displeasure. Grace and judgment ran side by side. The same people who were fed with manna were the people whose bodies would fall in the desert.

The answer: “Was it not with those who sinned?” (οὐχὶ τοῖς ἁμαρτήσασιν ouchi tois hamartēsasin). The aorist participle “those who sinned” points to the decisive sin at Kadesh-barnea, the refusal to enter the land. God’s anger was not arbitrary. It was directed against sin, and specifically against the sin of unbelief, as 3:19 will make clear. The emphatic negative οὐχί ouchi again expects the answer “yes.”

Their fate is described in stark terms: “whose corpses fell in the wilderness” (ὧν τὰ κῶλα ἔπεσεν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ hōn ta kōla epesen en tē erēmō). The word κῶλον kōlon means literally a limb or member of the body, and in the plural it came to mean dead bodies, corpses. It is the word the Greek translation of Numbers uses for God’s sentence: “In this wilderness your corpses shall fall” (Num 14:29, 32). The Hebrew is בַּמִּדְבָּר הַזֶּה יִפְּלוּ פִגְרֵיכֶם bammidbar hazzeh yippelu figreikhem, where peger is a corpse. The language is deliberately vivid. The author wants his hearers to picture a desert strewn with the graves of a generation that started well and never arrived. The Numbers narrative adds a terrible detail. The people had said, “If only we had died in this wilderness!” (Num 14:2), and God answered, “As I live, … what you have spoken in my hearing, so I will do to you” (Num 14:28). Unbelief received exactly what it asked for.

The image recalls Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 10:5: “they were struck down in the wilderness.” In both passages, the purpose is not to satisfy curiosity about Israel’s history. It is to warn the church. Those who have begun the journey with God can still perish along the way if they turn from him in unbelief.

3:18

And to whom did he swear that they would not enter his rest, if not to those who disobeyed?

The third question takes up the last line of the psalm: “So I swore in my wrath, ‘They shall not enter my rest’” (3:11). “To whom did he swear that they would not enter his rest?” (τίσιν δὲ ὤμοσεν μὴ εἰσελεύσεσθαι εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσιν αὐτοῦ tisin de ōmosen mē eiseleusesthai eis tēn katapausin autou). The author now states the oath plainly, in ordinary Greek, with a negative and a future infinitive, “that they would not enter.” In the psalm it was expressed in the Hebrew idiom “if they shall enter.” The meaning is the same.

The answer: “if not to those who disobeyed?” (εἰ μὴ τοῖς ἀπειθήσασιν ei mē tois apeithēsasin). The verb ἀπειθέω apeitheō means to disobey, to refuse to comply. It is built on the same root as peithō, “persuade,” so it carries the idea of refusing to be persuaded, refusing to yield. In the New Testament it is the opposite of believing and obeying the gospel (John 3:36: “whoever does not obey the Son shall not see life”; compare Rom 10:21; 1 Pet 2:7–8). In Hebrews the related noun ἀπείθεια apeitheia, “disobedience,” will appear twice in the next chapter (4:6, 11).

The progression through the three questions is instructive. Those who perished are described as those who “rebelled” (3:16), those who “sinned” (3:17), and those who “disobeyed” (3:18). The next verse will add the root of it all: “unbelief” (3:19). These are not four different groups but four descriptions of the same people, viewed from different angles. Rebellion, sin, disobedience, and unbelief belong together.

Old Testament Text: Deuteronomy 1:26, 32 and 9:23

When Moses recalled the events at Kadesh-barnea, he described the people’s sin in exactly the two terms Hebrews uses in 3:18–19, disobedience and unbelief.

Deut 1:26: וְלֹא אֲבִיתֶם לַעֲלֹת וַתַּמְרוּ אֶת־פִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם׃ — velo avitem la’alot vattamru et-pi YHWH eloheikhem, “But you were not willing to go up, and you rebelled against the command [literally, the mouth] of the Lord your God.”

Deut 1:32: וּבַדָּבָר הַזֶּה אֵינְכֶם מַאֲמִינִם בַּיהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם׃ — uvaddavar hazzeh einkhem ma’aminim bYHWH eloheikhem, “Yet in spite of this word you did not believe the Lord your God.”

Deut 9:23: וּבִשְׁלֹחַ יְהוָה אֶתְכֶם מִקָּדֵשׁ בַּרְנֵעַ לֵאמֹר עֲלוּ וּרְשׁוּ אֶת־הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר נָתַתִּי לָכֶם וַתַּמְרוּ אֶת־פִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם וְלֹא הֶאֱמַנְתֶּם לוֹ וְלֹא שְׁמַעְתֶּם בְּקֹלוֹ׃ — uvishloach YHWH etkhem miqqadesh barnea … vattamru et-pi YHWH eloheikhem velo he’emantem lo velo shema’tem beqolo, “When the Lord sent you from Kadesh-barnea, saying, ‘Go up and take possession of the land that I have given you,’ you rebelled against the command of the Lord your God, and you did not believe him and did not listen to his voice.”

The Hebrew verb for rebel, marah, means to be contrary, to defy. The verb for believe, he’emin, comes from the same root as ne’eman, “faithful,” in Numbers 12:7 (see 3:2). This is the same Hebrew root as “amen.” Moses was ne’eman, faithful, but the people did not he’emin, believe. Deuteronomy 9:23 gathers into one sentence nearly every element of Hebrews 3: Kadesh-barnea, the command to enter, rebellion, unbelief, and refusing to listen to God’s voice. The author of Hebrews did not need to invent his interpretation of Psalm 95. He found it already in Moses’ own account. The Greek translation of Deuteronomy 1:26 uses the same verb Hebrews uses here, apeitheō, “disobey.”

3:19

So we see that they were not able to enter because of unbelief.

The conclusion is brief and decisive. “So we see” (καὶ βλέπομεν kai blepomen) echoes “See to it” (blepete) in 3:12. What the hearers were told to watch out for in themselves, they now see clearly in the history of Israel. The present tense “we see” describes the conclusion that anyone can draw from the scriptural record just reviewed. The author includes himself again (“we”).

“They were not able to enter” (οὐκ ἠδυνήθησαν εἰσελθεῖν ouk ēdynēthēsan eiselthein). The author does not say merely that they were not permitted, though that is true, since God had sworn. He says they were not able. That is a striking choice of words when set beside the story in Numbers. When the spies returned, Caleb said, “Let us go up at once and possess it, for we are well able to overcome it” (כִּי־יָכוֹל נוּכַל לָהּ ki-yakhol nukhal lah, Num 13:30). The other spies replied, “We are not able to go up against the people” (לֹא נוּכַל לַעֲלוֹת lo nukhal la’alot, Num 13:31). The ten spies thought their inability lay in the strength of the Canaanites. In fact it lay in their own unbelief. When they later tried to enter on their own strength, after God had sworn his oath, they were indeed unable and were driven back (Num 14:40–45). Unbelief made them unable. It shut them out from the only power that could have brought them in.

The cause is stated last for emphasis: “because of unbelief” (δι’ ἀπιστίαν di’ apistian). The word apistia closes the paragraph just as it appeared in its first warning (3:12, “an evil heart of unbelief”). The repetition frames the whole passage. The root of the wilderness tragedy was not lack of evidence, lack of leadership, or lack of opportunity. They had God’s works, Moses’ leadership, and God’s promise. What they lacked was faith. In 3:18 the author called them “those who disobeyed,” and now he traces their disobedience to its source in unbelief. In biblical thought, unbelief and disobedience are two sides of one coin. Not to trust God’s promise is to refuse his command, and refusing his command reveals that one does not trust his promise. God had said of the generation at Kadesh, “How long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs that I have done among them?” (וְעַד־אָנָה לֹא־יַאֲמִינוּ בִי ve’ad-anah lo-ya’aminu vi, Num 14:11). Psalm 78 summed up the history in the same way: “they did not believe in God and did not trust his salvation” (Ps 78:22), and “in spite of all this they still sinned and did not believe in his wonders” (Ps 78:32).

This verse also prepares for what follows. Chapter 4 begins, “Therefore, let us fear,” and goes on to say that “we who have believed enter that rest” (4:3). The contrast is drawn sharply. Unbelief excludes, and faith enters. Later, in chapter 11, the author will present the long line of those who, unlike the wilderness generation, lived by faith and “obtained promises” (11:33). Chief among them is Jesus himself, “the pioneer and perfecter of faith” (12:2). The way to avoid the fate of the fathers is not to try harder in one’s own strength, as they did when it was too late. It is to trust the living God and his faithful Son, to keep listening to his voice “Today,” and to help one another do the same.

The section continues into chapter 4. There the author turns from the warning of the psalm to its promise. A rest remains for the people of God. Since God’s “Today” is still sounding, the readers must “strive to enter that rest, so that no one may fall by the same pattern of disobedience” (4:11).

For Reflection

  1. The author introduces Psalm 95 with “as the Holy Spirit says” (present tense), not “as David wrote.” How does hearing Scripture as God’s present voice “Today” change the way you read it?
  2. Israel had seen God’s works for forty years and still hardened their hearts. What does this teach about the difference between witnessing God’s power and trusting his word?
  3. Verse 12 traces apostasy to “an evil heart of unbelief.” Why does Hebrews locate the root of falling away in unbelief rather than in outward behavior alone?
  4. The remedy in verse 13 is mutual: “exhort one another every day.” Who in your life exhorts you, and whom are you responsible to encourage? What would daily exhortation look like in practice?
  5. Verse 14 says “we have become partakers of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end.” How does perseverance serve as evidence of genuine union with Christ (compare 3:6)?
