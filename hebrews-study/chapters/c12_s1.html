Looking to Jesus (12:1–2)

Translation

1So then, we too—since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us—let us lay aside every weight and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with endurance the race that lies before us, 2looking away to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith, who for the sake of the joy that lay before him endured a cross, despising its shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.

Setting and Structure

These two verses form a single Greek sentence, and they are the hinge between the great roll call of faith in chapter 11 and the exhortations of chapters 12–13. The sentence has one main verb, a first-person plural exhortation: τρέχωμεν trechōmen, “let us run.” Everything else is arranged around it. Before the verb come the reason (we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses) and the preparation (laying aside every weight and the entangling sin). After it come the manner (with endurance), the object (the race set before us), and the focus (looking away to Jesus). The sentence then expands into a short confession of Christ: his goal (the joy set before him), his suffering (he endured a cross), his attitude (despising the shame), and his present position (he has sat down at God’s right hand).

The preacher has used the first-person plural exhortation (“let us …”) at the key turning points of his sermon: “let us fear” (4:1), “let us strive” (4:11), “let us hold fast” (4:14; 10:23), “let us draw near” (4:16; 10:22), “let us press on” (6:1), “let us consider one another” (10:24). Here he adds “let us run.” By saying “we too,” he places himself beside his hearers as a fellow runner, not as a spectator shouting from the stands.

The image is drawn from the athletic games that were a central feature of Greek and Roman city life. The four great “crown” festivals—the Olympic, Pythian, Isthmian, and Nemean games—drew competitors and spectators from across the Mediterranean, and countless local games imitated them. In Judea itself, Herod the Great had built a theater in Jerusalem and an amphitheater in the plain nearby, and established games in honor of Caesar (Josephus, Ant. 15.268–271), to the scandal of many Jews; earlier still, the building of a gymnasium in Jerusalem under the high priest Jason had become a symbol of compromise with Greek culture (1 Macc 1:14–15; 2 Macc 4:9–15). Yet Jewish writers also adopted the language of the games for the moral and spiritual life. 4 Maccabees, written perhaps in the first century, describes the martyrdom of Eleazar and the seven brothers as a divine contest (ἀγών agōn) with the world as its spectators and immortality as its prize (4 Macc 17:11–16). Paul used the same imagery repeatedly (1 Cor 9:24–27; Phil 3:12–14; 2 Tim 4:7–8). The readers of Hebrews would have instantly pictured the stadium: the runners stripped for the race, the long straight track, the crowds in the tiered seats on either side, the judge’s stand at the finish line.

The flow from chapter 11 is essential. The “witnesses” of 12:1 are the men and women of chapter 11; the “faith” of 12:2 is the faith that chapter 11 has defined and illustrated (11:1, 6). But chapter 11 closed with a striking qualification: none of those heroes received what was promised, “since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they would not be made perfect” (11:39–40). Chapter 12 explains what that “something better” is. It is Jesus himself, who has gone further than any of them. Abraham looked forward to the city (11:10); Moses looked to the reward (11:26); Jesus has not only looked to the joy set before him but has arrived and taken his seat. That is why the readers are told to look away from even the greatest of the witnesses to Jesus. He is not merely the last and greatest name on the list. He is the one in whom faith reached its goal.

The vocabulary also reaches back to earlier passages. Jesus was called “the pioneer (ἀρχηγός archēgos) of their salvation,” made perfect through sufferings (2:10). He “endured” as the faithful Son (3:6), learned obedience from what he suffered (5:8), and “sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high” (1:3; 8:1; 10:12). The readers had already been told that they “need endurance” (10:36) and that God’s righteous one lives by faith and does not shrink back (10:38–39). In 12:1–2 all of these threads are tied together. The faith that endures is a faith fixed on the Jesus who endured.

Verse by Verse

12:1

So then, we too—since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us—let us lay aside every weight and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with endurance the race that lies before us,

“So then, we too.” The opening Τοιγαροῦν toigaroun is a rare and emphatic word (elsewhere in the New Testament only at 1 Thess 4:8). It draws a firm conclusion from what precedes: in light of everything chapter 11 has shown, here is what follows. The phrase “we too” (καὶ ἡμεῖς kai hēmeis) sets the readers in the same line as the faithful of old. The race those men and women ran is not finished; it continues, and now it is our turn on the track.

“Since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us.” The word νέφος nephos, “cloud,” was used in Greek for a dense mass or multitude. Homer could speak of a “cloud” of foot soldiers following their leaders, and later writers used the same idiom for any great throng. The point is not vagueness but number and density: the people of faith who have gone before are a vast company. The participle περικείμενον perikeimenon, “lying around, surrounding,” pictures that company encircling us, as the banked seats of a stadium encircled the runners.

Word Study: μαρτύρων martyrōn, “witnesses”

The noun μάρτυς martys means a witness, someone who testifies to what he knows. In the second century it came to mean a “martyr,” one who bears witness by death, but that technical sense had not yet developed when Hebrews was written, though it was on its way (compare Acts 22:20; Rev 2:13). In chapter 11 the preacher has repeatedly used the related verb μαρτυρέω martyreō, “to bear witness, testify,” in the passive voice: the ancients “were attested” or “received a good testimony” (11:2); Abel “was attested as righteous, God testifying about his gifts” (11:4); Enoch “was attested as having pleased God” (11:5); and all these “were attested through their faith” (11:39). In every case it is God who bears witness to them. At the same time, these people themselves testify: Abel “still speaks” through his faith though he died (11:4), and the patriarchs “made it clear” by their words that they were seeking a homeland (11:14).

Interpretive Options: Spectators or Testifiers?

  • Spectators. Because the image is a race in a stadium, many readers have pictured the saints of chapter 11 as spectators in the stands, watching and cheering the present generation of runners. The participle “surrounding us” supports some such picture, and 4 Maccabees 17:14 likewise speaks of the world and humanity as the onlookers at the martyrs’ contest.
  • Testifiers. Others stress that martys never simply means “spectator”; it means one who testifies. On this view, the faithful of chapter 11 surround us not chiefly to watch us but to witness to us—by their lives, attested by God, they testify that faith in God’s promise is worth everything.

The word itself and its use throughout chapter 11 point first to the second view. The preacher’s concern is what these witnesses say to us, not what they see of us: they are living proof, attested by God, that faith endures and that God keeps his word. The athletic setting does color the image, so that a reader naturally feels the encouragement of a great crowd that has run this course before. But the text is not teaching that departed believers are monitoring the details of our lives; Scripture is reserved on that question, and Hebrews does not build anything on it. The witnesses are an encouragement because of what they testify about God. Notice, too, that they are not the focus of the runner’s gaze: the eyes are fixed on Jesus (12:2).

“Let us lay aside every weight.” The participle ἀποθέμενοι apothemenoi (from ἀποτίθημι apotithēmi, “put off, lay aside”) is in the aorist tense, describing an action that comes before and makes possible the running. The same verb is used elsewhere in the New Testament for putting off sinful practices like a garment (Rom 13:12; Eph 4:22, 25; Col 3:8; Jas 1:21; 1 Pet 2:1). Greek runners competed naked, precisely so that nothing would hinder them; they stripped off their clothing at the start of the race.

Word Study: ὄγκος onkos, “weight”

The noun ὄγκος onkos occurs only here in the New Testament. It means bulk, mass, or a burden; it could be used of excess body weight that an athlete worked to shed in training, as well as of anything heavy that impedes movement. The word is distinguished from “the sin” that follows, joined by “and.” The preacher seems to mean that some things that slow the runner are not in themselves sinful; they are simply weights. A runner does not carry even good things that keep him from finishing. For the readers, such weights might have included attachments to the security and respectability they had once enjoyed (10:34), fear of further loss, or the pull of old patterns of worship that had been fulfilled in Christ (13:9–14). The word “every” (πάντα panta) is placed last in its phrase for emphasis: every weight, without exception.

“And the sin that so easily entangles.” The article “the” before “sin” may point to a particular sin, or to sin in general viewed as a single power. Since the whole sermon warns against “an evil heart of unbelief that turns away from the living God” and against being “hardened by the deceitfulness of sin” (3:12–13), and since chapter 11 has been about faith, many commentators reasonably see here especially the sin of unbelief that leads to drawing back (10:38–39). Yet the preacher does not narrow it so tightly that any sin is excluded; Owen applied the phrase to the indwelling sin that clings to every believer, and that application is fully in keeping with the text. Whatever the sin, it must be laid aside as decisively as a runner drops his cloak.

Textual Note: “Easily entangling” or “easily distracting”?

The adjective εὐπερίστατον euperistaton is not found in any surviving Greek writing before Hebrews, so its meaning must be inferred from its parts: εὖ eu, “well, easily,” and περιΐστημι periistēmi, “to stand around, surround.” The most likely sense is “easily surrounding,” that is, besetting or entangling, like a long garment wrapping itself around a runner’s legs. Other suggested meanings (“much admired,” “easily avoided,” “dangerous”) have found little support. The oldest manuscript of Hebrews, the papyrus P46 (about AD 200), reads instead εὐπερίσπαστον euperispaston, “easily distracting.” Metzger’s committee kept the reading of the great majority of witnesses, judging the P46 reading to be an early attempt to replace a rare word with a more understandable one. Either reading describes how sin works on a runner: it trips and it distracts.

“Let us run with endurance the race that lies before us.” The verb τρέχωμεν trechōmen is a present subjunctive: “let us keep on running.” The present tense pictures ongoing action—not a sprint, but a sustained effort. The phrase “with endurance” (δι’ ὑπομονῆς di’ hypomonēs, literally “through endurance”) names the quality the readers most need. The preacher has just quoted Habakkuk: “you have need of endurance” (10:36). ὑπομονή hypomonē is not passive resignation but steadfast perseverance under pressure, the capacity to remain on course when the course is long and hard. The race of faith is not won by the fastest start but by finishing.

The word ἀγών agōn, “race, contest,” referred to any athletic competition, and from there to any struggle or conflict (Phil 1:30; Col 2:1; 1 Thess 2:2; 1 Tim 6:12; 2 Tim 4:7). The preacher will shortly widen the image to include combat (12:4). The race is said to “lie before us” (προκείμενον prokeimenon, from πρόκειμαι prokeimai). We do not choose its route or length; it is set before us by God, as the course of the games was set by the officials. The same verb will describe the joy set before Jesus in the next verse. The runner’s course and the Savior’s joy are both appointed realities that lie ahead.

The Old Testament already linked endurance with running. Isaiah promised that “those who wait for the Lord will renew their strength … they will run and not grow weary; they will walk and not faint” (Isa 40:31; Hebrew יָרוּצוּ וְלֹא יִיגָעוּ yarutsu velo yiga‘u, “they will run and not grow weary”). Hebrews will echo that promise of strength for the weary in 12:3 and 12:12. The life of faith is a long-distance race, and its strength does not come from the runner himself.

12:2

looking away to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith, who for the sake of the joy that lay before him endured a cross, despising its shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.

“Looking away to Jesus.” The participle ἀφορῶντες aphorōntes (from ἀφοράω aphoraō) combines “to look” with the prefix ἀπό apo, “away from.” It means to look away from everything else in order to fix one’s eyes on a single object. The present tense describes a continuing gaze, the runner’s steady attention to the goal. Moses endured “as seeing him who is invisible” and “looked away to the reward” (11:26–27, using a closely related verb); now the readers are to look to Jesus. The memorial for the Maccabean martyrs in 4 Maccabees 17:10 praises them for looking away to God and enduring torment to the point of death; Hebrews directs the same steady gaze to Jesus, a striking indication of how fully the preacher identifies Jesus with God (1:3, 8, 10).

The verse uses the simple human name “Jesus,” placed emphatically at the end of its phrase in Greek. The preacher often uses the bare name when he wants to stress Jesus’ true humanity and earthly suffering (2:9; 3:1; 6:20; 7:22; 10:19; 13:12). The one we look to is not an abstraction but the man who ran this race in flesh and blood. Notice, too, what the runner is not told to look at: not the other runners, not the witnesses in the stands, not his own progress, and not the obstacles. Even the heroes of chapter 11 are not the focus. They witness; Jesus is the one we watch.

Word Study: ἀρχηγὸν καὶ τελειωτήν archēgon kai teleiōtēn, “pioneer and perfecter”

ἀρχηγός archēgos, rendered “pioneer” throughout this study, is built from ἀρχή archē, “beginning, first place,” and the verb ἄγω agō, “to lead.” It can mean a leader who goes first and opens a way for others, a founder, or an originator (compare Acts 3:15; 5:31). In 2:10 Jesus is the archēgos who leads “many sons to glory,” having been made perfect through sufferings. The word combines priority and leadership: he goes first, and he brings others with him.

τελειωτής teleiōtēs, “perfecter, one who brings to completion,” is not found in any earlier surviving Greek writing and may have been coined by the author. It belongs to the family of τελειόω teleioō, “to bring to perfection, complete,” which is one of the key words of Hebrews: Jesus was “made perfect” through suffering (2:10; 5:9; 7:28), the law “made nothing perfect” (7:19), but by a single offering Christ “has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified” (10:14), and the faithful of old will not be “made perfect” apart from us (11:40). The single article governing both nouns (τὸν … ἀρχηγὸν καὶ τελειωτήν) presents them as two sides of one role: Jesus is both the one who begins and leads the way of faith and the one who brings it to its full completion.

“Of faith”—not “our faith.” Many English versions read “the author and finisher of our faith,” but the Greek has no word for “our.” It reads τῆς πίστεως tēs pisteōs, “of the faith” or simply “of faith,” with the article referring back to the faith described throughout chapter 11. The difference matters. If we add “our,” the phrase speaks mainly of Jesus as the source who creates faith in us and sustains it to the end—a precious truth taught elsewhere (Eph 2:8; Phil 1:6; 1:29). Without “our,” the phrase says something more, and something that fits the context exactly. Chapter 11 has paraded the great examples of faith; now the preacher names the one in whom faith itself reached its perfect expression. Jesus lived the life of trust in God from beginning to end. Hebrews has already put on his lips the words, “I will put my trust in him” (2:13, quoting Isa 8:17), and has said that he “learned obedience through what he suffered” (5:8) and was “faithful to him who appointed him” (3:2). He ran the whole course of faith without stumbling, through the cross to the throne. He is the pioneer because he went first on that road and opened it for us; he is the perfecter because he brought faith to its goal in his own person and, as our high priest, brings it to completion in those who follow him. The two senses are not rivals. Because he has run the race of faith to the finish, he is able to bring his people to the finish (7:25).

“Who for the sake of the joy that lay before him endured a cross.” The phrase ἀντὶ τῆς προκειμένης αὐτῷ χαρᾶς anti tēs prokeimenēs autō charas has long been debated because the preposition ἀντί anti can mean either “instead of” or “in exchange for, for the sake of.”

Interpretive Options: “For the sake of” or “instead of” the joy?

  • “Instead of the joy.” Some ancient and modern interpreters take anti in its common sense of substitution: Jesus chose the cross instead of a joy that was available to him. Some identify that joy with the glory he had with the Father before the incarnation, which he laid aside (Phil 2:6–8; John 17:5); others with the joy he could have had by avoiding the cross (Matt 26:53). This reading can appeal to Moses, who chose ill-treatment with God’s people “rather than” the passing pleasures of sin (11:25).
  • “For the sake of the joy.” Others take anti in its sense of exchange or price: Jesus endured the cross in order to obtain the joy set before him, as a runner endures the race for the prize. This is the view of most modern commentators.

Three features of the text favor the second view. First, the joy is described as προκειμένης prokeimenēs, “lying before him,” the same verb used one line earlier of the race “lying before us” (12:1). In both cases something lies ahead as a future goal, not something already possessed and given up. A joy that already belonged to the pre-incarnate Son would hardly be described as “set before him.” Second, the same preposition appears in this same chapter in exactly this sense of exchange: Esau sold his birthright ἀντὶ βρώσεως μιᾶς anti brōseōs mias, “in exchange for a single meal” (12:16). The preacher sets the two exchanges side by side: Esau gave up the eternal for a moment’s pleasure; Jesus endured a moment’s shame for eternal joy. Third, Hebrews consistently presents faith as looking ahead to the reward (10:35; 11:6, 26), and Jesus is here the supreme example of the faith of chapter 11. Moses did choose suffering “rather than” pleasure, but he did so because “he was looking to the reward” (11:26)—which is exactly the logic of the second view. The truth emphasized by the first view, the Son’s self-humbling (Phil 2:6–8), is taught elsewhere in Scripture and in Hebrews itself (2:9, 14–17), but it is not the point of this phrase.

What was the joy? The context suggests his exaltation to God’s right hand, mentioned at the end of the verse, together with the fruit of his suffering: “bringing many sons to glory” (2:10), the people for whom he would be a merciful high priest. Isaiah’s Servant song speaks in the same way of joy beyond suffering: “Out of the anguish of his soul he will see; he will be satisfied” (Isa 53:11; Hebrew מֵעֲמַל נַפְשׁוֹ יִרְאֶה יִשְׂבָּע me‘amal nafsho yir’eh yisba‘). The cross was not the goal; it was the road to the goal. Jesus endured it because he saw past it.

The verb ὑπέμεινεν hypemeinen, “he endured,” is from the same root as “endurance” in 12:1. The preacher deliberately connects them: the endurance required of us is the endurance first displayed by Jesus. And note that “cross” (σταυρόν stauron) has no article in Greek. This is the only explicit mention of the cross in Hebrews, and the anarthrous form likely emphasizes its character: he endured a cross—that kind of death, the most degrading death the ancient world knew.

Historical Background: The Shame of the Cross

Modern readers, accustomed to the cross as a religious symbol, can easily miss how shocking this phrase was. Crucifixion was a Roman punishment reserved mostly for slaves, rebels, and the lowest classes; Roman citizens were generally exempt. It was designed not only to kill slowly and painfully but to humiliate publicly: the victim was usually stripped, displayed along a road or in a public place, mocked, and often left unburied. Cicero, defending a Roman senator in his speech Pro Rabirio, argued that the very word “cross” should be kept far away not only from the body of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes, and his ears; elsewhere (Against Verres 2.5.165) he called it the most cruel and loathsome of punishments. Polite society did not talk about it.

For Jews, there was an added horror. The law said that one hanged on a tree is “cursed by God” (Deut 21:23; Hebrew קִלְלַת אֱלֹהִים תָּלוּי qilelat Elohim talui, “a curse of God is the one hanged”), a text Paul applies to Christ’s death (Gal 3:13). Paul knew that the message of “Christ crucified” was “a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles” (1 Cor 1:23).

The first-century Mediterranean world was an honor-and-shame culture, in which a person’s worth was measured largely by public reputation. David deSilva has shown how much of Hebrews’ exhortation addresses this world: the readers had been “publicly exposed to reproaches and afflictions” (10:33), and the pressure to restore their honor in the eyes of their neighbors was a powerful temptation to abandon Christ. In that setting, the words “despising the shame” are revolutionary. Jesus did not merely tolerate shame; he treated the verdict of human society as worthless compared with the verdict of God, who seated him at his right hand. The readers are called to do the same: to go to him “outside the camp, bearing his reproach” (13:13), counting reproach for Christ as greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt (11:26).

“Despising its shame.” The participle καταφρονήσας kataphronēsas, from καταφρονέω kataphroneō, means to look down on, think little of, disregard. It does not mean that Jesus felt no shame or that the cross was not truly shameful. It means that he refused to let the shame govern his choices. Measured against the joy set before him, the world’s contempt counted for nothing. The aorist tense views his attitude as a single, decisive act, matching the once-for-all character of his suffering.

“And has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.” The sentence ends with a change of tense that carries much of its message. “Endured” and “despising” are aorists, describing completed events. But “has sat down” is a perfect: κεκάθικεν kekathiken. The Greek perfect describes a past action whose result continues in the present. Jesus sat down, and he remains seated. The cross is over; the enthronement is permanent. The shame was temporary; the honor is eternal.

Hebrews has repeatedly drawn on Psalm 110:1 to describe the exalted Jesus (1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12), and here it does so for the last time. In Hebrew the psalm reads נְאֻם יְהוָה לַאדֹנִי שֵׁב לִימִינִי ne’um YHWH ladoni shev limini, “The declaration of the Lord to my lord: ‘Sit at my right hand.’” In earlier chapters the session at God’s right hand showed that Jesus’ priestly sacrifice was finished (10:11–14: the Levitical priests stand daily, but Christ sat down). Here it shows the reward of his endurance. As in 8:1, the preacher speaks of the throne: Jesus sits at the right hand of “the throne of God,” the very throne that 4:16 called “the throne of grace,” to which believers may now draw near with boldness. The runner who looks to Jesus is not looking at a distant hero but at a living, reigning high priest, who is seated where he can help (2:18; 4:15–16; 7:25).

The verse thus traces the whole shape of the Christian life in the shape of Christ’s own: a joy set before, a cross endured, shame despised, and a throne attained. The readers were tempted to regard their suffering and loss of honor as signs that they had chosen the wrong path. The preacher answers by pointing to Jesus. His path, too, went through shame. It ended at the right hand of God.

For Reflection

  1. The “witnesses” of chapter 11 testify that faith in God’s promise is worth everything. Which of them speaks most clearly to your own situation, and what does his or her life testify to you?
  2. The preacher distinguishes “every weight” from “the sin that so easily entangles.” What things in your life, though not sinful in themselves, are slowing your race?
  3. Endurance is a long-distance virtue. Where are you tempted to think of the Christian life as a sprint rather than a marathon?
  4. Jesus endured the cross “for the sake of the joy set before him.” How does fixing your eyes on what lies ahead change the way you face present hardship or shame?
  5. Jesus “despised the shame”—he refused to let the world’s contempt govern his choices. Whose approval most shapes your decisions, and what would it look like to live for the verdict of God?
