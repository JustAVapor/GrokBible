Hebrews 4

The Rest That Remains and the Throne of Grace

Hebrews 4 opens in the middle of a sermon on a single psalm. Beginning at 3:7 the preacher has been expounding Psalm 95:7–11, a psalm that called Israel to worship and then warned them not to repeat the sin of the wilderness generation, who heard God’s voice, hardened their hearts, and were barred by divine oath from entering his “rest.” Chapter 3 drew the first lesson: the generation that left Egypt under Moses fell in the desert “because of unbelief” (3:19). Chapter 4 now draws the second and more hopeful lesson: the promise of entering God’s rest was never cancelled. It still stands open, it is offered to “us” in the gospel, and so the same psalm that once pronounced judgment now becomes an urgent invitation.

The chapter falls into two unequal parts. Verses 1–13 complete the exposition of Psalm 95 that began at 3:7. Here the preacher works like a careful Jewish interpreter, laying Scripture beside Scripture. He sets the psalm’s phrase “my rest” beside Genesis 2:2, where God “rested on the seventh day from all his works,” and concludes that the rest God swore to withhold from the rebels is nothing less than God’s own rest, which has existed since creation was finished. He then notes that the psalm says “Today” through David, centuries after Joshua led Israel into Canaan. If Joshua’s conquest had been the final rest, God would not have spoken later of “another day.” Therefore “a Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God” (4:9). The section ends with a call to strive to enter that rest (4:11), grounded in a majestic description of the word of God, living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, before which every creature lies naked and exposed (4:12–13).

Verses 14–16 then turn a corner. They gather up the brief mention of Jesus as “high priest” from 2:17–18 and 3:1, and they open the long central section of the sermon (4:14–10:18) on the priesthood of Christ. In three verses the preacher sets out its heart: we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens; he is able to sympathize with our weaknesses because he was tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin; therefore let us draw near with boldness to the throne of grace. The chapter that began with “Let us fear” ends with “Let us draw near.” Fear of falling short and confidence in approaching God are not opposites in Hebrews; both arise from taking God’s word seriously, and the second is the remedy for the first.

Several key words carry the argument and are worth watching. The noun κατάπαυσις katapausis, “rest,” appears eight times between 3:11 and 4:11, and the related verb καταπαύω katapauō, “to rest” or “give rest,” three times in chapter 4. The verb εἰσέρχομαι eiserchomai, “enter,” appears eleven times in 3:11–4:11; the whole passage is about entering or failing to enter. The word “Today” (σήμερον sēmeron) returns twice in 4:7, reminding the hearers that the moment of decision is their own. The sermon also introduces a new word, σαββατισμός sabbatismos, “Sabbath-rest” (4:9), which appears here for the first time in surviving Greek literature. And the word λόγος logos frames the end of the exposition: the “word” of hearing that did not profit Israel (4:2), the “word” of God that pierces the heart (4:12), and the “account” (logos) we must give to God (4:13).

Readers should notice the preacher’s constant use of the first person plural: “let us fear” (4:1), “we who have believed” (4:3), “let us strive” (4:11), “let us hold fast” (4:14), “let us draw near” (4:16). He does not stand above his hearers. He places himself with them in the wilderness between redemption and arrival, sharing both their danger and their hope. Notice too the balance between the present and the future. Believers “are entering” the rest now (4:3), yet the rest still “remains” (4:9) and must be striven for (4:11). This tension between “already” and “not yet” runs throughout Hebrews and the whole New Testament.

Finally, watch how the chapter moves from the word to the priest. The word of God searches us and exposes us, and we must give account to him (4:12–13). Left there, the hearer might despair. But the very next sentence announces that the God before whom we stand exposed has provided a high priest who knows our weakness from the inside and who now sits at the throne of grace (4:14–16). The sword of the word and the sympathy of the priest belong together. The one shows us our need; the other meets it.
